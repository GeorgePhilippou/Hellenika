<!-- field: summary -->
Odysseus' ten-year journey home from Troy, and the reclaiming of a household that has almost given him up.

<!-- field: significance -->
With the Iliad, the founding work of Greek literature: the model for every later story of a journey home, and the fullest picture of household, hospitality and status in early Greek society.

<!-- field: body -->
An epic of about 12,100 lines of hexameter verse in 24 books, the Odyssey was composed around 725–675 BC, probably in the eastern Aegean, and probably somewhat later than the Iliad. Ancient tradition credits both poems to Homer; whether one poet made both is still debated. It grew out of centuries of oral poetry, and its repeated phrases — "rosy-fingered Dawn", "the wine-dark sea" — are the working toolkit of singers who composed as they performed.

The story begins near its end. Ten years after the fall of Troy, Odysseus is held on the island of Ogygia while suitors occupy his house on Ithaca and court his wife, Penelope. The first four books follow his son Telemachus; Odysseus then reaches the Phaeacians and tells his own wanderings — the Cyclops, Circe, the land of the dead, the Sirens — as a flashback. The second half is set on Ithaca, where he returns in disguise, tests his household and kills the suitors.

Where the Iliad is about glory in war, the Odyssey is about survival, cunning and home. Its hero is introduced as *polytropos*, "of many turns", and is admired for his lies. Its world of households, sea voyages and guest-friendship (*xenia*) between hosts and strangers belongs more to the poet's own age of trade and colonisation than to the Bronze Age in which the story is set.

The poem reached its fixed form through Athenian recitation and Alexandrian editing, and has shaped storytelling ever since, from Virgil's *Aeneid* to James Joyce's *Ulysses*.
