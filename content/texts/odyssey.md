<!-- field: summary -->
Odysseus' ten-year journey home from Troy, and the reclaiming of a household that has almost given him up.

<!-- field: body -->
The Odyssey is a different kind of poem from the Iliad: domestic rather than martial, structurally intricate, and interested in survival by cunning rather than glory by death.

Its hero lies constantly, and the poem admires him for it. He tells the Cyclops his name is Nobody; he tests his own household in disguise; he is introduced as *polytropos*, the man of many turns. The moral universe is less absolute than the Iliad's.

It is also the first European narrative to use non-linear structure. It begins near the end, spends four books on Telemachus before Odysseus appears, and has the hero narrate his own adventures at a feast — a flashback within a first-person account.

Penelope is the other half of the story. She holds off a hundred suitors for years by unweaving a shroud each night, and when Odysseus returns she tests him with a lie about their bed. She is as careful and as strategic as he is.

The poem's opening word, *polytropos*, describing Odysseus himself, has become a small battleground of its own in translation history precisely because it resists any single English equivalent: older translations rendered it as "the man of many wiles" or "of many turns," emphasising cunning, while Emily Wilson's widely read 2018 translation — the first published English version of the poem by a woman — chose the plainer and more ambiguous "complicated," a translation choice that shifts the poem's very first line toward psychological description rather than heroic epithet. The question of who actually composed the poem has its own long, mostly abandoned side-branch: the Victorian novelist Samuel Butler argued at length that internal evidence pointed to a young Sicilian noblewoman as the Odyssey's true author, a theory virtually no specialist today accepts but one still cited as a curiosity of the poem's reception, not least because the poem's sustained, sympathetic attention to Penelope's household and to the practicalities of weaving, textiles and domestic management struck Butler, and has struck some readers since, as more attentive to a woman's world than the Iliad's battlefield ever is.
