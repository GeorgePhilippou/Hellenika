<!-- field: summary -->
Xenophon's first-hand account of the Greek mercenaries who marched into Persia with Cyrus the Younger and fought their way home.

<!-- field: significance -->
The great eyewitness adventure story of antiquity, and a lesson to later Greeks — Alexander among them — that Persia could be crossed by a Greek army.

<!-- field: body -->
The *Anabasis*, "The March Up-Country", was written by the Athenian Xenophon, probably in the 370s or 360s BC, decades after the events it describes. In seven books it tells the story of the expedition of 401–399 BC in which he took part.

Cyrus the Younger, brother of the Persian king Artaxerxes II, hired some 10,000 Greek mercenaries and led them from Sardis into Mesopotamia, at first concealing his aim to seize the throne. At Cunaxa near Babylon in 401 BC the Greeks won their part of the battle, but Cyrus was killed. When the Persians treacherously murdered their generals, Xenophon was elected one of the new leaders. The rest of the book follows their retreat north through the mountains of Kurdistan and Armenia in winter, harassed by enemies and weather, until they sighted the Black Sea and cried "The sea! The sea!"

Xenophon writes clearly and vividly, describing marches, battles, councils, foraging and the problems of leading an army of independent-minded Greeks. He writes about himself in the third person, presenting his own decisions in a favourable light.

The *Anabasis* was used in antiquity as a guide to Persia and to leadership; Arrian named his history of Alexander after it. For centuries it was a standard first Greek reading book for students.
