<!-- field: summary -->
Aeschylus' tragedy of 472 BC about the Persian defeat at Salamis, told from the Persian court — the oldest surviving Greek play.

<!-- field: significance -->
The oldest surviving play in the Western tradition, and a rare tragedy about real recent events, written by a veteran of the war it portrays.

<!-- field: body -->
*The Persians* was staged by Aeschylus at the City Dionysia in Athens in 472 BC, eight years after the battle of Salamis, and won first prize. The wealthy young Pericles paid for its chorus. It is the oldest Greek tragedy to survive, and the only surviving one that takes its subject not from myth but from recent history.

The play is set at the Persian royal court at Susa. The chorus of elders and the queen mother Atossa wait anxiously for news of Xerxes' invasion of Greece. A messenger arrives and describes the disaster at Salamis in a long, vivid speech, the earliest account of the battle. The ghost of Xerxes' father, Darius, is summoned from the tomb and blames his son's arrogance in bridging the Hellespont and offending the gods. Finally Xerxes himself enters in rags, and the play ends in a long lament.

Aeschylus had himself fought against the Persians, probably at Salamis as well as Marathon. Yet the play does not simply gloat. It shows the enemy's grief with sympathy and presents the defeat as a warning about *hubris*, the overconfidence that brings ruin — a lesson Athenians, already building an empire, might apply to themselves.

It is a key source for how Greeks saw the Persian Wars, and remains one of the most performed Greek plays.
