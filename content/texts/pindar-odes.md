<!-- field: summary -->
The choral songs Pindar composed to celebrate victors at the four great Panhellenic games, the summit of Greek lyric poetry.

<!-- field: significance -->
The greatest surviving Greek choral poetry, and a window on the values of the athletic aristocracy of the early Classical age.

<!-- field: body -->
Pindar of Thebes (c. 518–438 BC) was the most celebrated lyric poet of ancient Greece. Of his many works, four books of *epinikia*, or victory odes, survive almost complete — 45 poems arranged by the games they celebrate: Olympian, Pythian, Isthmian and Nemean. The datable odes run from 498 to 446 BC.

A victory ode was commissioned by the winner, his family or his city, and performed by a chorus with music and dance, either at the games or at a celebration back home. Pindar's patrons included the tyrants Hieron of Syracuse and Theron of Akragas, kings of Cyrene and Macedon, and aristocratic families from across the Greek world.

The odes rarely describe the race itself. Instead they set the victory in the light of the gods and of myth, praising the victor's family and city, and linking his success to the heroes of the past. They combine a myth — such as Pelops' chariot race in *Olympian* 1 or the founding of Cyrene in *Pythian* 4 — with prayers, moral sayings and reflections on human fortune: "Creatures of a day! What is a man? What is he not? A dream of a shadow is man."

Pindar's language is dense, allusive and soaring, and was admired and imitated in antiquity; Horace said that trying to rival him was like flying on wings of wax. He gave his name to the ode in European poetry.
