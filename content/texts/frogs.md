<!-- field: summary -->
Aristophanes' comedy of 405 BC in which Dionysus goes down to Hades to bring back a great tragic poet, and judges a contest between Aeschylus and Euripides.

<!-- field: significance -->
A brilliant comedy and the first great work of literary criticism, staged as Athens faced defeat.

<!-- field: body -->
*The Frogs* won first prize at the Lenaia festival in 405 BC, the year after the deaths of Euripides and Sophocles, and only months before Athens' final defeat in the Peloponnesian War. It was so admired that it was given a rare second performance.

Dionysus, god of the theatre, complains that there are no good tragic poets left, and sets off for the underworld to bring Euripides back, disguised as Heracles and accompanied by his slave Xanthias. He is rowed across the lake of the dead, accompanied by a chorus of croaking frogs, which gives the play its title, and meets a chorus of initiates of the Mysteries.

In Hades, Aeschylus and Euripides are quarrelling over who is the best tragedian. Dionysus judges a contest between them. They criticise each other's style, prologues and songs, and their verses are weighed on a pair of scales. Aeschylus is grand, old-fashioned and patriotic; Euripides clever, realistic and argumentative. In the end Dionysus asks them for advice to save the city, and chooses to bring Aeschylus back.

The play mixes slapstick with sharp comments on Athenian politics, including the famous comparison of good citizens with good coins driven out by bad. Its contest is the earliest extended piece of literary criticism in Greek.
