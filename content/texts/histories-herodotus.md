<!-- field: summary -->
An enquiry into why Greeks and Persians went to war, which becomes an account of the whole known world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first work of history in the Western tradition, and the main source for the Persian Wars and for much of the 6th-century Mediterranean world.

<!-- field: body -->
A prose history in the Ionic dialect, written by Herodotus of Halicarnassus between about 440 and 425 BC and later divided into nine books. Its stated aim is to preserve the great deeds of Greeks and non-Greeks from being forgotten, and to explain why they went to war with one another.

The first half ranges across the known world as the Persian Empire expands: the fall of Croesus of Lydia, the customs of Egypt, Babylon and the Scythians, and the rise of Cyrus, Cambyses and Darius. The second half narrates the Ionian Revolt and the Persian invasions of Greece — Marathon in 490 BC, then Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea in 480–479 BC.

Herodotus is open about his method. He names his informants where he can, gives competing versions side by side, and says repeatedly that he must report what he was told but need not believe it. Ancient critics called him "the father of lies", yet archaeology has confirmed many of his specific details, from Scythian burial customs to the canal Xerxes cut through the Athos peninsula. His weakness is numbers: the Persian armies he describes are far too large to be credible.

Cicero called him "the father of history", and the word *historia*, his "enquiry", gave the discipline its name.
