<!-- field: summary -->
Euripides' last tragedy, in which the god Dionysus takes terrible revenge on the king of Thebes who refuses to worship him.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only surviving tragedy about Dionysus, the god of the theatre itself, and one of the most powerful plays about religion and the irrational.

<!-- field: body -->
Euripides wrote the *Bacchae* late in life at the court of King Archelaus of Macedon, where he died in 406 BC. His son staged it in Athens the following year, and it won first prize.

The god Dionysus arrives in Thebes disguised as a young man, bringing his ecstatic cult from Asia. His mother Semele was a Theban princess, but the city has denied that he is a god. He has driven the women of Thebes, among them King Pentheus' mother Agave, into the mountains, where as Bacchants they dance, handle snakes and tear animals apart with their bare hands. The young king Pentheus, rigid and hostile, tries to suppress the cult and imprisons the stranger, who escapes as the palace shakes.

Dionysus then plays on Pentheus' curiosity and lures him, dressed as a woman, to spy on the women. There they discover him and, in their frenzy, tear him limb from limb, with Agave leading them in the belief that he is a lion. She returns to the city carrying her son's head in triumph, and slowly realises what she has done.

The play shows both the joy and the terrible violence of the god of wine and ecstasy, the god in whose honour the play itself was performed. Its meaning — whether it celebrates or condemns Dionysus — is still debated.
