<!-- field: summary -->
The collection of some sixty Greek medical writings attributed to Hippocrates of Kos, the foundation of Western medicine.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first body of rational medicine, explaining disease by natural causes and setting the ethical ideals of doctors still invoked today.

<!-- field: body -->
The Hippocratic Corpus is a collection of about sixty medical treatises in Ionic Greek, attributed in antiquity to Hippocrates of Kos (c. 460–370 BC). In fact they were written by many authors, mostly in the late 5th and 4th centuries BC, and were probably gathered in the library at Alexandria. Which, if any, Hippocrates himself wrote is unknown.

The treatises cover the whole of medicine: anatomy, diet, surgery, fractures and dislocations, gynaecology, prognosis, epidemics and the effects of climate. Their central idea is that diseases have natural causes, such as diet, environment and the balance of the body's fluids, rather than being sent by gods. *On the Sacred Disease* argues that epilepsy is no more divine than any other illness. *Airs, Waters, Places* links health to climate and geography. The *Epidemics* record individual case histories in careful detail, including many patients who died. Several works developed the theory of four bodily "humours" — blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile — which dominated medicine for two thousand years.

The most famous text is the *Oath*, in which a new doctor swears by Apollo, Asclepius and the healing gods to help the sick and do them no harm, not to give poison, and to keep patients' secrets.

Through Galen in the Roman period, and later Arabic and Latin translations, the Hippocratic writings shaped medicine until modern times.
