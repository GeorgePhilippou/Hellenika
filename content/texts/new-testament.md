<!-- field: summary -->
The 27 Christian books written in koine Greek between about AD 50 and 120: gospels, letters, the Acts of the Apostles and Revelation.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most widely read and translated Greek text ever written, which carried the everyday Greek of the Roman east into every language of the world.

<!-- field: body -->
The New Testament was written by Jews and Gentiles of the eastern Roman empire in the common Greek (*koinē*) of their day, not in classical Attic. The oldest parts are the letters of Paul, a Greek-speaking Jew from Tarsus, written in the 50s AD to the Christian communities he had founded in Greek cities — Thessalonica, Philippi, Corinth — and in Galatia and Rome. The four Gospels, telling the life and teaching of Jesus, followed in about AD 70–100, with the Acts of the Apostles, other letters and the Revelation of John. When they quote Jewish scripture, they mostly use the Greek Septuagint.

The books were gathered into an accepted list over the following centuries; the 27-book canon is first listed by Athanasius of Alexandria in AD 367.

No other ancient text survives in so many copies: over 5,000 Greek manuscripts, from papyrus fragments found in Egypt — the Rylands fragment of John is often dated to the 2nd century — to great parchment codices of the 4th century such as Codex Sinaiticus. Erasmus published the first printed Greek edition at Basel in 1516, and translations from the Greek by Luther, Tyndale and others shaped the languages of Europe.
