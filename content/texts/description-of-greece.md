<!-- field: summary -->
Pausanias' ten-book guide to the sanctuaries, monuments and works of art of mainland Greece, written in the 2nd century AD.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most important guidebook of the ancient world, and an indispensable source for Greek art, religion and topography.

<!-- field: body -->
The *Description of Greece* was written by Pausanias in the mid-2nd century AD, under the Roman emperors Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius. In ten books it describes mainland Greece region by region: Attica and Megara, Corinth and the Argolid, Laconia, Messenia, Elis and Olympia (two books), Achaea, Arcadia, Boeotia and Phocis with Delphi.

Pausanias travelled along the main roads, describing what he saw: temples, altars, tombs, statues and paintings, and the myths, local histories and religious customs connected with them. He was interested above all in the past of Greece before the Romans, and passed over most recent buildings. His accounts of the Acropolis of Athens, the sanctuaries of Olympia and Delphi, the Statue of Zeus by Pheidias and the ruins of Mycenae are among the fullest that survive.

Modern scholars once dismissed him as a dull compiler of second-hand information. Archaeology has proved him a careful and accurate observer: excavators at Olympia, Delphi and Athens have used his descriptions to identify buildings and statues, and Schliemann used him at Mycenae. Many works of art are known only from his descriptions.

The book was rediscovered in the Renaissance and printed in 1516, and has been the guide of travellers to Greece ever since.
