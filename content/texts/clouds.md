<!-- field: summary -->
Aristophanes' comedy of 423 BC mocking Socrates as a quack teacher of clever argument — a caricature remembered at his trial.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only contemporary portrait of Socrates by someone other than his followers, and a sharp satire of the new learning in Athens.

<!-- field: body -->
*The Clouds* was first staged at the City Dionysia of 423 BC, where it came third of three. Aristophanes later partly revised it, and the version we have is this revision, which was probably never performed.

The old farmer Strepsiades is drowning in debts run up by his horse-mad son Pheidippides. He enrols at the "Thinkery" (*phrontistērion*) of Socrates, hoping to learn the "Worse Argument" that can win any case, so that he can cheat his creditors. Socrates is shown suspended in a basket, studying the heavens, worshipping the Clouds instead of Zeus, and teaching absurd science. Strepsiades is too stupid to learn, so he sends his son, who learns all too well: he beats his father and proves, by clever argument, that he was right to do so. In fury, Strepsiades burns down the Thinkery.

Aristophanes' Socrates is a caricature, mixing him with the natural philosophers and the paid Sophists, though the real Socrates took no fees. But the play shows how many Athenians saw the new learning — as a threat to religion, morality and the authority of fathers.

In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates tells the jury at his trial in 399 BC that the prejudice against him goes back to plays like this one, in which a "Socrates" swings around claiming to walk on air.
