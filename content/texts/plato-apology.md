<!-- field: summary -->
Plato's account of Socrates' defence speech at his trial in 399 BC, and of his response to the death sentence.

<!-- field: significance -->
The founding portrait of the philosopher who dies rather than give up questioning — and the source of "the unexamined life is not worth living".

<!-- field: body -->
The *Apology* (from the Greek *apologia*, "defence speech") was written by Plato some years after the trial of Socrates in 399 BC, which Plato attended. It presents three speeches by Socrates to the jury of 500 Athenian citizens. How closely it reproduces his actual words is unknown, but it is the fullest account of the trial; Xenophon wrote a shorter *Apology* of his own.

In the first speech Socrates answers the charges of impiety and corrupting the young. He says the prejudice against him comes partly from comedies such as Aristophanes' *Clouds*, and explains his way of life: when the oracle at Delphi said no one was wiser than him, he set out to test it by questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen, and found that his only wisdom was to know that he knew nothing. He cross-examines his accuser Meletus, and insists he will never stop practising philosophy, for "the unexamined life is not worth living".

Found guilty, he proposes, half seriously, that he should be given free meals at public expense as a benefactor of the city, then offers a fine of thirty minas. Condemned to death, he tells the jury that death is either a dreamless sleep or a journey to meet the great men of the past, and that no harm can come to a good man.

The *Apology* made Socrates the model of intellectual integrity.
