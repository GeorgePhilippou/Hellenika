<!-- field: summary -->
Plato's dialogue set at an Athenian drinking party, where the guests take turns praising love — the most famous work of philosophy on the subject.

<!-- field: significance -->
Plato's great work on love and beauty, the source of the idea of "Platonic love" and a vivid picture of Athenian intellectual life.

<!-- field: body -->
Plato wrote the *Symposium* around 385–370 BC. It is set in 416 BC at a drinking party (*symposion*) held by the tragic poet Agathon to celebrate his first victory at the dramatic festival, and is told at second hand years later.

Instead of heavy drinking, the guests agree that each will give a speech in praise of Eros, the god of love. The speakers include Phaedrus, the doctor Eryximachus and the comic poet Aristophanes. Aristophanes tells a famous myth: humans were once double beings, with four arms and four legs, until Zeus split them in two, and ever since each half has searched for its other half; love is the longing to be whole again.

Socrates speaks last. He says he learned about love from a wise woman, Diotima of Mantinea. Love, she taught, is the desire for beauty and goodness, and can lead the soul upwards like a ladder: from love of one beautiful body, to all beautiful bodies, to beautiful souls, laws and knowledge, and finally to the vision of Beauty itself, eternal and unchanging.

Then the drunken Alcibiades bursts in and gives a speech praising Socrates himself — his self-control, his courage in battle and his strange power to move those who listen to him.

The dialogue gave the world the idea of "Platonic love" and remains one of Plato's most widely read works.
