<!-- field: summary -->
Short moral tales of talking animals credited to Aesop, a slave storyteller of the 6th century BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous fables in the world — the tortoise and the hare, the fox and the grapes — and among the most widely read works of Greek origin.

<!-- field: body -->
Aesop was a storyteller of the 6th century BC whom Herodotus describes as a slave on the island of Samos. Later legend made him an ugly but brilliant man from Thrace or Phrygia who won his freedom by his wit, advised kings such as Croesus, and was unjustly killed at Delphi. Almost nothing about him is certain, and he probably wrote nothing down.

Aesop's fables are short tales, usually about animals who talk and act like people, each ending in a lesson. The tortoise beats the boastful hare in a race; the fox, unable to reach the grapes, decides they are sour; the boy who cries "wolf" is not believed when the wolf really comes; the ant stores food for winter while the grasshopper sings. Such stories were part of everyday Greek culture: Aristophanes' characters quote them, and Plato says Socrates spent his last days in prison putting Aesop's fables into verse.

The fables were passed down orally and collected later. Demetrius of Phalerum made a collection around 300 BC, now lost. The surviving versions come from the Latin verse of Phaedrus in the 1st century AD, the Greek verse of Babrius, and later prose collections.

Aesop's fables became a standard school text in antiquity and the Middle Ages, and were retold by La Fontaine and countless others. Many of their lessons survive as proverbs.
