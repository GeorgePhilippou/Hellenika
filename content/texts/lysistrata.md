<!-- field: summary -->
Aristophanes' comedy of 411 BC in which the women of Athens and Sparta end the war by refusing sex to their husbands.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous ancient comedy, and a wartime plea for peace among the Greeks that is still staged as an anti-war play.

<!-- field: body -->
*Lysistrata* was staged in 411 BC, probably at the Lenaia festival, two years after the destruction of Athens' army in Sicily, when the Peloponnesian War was going badly and Athenian democracy was about to be overthrown by an oligarchic coup.

The Athenian woman Lysistrata, whose name means "disbander of armies", gathers women from across Greece, including the Spartan Lampito, and persuades them to swear an oath: they will refuse to sleep with their husbands until the men make peace. Meanwhile the older women seize the Acropolis, where the city's war treasury is kept. A chorus of old men tries to smoke them out and is drenched with water by a chorus of old women. The men, in increasing physical discomfort, grow desperate, and envoys from Sparta arrive in the same state. Lysistrata brings the two sides together, reminds them of their shared history against Persia, and a peace is celebrated with a feast and dancing.

It is a bawdy fantasy — in reality Athenian women had no political voice, and all the parts were played by men. But beneath the jokes lies a serious appeal to end a ruinous war between Greeks.

The play has been revived and adapted around the world, often in protest against war.
