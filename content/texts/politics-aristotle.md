<!-- field: summary -->
Aristotle's treatise on the city-state and its constitutions, the foundation of political science.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first systematic study of politics, which shaped political thought from the Middle Ages to the founders of modern republics.

<!-- field: body -->
The *Politics*, in eight books, was written by Aristotle at the Lyceum in Athens, probably in the 330s and 320s BC, and is closely linked to his *Nicomachean Ethics*: ethics asks what the good life is, politics how a community can make it possible. It draws on the collection of 158 Greek constitutions made by Aristotle and his students.

Aristotle begins with the household and the village, and argues that the polis is the natural end of human association, existing "for the sake of the good life". The human being, he says, is by nature a political animal (*zōon politikon*): anyone who can live without a city is either a beast or a god. In the same first book he defends slavery, arguing that some people are slaves by nature, and the rule of men over women.

He then classifies constitutions by whether one, a few or many rule, and whether they rule for the common good or their own interest. The good forms are kingship, aristocracy and "polity"; their corrupt forms are tyranny, oligarchy and democracy. He argues that the most stable practical constitution is a mixed one resting on a strong middle class. He discusses the causes of revolution, and sketches an ideal city with careful education of its citizens.

Translated into Latin in the 13th century, the *Politics* became a founding text of Western political science.
