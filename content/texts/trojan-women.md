<!-- field: summary -->
Euripides' tragedy of 415 BC about the women of Troy awaiting slavery after the city's fall — one of the most powerful anti-war plays ever written.

<!-- field: significance -->
A stark portrait of the suffering war inflicts on the defeated, staged by Athens in the year it massacred the people of Melos.

<!-- field: body -->
*The Trojan Women* was staged at the City Dionysia in Athens in 415 BC, as part of a set of plays on the Trojan War. A few months earlier, Athens had captured the neutral island of Melos, killed its men and enslaved its women and children, and it was about to launch the Sicilian Expedition.

The play has almost no plot. Troy has fallen, and its women wait by the shore to be shared out as slaves among the Greek victors. The aged queen Hecuba learns the fate of each of her family in turn. Her daughter Cassandra, given to Agamemnon, foresees his murder and her own. Hector's widow Andromache is to go to the son of the man who killed her husband, and her little son Astyanax is taken from her and thrown from the walls of Troy, for fear he will grow up to avenge his father; Hecuba buries him in his father's shield. Helen pleads for her life before her husband Menelaus. The play ends as Troy burns and the women are led to the ships.

The gods Poseidon and Athena, in the opening scene, plan to punish the Greeks on their voyage home, suggesting that the victors too will suffer. Whether Euripides meant the play as a protest against Melos is debated, but it has been revived many times as a protest against war.
