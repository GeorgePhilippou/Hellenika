<!-- field: summary -->
The private philosophical notebook of the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, written in Greek around AD 170–180.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most personal work of ancient Stoicism, written by the most powerful man in the world, and one of the most widely read books of philosophy.

<!-- field: body -->
Marcus Aurelius (AD 121–180) ruled the Roman empire from 161 until his death. Educated by Greek teachers, including the sophist Herodes Atticus, he was drawn to Stoic philosophy as a young man. His reign was troubled by plague and war, and he spent much of his last decade campaigning against Germanic peoples on the Danube.

During these years he wrote notes to himself, in Greek, the language of philosophy. They were not meant for publication: the title in the manuscripts is *Ta eis heauton*, "Things to himself". The twelve books are a series of reflections, reminders and quotations. Book 1 thanks the people who shaped him; the rest return again and again to Stoic themes — that only our own judgements are in our power, that we should accept what nature brings, that life is short and fame worthless, and that human beings are made for one another. He quotes Epictetus, whose *Discourses* he had read.

The work survived through Byzantium; the first printed edition, by Wilhelm Xylander in 1559, was based on a manuscript now lost. It has been widely read since, by soldiers, statesmen and general readers.
