<!-- field: summary -->
A woman buries her brother against the king's edict — the founding text of the conflict between conscience and law.

<!-- field: body -->
Creon, newly installed as king of Thebes, forbids the burial of Polynices, who had attacked his own city leading a foreign army against it. Antigone, his sister, defies the edict and buries him anyway, invoking the unwritten and unfailing laws of the gods as a higher authority than any human decree. Creon, unwilling to be seen bending to a woman's defiance in front of the city, condemns her to be walled up alive in a rock tomb rather than executed outright.

The German philosopher Hegel's reading of the play remains the most influential interpretation and still holds up remarkably well: this is not a simple conflict of right against wrong, but of right against right. Creon has a genuine, defensible political case — a city under threat cannot afford to honour a traitor who attacked it, and order requires consistent enforcement of the law — and that legitimate position is ultimately destroyed by his own rigid refusal to bend or compromise once challenged. Antigone's religious and familial claim is equally absolute in its own terms, and equally destructive in practice: she dismisses her own sister Ismene's belated offer to share the blame and the punishment with real contempt, unwilling to let anyone else share credit for an act she sees as entirely her own moral duty.

Creon relents only when it is already too late to prevent disaster. Antigone has hanged herself in her sealed tomb; his own son Haemon, who was betrothed to her, kills himself beside her body in grief and rage at his father; and Creon's wife Eurydice, on hearing of their son's death, takes her own life as well. Creon alone is left alive at the end, stripped of everything he tried to protect by insisting on his own authority — arguably a harsher fate than death would have been.

The play has been repeatedly restaged as resistance literature under later occupations and dictatorships. Jean Anouilh's adaptation, staged in Nazi-occupied Paris in 1944, was ambiguous enough in its sympathies that both the French Resistance and the Vichy government's own censors read it as supporting their own, entirely opposed positions, allowing it to be performed openly under German occupation while still carrying real subversive meaning for audiences who wanted to find it there.
