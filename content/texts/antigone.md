<!-- field: summary -->
A woman buries her brother against the king's edict — the founding text of the conflict between conscience and law.

<!-- field: significance -->
The classic statement of conscience against the law of the state, and one of the most adapted plays ever written.

<!-- field: body -->
A tragedy by Sophocles, first staged in Athens around 441 BC. It continues the story of Oedipus' family after his sons Eteocles and Polynices have killed each other fighting for the throne of Thebes.

Creon, the new king, forbids burial for Polynices, who led a foreign army against his own city. His sister Antigone buries him anyway, appealing to the unwritten laws of the gods above any human decree. Creon has her walled up alive in a rock tomb. When he finally relents, it is too late: Antigone has hanged herself, Creon's son Haemon, who was to marry her, kills himself beside her, and Creon's wife then takes her own life. Creon is left alive with nothing.

The philosopher Hegel read the play as a clash not of right against wrong but of right against right. Creon defends the city's need for law and order; Antigone defends family and religious duty. Each holds to an absolute position, and each destroys what they care about.

The play has been reworked again and again as a drama of resistance to tyranny. Jean Anouilh's version, staged in occupied Paris in 1944, was ambiguous enough to be claimed by both the Resistance and the collaborationist authorities.
