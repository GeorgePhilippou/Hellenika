<!-- field: summary -->
A woman buries her brother against the king's edict — the founding text of the conflict between conscience and law.

<!-- field: body -->
Creon forbids burial of Polynices, who attacked the city. Antigone, his sister, buries him anyway, invoking the unwritten and unfailing laws of the gods. Creon condemns her to be walled up alive.

Hegel's reading is the influential one and remains persuasive: this is not right against wrong but right against right. Creon has a genuine case — the city must survive, and traitors cannot be honoured — and it is destroyed by his refusal to bend. Antigone is equally absolute and equally destructive, dismissing her sister's offer of help with contempt.

Creon relents too late. Antigone has hanged herself; his son, betrothed to her, kills himself; his wife follows. He is left alive, which is the harsher outcome.

The play has been used as a resistance text under occupations and dictatorships repeatedly — Anouilh staged it in occupied Paris in 1944, and both the Resistance and the censors approved.
