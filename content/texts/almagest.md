<!-- field: summary -->
Ptolemy's great treatise of astronomy, written in Alexandria around AD 150, which explained the heavens for fourteen centuries.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most influential scientific book of antiquity, preserved and studied through Arabic and Latin until the Copernican revolution.

<!-- field: body -->
Ptolemy called his work the *Mathematical Syntaxis* ("compilation"). Later Greeks called it the *Megistē*, "the greatest", and when it was translated into Arabic in the 9th century this became *al-Majisṭī*, from which comes the Latin *Almagest*.

In thirteen books, Ptolemy set out to explain all the observed movements of the heavens with geometry. He argued that the Earth is a sphere at rest at the centre of the universe, then built models for the Sun, the Moon and each planet, combining circles moving on circles (epicycles) with off-centre orbits and a device called the equant, to reproduce their changing speeds and backward loops. He showed how to predict eclipses, and gave tables from which the positions of the planets could be calculated for any date. Books 7 and 8 contain a catalogue of 1,022 stars, with their positions and brightness, grouped in 48 constellations.

Ptolemy drew heavily on Hipparchus, much of whose work is known only through the *Almagest*. It was studied in Byzantium, translated into Arabic and refined by Islamic astronomers, and translated into Latin in the 12th century. It was overthrown only by Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo.
