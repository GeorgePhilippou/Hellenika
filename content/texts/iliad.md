<!-- field: summary -->
Fifty-one days in the tenth year of the Trojan War, framed entirely by the rage of Achilles and its consequences.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first and most influential work of European literature: the poem through which Greeks of every later age understood heroism, war and the gods.

<!-- field: body -->
An epic of about 15,700 lines of hexameter verse in 24 books, the Iliad was composed around 750–700 BC, probably in Ionia on the coast of Asia Minor. Tradition credits it to Homer. Its repeated phrases — "swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn" — are the tools of oral poets who composed as they performed, as Milman Parry showed in the 1930s by recording singers in the Balkans who still worked the same way.

It is not the story of the whole Trojan War. It covers about fifty days in the tenth year of the siege, and its subject is named in its first word: *mēnis*, wrath. Achilles quarrels with Agamemnon over a captive woman and withdraws from the fighting. His friend Patroclus is killed by the Trojan prince Hector, and Achilles returns to kill Hector and drag his body behind his chariot. The poem ends not with the fall of Troy but with Priam, Hector's father, coming alone to Achilles' tent to ransom his son's body, and the two enemies weeping together.

The story is set in the Bronze Age, and some of the objects it describes, such as the boar's-tusk helmet, are genuinely Mycenaean. But much of its world — its weapons, burials and politics — belongs to the poet's own centuries, making it a blend of remembered past and Archaic present.

Probably written down by the 6th century BC and later edited by scholars at Alexandria, it became the core of Greek education for a thousand years.
