<!-- field: summary -->
Fifty-one days in the tenth year of the Trojan War, framed entirely by the rage of Achilles and its consequences.

<!-- field: body -->
The Iliad is not the story of the Trojan War. It opens in the tenth year with a quarrel over a captive woman, and ends before the city falls, with the burial of Hector. The wooden horse is not in it.

What it is about is stated in the first word: *mēnis*, wrath. Achilles withdraws, his friend Patroclus dies wearing his armour, and he returns to kill Hector in a rage that carries him past any recognisable limit — dragging the body behind his chariot for days.

The last book is the answer to the first. Priam comes alone at night to the tent of the man who killed his son, and kisses his hands. They weep together, and Achilles gives the body back. A poem about rage ends in shared grief between enemies, which is why it has never stopped being read.

The formulaic language — "swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered dawn" — is the working technique of oral composition in performance rather than decorative style. Milman Parry demonstrated this in the 1930s not just through analysis of the Greek text but through direct fieldwork, travelling to the Balkans with Albert Lord to record living South Slavic epic singers who could improvise poems of comparable length in performance using exactly the same kind of reusable formulaic phrases scaled to fit the metre — evidence that this was a genuine technique for composing long poetry aloud without writing, not merely a stylistic quirk of a literate author imitating oral style.

How the poem then reached the fixed text later antiquity read is its own long story. Once writing made a stable version possible, the text still had centuries to drift before Hellenistic scholars at Alexandria, above all Aristarchus of Samothrace in the second century BC, produced critical editions comparing multiple manuscript traditions and marking lines they judged spurious rather than silently deleting them — an editorial caution that means variant readings from before Aristarchus' time still turn up in surviving papyri, giving modern scholars a rare window onto just how much the text could still vary before ancient scholarship settled it into something close to the version read today.
