<!-- field: summary -->
The secret incendiary weapon of the Byzantine navy, invented around 672, which burned even on water.

<!-- field: significance -->
It helped save Constantinople from the Arab sieges of the 7th and 8th centuries, and its secret was so well kept that it is still unknown.

<!-- field: body -->
According to the chronicler Theophanes, around 672 a refugee architect named Kallinikos, from Heliopolis in Syria, brought the Byzantines a new kind of fire. It was a liquid that was heated and pumped under pressure through bronze tubes (*siphōnes*) mounted in the bows of warships, projecting a stream of flame that stuck to whatever it hit and could not easily be put out with water. Smaller hand-held versions and grenades were also used.

Greek fire was decisive in the great Arab sieges of Constantinople in 674–678 and 717–718, when it destroyed many enemy ships, and it was used later against the Rus' fleets that attacked the city in 941 and 1043. The Byzantines called it "liquid fire" or "sea fire"; "Greek fire" is the name later used by western Europeans.

The recipe was a state secret, known only to a few families, and the emperor Constantine VII warned his son never to reveal it to foreigners. It has been lost. Most historians think it was based on crude oil from the Black Sea region, perhaps mixed with resins, and that the secret lay as much in the pumping apparatus as in the liquid. It seems to have fallen out of use by the time of the Fourth Crusade in 1204.
