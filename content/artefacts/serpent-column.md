<!-- field: summary -->
The bronze victory monument of three entwined snakes dedicated at Delphi after Plataea in 479 BC, now in Istanbul.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Greeks' own monument to their victory over Persia, listing the states that fought — one of the most important inscriptions of the Classical age.

<!-- field: body -->
After the Greek victory over the Persians at Plataea in 479 BC, the allies dedicated a tenth of the spoils to Apollo at Delphi. The monument was a golden tripod bowl raised on a bronze column of three entwined serpents, about 8 m tall, whose heads supported the bowl.

The Spartan commander Pausanias first had an epigram inscribed claiming the victory for himself. The Spartans erased it and instead inscribed on the coils of the serpents the names of the 31 Greek states that had fought, headed by Sparta, Athens and Corinth. Herodotus and Thucydides mention the monument, and the list largely matches their accounts.

The gold bowl was melted down by the Phocians in the Sacred War of the 4th century BC. In the 4th century AD the emperor Constantine moved the bronze column to his new capital, Constantinople, and set it up in the Hippodrome, where it still stands in what is now Sultanahmet Square in Istanbul. The serpents' heads broke off around 1700; part of one head is in the Istanbul Archaeological Museums.

About 5.5 m of the column survives, with its inscription still legible.
