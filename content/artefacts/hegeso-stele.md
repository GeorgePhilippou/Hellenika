<!-- field: summary -->
An Athenian grave relief of about 410–400 BC showing the dead woman Hegeso choosing a necklace from a jewellery box held by her maid.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most celebrated Classical grave relief, a quiet image of a woman's life and death in the style of the Parthenon sculptors.

<!-- field: body -->
The grave stele of Hegeso was carved in Athens around 410–400 BC and stood over her grave in the Kerameikos, the city's main cemetery outside the Dipylon Gate. It was found there in 1870 and is now in the National Archaeological Museum, Athens; a copy stands on the original spot.

The relief, framed like a small shrine with pillars and a pediment, shows Hegeso, identified by the inscription as "Hegeso, daughter of Proxenos", seated on an elegant chair with her feet on a footstool. She lifts an item of jewellery — once painted, probably a necklace — from a box held open by a young servant girl standing before her. Both look down at it in silence.

The scene is not the moment of death but an image of the dead woman as she was in life, absorbed in an everyday act within the household. Her rich clothes and the jewellery show her family's wealth and her role as a well-born Athenian wife, the servant reminds us of the enslaved women in such households, and the calm, gentle mood is typical of Athenian grave reliefs, which avoided showing grief openly.

The figures' soft drapery and serene faces closely resemble the Parthenon sculptures, and the relief may come from a workshop trained on them. Thousands of such stelae were made in Athens until a law of around 317 BC banned luxurious grave monuments.
