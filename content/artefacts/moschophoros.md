An Archaic marble statue of a bearded man carrying a calf on his shoulders, dedicated on the Acropolis around 570–560 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
A vivid early image of Greek sacrifice and piety, and one of the masterpieces of Archaic Athenian sculpture.

<!-- field: body -->
The Moschophoros ("calf-bearer") is a marble statue made in Athens around 570–560 BC and dedicated on the Acropolis. It was found in 1864 in the debris the Athenians buried after the Persians sacked the Acropolis in 480 BC, and is now in the Acropolis Museum.

A bearded man, wearing a thin cloak, carries a calf across his shoulders, holding its legs in front of his chest. The man's arms and the calf's legs form an X, binding the two together, and the man looks out with the faint "Archaic smile". His eyes were once inlaid with coloured stone. He is bringing the calf to Athena as a sacrifice, a scene repeated in Greek religion for centuries.

The inscription on its base records that it was dedicated by a man whose name was probably Rhombos or Kombos, perhaps the man portrayed. It was carved from Hymettian marble, one of the earliest uses of Attic marble for large sculpture.

The statue is one of the finest works of early Athenian sculpture, combining the stiff frontality of the Archaic style with a tender relationship between man and animal. Its image of a man carrying an animal later shaped early Christian images of the Good Shepherd.
