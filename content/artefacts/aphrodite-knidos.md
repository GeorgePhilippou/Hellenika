<!-- field: summary -->
Praxiteles' nude Aphrodite, the first monumental Greek cult statue to show a goddess fully undraped, and by ancient reputation the single most admired statue anywhere.

<!-- field: significance -->
It reset what a Greek cult image of a goddess was allowed to look like, and its lost original was famous and imitated for centuries after -- a rare case where a work's later reputation is better attested than the work itself.

<!-- field: body -->
Ancient tradition, reported by Pliny the Elder, holds that Praxiteles carved two versions of Aphrodite and let the buyer choose: one modestly draped, one fully nude. The people of Kos, offered first pick, took the draped statue as the safer, more traditional option; Knidos took the one Kos had turned down. Whatever the anecdote's exact accuracy, the outcome was real: Knidos ended up with the more radical work, and it made the small city a pilgrimage destination.

The statue showed Aphrodite apparently caught undressing for a ritual bath, one hand loosely shielding her body rather than concealing it, her clothing draped over a nearby vessel. A circular shrine at Knidos was arranged so she could be seen from all sides -- pointedly unlike the frontal viewing typical of temple cult statues, and itself a sign of how much the statue's physical presence, not just its religious function, mattered to viewers.

No original survives. What is known of it comes from Roman marble copies of varying quality (the best-known called the Venus Colonna), coins from Knidos that show its pose, and ancient authors who describe travelling to see it and record Praxiteles' own reported preference for it above all his other work. Its influence outlasted the statue itself by centuries, shaping essentially every later reclining or bathing Aphrodite in Greek and Roman art.
