<!-- field: summary -->
A 160 m continuous relief of a procession — and the most contested cultural property dispute in the world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The masterpiece of Classical relief sculpture — and the most famous dispute in the world over the return of cultural property.

<!-- field: body -->
A carved marble frieze that ran for 160 m around the top of the inner walls of the Parthenon in Athens, made around 442–438 BC under the direction of Pheidias as part of Pericles' building programme. About half of what survives is in the British Museum, London; most of the rest is in the Acropolis Museum, Athens.

It shows a single great procession, with about 380 human figures and more than 200 animals: horsemen in ranks, chariots, elders, musicians, girls carrying vessels and cattle led to sacrifice. Over the east entrance, seated gods watch as a folded cloth is handled. The scene is usually identified as the Panathenaic procession bringing a new robe to Athena — unusual, since Greek temple sculpture normally showed myth, not citizens. The relief is only a few centimetres deep, carved slightly deeper at the top to be legible from far below.

The building later became a church and a mosque, and was badly damaged in an explosion in 1687. Between 1801 and 1812 agents of Lord Elgin, British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, removed about half the surviving frieze; the British Museum bought it in 1816. Whether his Ottoman permit allowed this is disputed.

Greece has long sought the frieze's return. The Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009, displays the Athens blocks with space left for those in London.
