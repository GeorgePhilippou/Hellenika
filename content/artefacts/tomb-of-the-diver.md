<!-- field: summary -->
Five painted slabs from a tomb at Paestum — the only surviving Greek painted tomb with figural scenes from this era.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the very few surviving examples of Greek painting from the Classical period, an art otherwise known only from texts and vases.

<!-- field: body -->
A stone burial chest (cist tomb) with five painted limestone slabs, made around 480–470 BC at Poseidonia (Paestum), a Greek colony in southern Italy. It was found in 1968 by Mario Napoli in a small cemetery near the city, and is now in the National Archaeological Museum of Paestum.

The four side walls show a symposium, the Greek drinking party: men recline on couches, drinking, talking, playing the lyre and the game of *kottabos*, flicking wine dregs at a target, while a young man plays the double pipe and others arrive. On the underside of the lid, facing the dead man, a lone young man dives from a stone platform into water, between two trees.

Greek painters such as Polygnotus were famous in antiquity, but their works are lost, and Greek painting is known mainly from descriptions and from vase painting. This tomb is one of the only surviving examples of Greek painting on a wall from the period.

The diver is usually read as an image of passing from life into death, perhaps linked to the mystery religions and Pythagorean ideas popular in southern Italy. Nothing in the tomb confirms this, and the meaning of the single scene remains open.
