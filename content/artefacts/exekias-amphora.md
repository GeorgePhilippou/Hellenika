<!-- field: summary -->
A black-figure amphora of about 540–530 BC, signed by Exekias, showing Achilles and Ajax playing a board game at Troy.

<!-- field: significance -->
The masterpiece of Athens' greatest black-figure painter — a quiet moment of war, full of tension.

<!-- field: body -->
Exekias was the greatest Athenian painter of black-figure pottery, active around 545–525 BC, and also a potter; he signed this amphora as both maker and painter. It was found in an Etruscan tomb at Vulci in Italy and is now in the Vatican Museums.

On its main side, the heroes Achilles and Ajax sit facing each other on blocks, bent over a gaming board during a lull in the fighting at Troy, their spears over their shoulders and shields propped behind them. Inscriptions give their names and their calls: Achilles says "four", Ajax "three". Achilles, still wearing his helmet, is the stronger player.

The scene is not in Homer or any surviving poem, and may be Exekias' own invention. Its power lies in its stillness and concentration: the two greatest Greek warriors absorbed in a game, while the war waits around them. Later readers have seen foreboding in it — both heroes will die at Troy.

Exekias' work shows black-figure painting at its height: figures picked out in fine incised detail, with patterned cloaks and armour drawn with astonishing precision. Within a few years, Athenian painters began to adopt the new red-figure technique, and the scene of the board game was copied many times.
