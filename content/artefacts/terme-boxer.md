<!-- field: summary -->
A bronze boxer shown battered and resting after a bout, one of the few large original Greek bronzes to survive at all, dug up on Rome's Quirinal Hill in 1885.

<!-- field: significance -->
Greek sculpture turning away from victorious youth to a battered professional resting after a fight — and one of the very few large Greek bronzes to survive at all, so that shift can be read in an original rather than a Roman marble copy.

<!-- field: body -->
Workers clearing ground on the Quirinal Hill for a planned theatre in March 1885 struck bronze several metres below the surface. What they had found was a life-size seated boxer, still wearing the leather thongs boxers bound around their hands and forearms, his head turned as if someone had just spoken to him. The scholar overseeing the dig, Rodolfo Lanciani, recognised immediately that this was no ordinary find.

The figure is not an idealised athlete but a specific, damaged body: a broken nose, cauliflower ears, and facial wounds picked out with inlaid copper to suggest fresh blood and bruising under strain and exhaustion. It is exactly the kind of unsparing, individualised realism associated with Hellenistic sculpture's break from Classical idealism, though scholars have argued for dates anywhere from the late 4th to the 2nd century BC and disagree on which historical boxer, if any, it represents.

Its depth below ground and careful positioning have led some to suggest it was deliberately buried in antiquity, perhaps to protect it during a period of unrest, rather than simply lost or discarded. Whatever the reason, that burial is exactly why it survived: alongside the Riace Bronzes and the Artemision Bronze, it is one of the vanishingly rare large Greek bronzes that escaped being melted down for scrap.
