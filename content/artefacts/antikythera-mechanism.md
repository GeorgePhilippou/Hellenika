<!-- field: summary -->
A geared bronze astronomical calculator from a shipwreck — technology with no known equal for over a thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Sponge divers found it in 1901 in a Roman-era wreck off Antikythera. For decades it was a corroded lump. X-ray and, from 2005, microfocus CT scanning revealed at least 30 interlocking bronze gears, some with teeth under 1.5 mm, and thousands of characters of Greek text serving as an instruction manual.

Turning a handle set the whole solar system moving: the date on an Egyptian calendar dial, the position of the sun and moon in the zodiac, the phase of the moon on a rotating silver-and-black ball, eclipse predictions on a 223-month Saros spiral, and the four-year cycle of Panhellenic games. A pin-and-slot mechanism reproduces the moon's varying speed — an epicyclic correction implemented in bronze.

Nothing of comparable complexity survives from anywhere for at least 1,400 years. That such a device existed at all implies a workshop tradition behind it, of which nothing else has been found.
