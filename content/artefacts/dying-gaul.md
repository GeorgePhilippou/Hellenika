A defeated Celtic warrior dying on his shield — a monument by the victors that treats the enemy with dignity.

<!-- field: significance -->
A victory monument that gives the defeated enemy full dignity — one of the most moving images of death to survive from antiquity.

<!-- field: body -->
A marble statue of a dying Celtic warrior, life-size, a Roman copy of a bronze original made at Pergamon around 230–220 BC. The copy came to light in Rome in the early 17th century and is recorded in the Ludovisi collection by 1623. It is now in the Capitoline Museums, Rome.

The original belonged to a group of statues dedicated by King Attalus I of Pergamon after his victories over the Galatians, Celtic invaders who had settled in central Anatolia. The warrior sits on his fallen shield beside a broken trumpet, propped on one weakening arm, bleeding from a wound in his side, head bowed. His spiky, lime-stiffened hair, moustache and the torc (neck ring) at his throat match ancient descriptions of Celts.

Although it is a monument to victory, it shows the defeated enemy with strength and dignity, his death a private, quiet moment. There is calculation in this — a formidable enemy made the Attalids' victory greater — but the result is one of the most humane images of ancient art.

Napoleon's forces took it to Paris in 1797; it returned to Rome in 1816. Admired by Grand Tour travellers, it was one of the most copied ancient sculptures in Europe.
