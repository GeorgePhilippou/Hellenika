<!-- field: summary -->
A floor mosaic from Pompeii made of over a million tesserae, copying a lost Greek painting of Alexander facing Darius.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous image of Alexander the Great, and the closest surviving view of a lost masterpiece of Greek painting.

<!-- field: body -->
A floor mosaic about 5.8 by 3.1 m, made of more than a million tiny stone pieces, laid around 120–100 BC in the House of the Faun, one of the grandest houses in Pompeii. It was uncovered in 1831, lifted and moved to Naples in 1843, and is now in the National Archaeological Museum, Naples.

It shows Alexander's battle against the Persian king Darius III, probably at Issus in 333 BC. Alexander charges in from the left, bareheaded, spearing a Persian noble who has put himself between Alexander and the king. Darius, at the centre in his chariot, turns back with an arm outstretched as his driver whips the horses away. He is shown not as a coward but as a man watching a disaster he can no longer stop. A horse seen from behind, in bold foreshortening, anchors the middle of the scene.

The mosaic is a copy of a Greek painting made within a generation of the battle, perhaps by Philoxenos of Eretria, whom ancient writers say painted Alexander's battle against Darius. It uses the four colours — red, yellow, black and white — that Pliny says the great Greek painters favoured.

Almost no large Greek paintings survive, so this Roman-era copy is the best guide to what they were like.
