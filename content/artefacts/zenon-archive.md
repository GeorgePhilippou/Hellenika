<!-- field: summary -->
About 2,000 Greek papyri of the 3rd century BC from the files of Zenon, manager of a great estate in the Fayum under Ptolemy II.

<!-- field: significance -->
The richest window onto daily life and government in Ptolemaic Egypt.

<!-- field: body -->
Zenon was a Greek from Kaunos in Caria who worked for Apollonius, the *dioikētēs* or finance minister of Ptolemy II. Ptolemy gave Apollonius a huge estate of about 10,000 arouras (some 2,750 hectares) of reclaimed land at Philadelphia, a new town in the Fayum, the fertile basin west of the Nile. From about 256 BC Zenon ran it.

He kept his papers, and around 1914–15 local diggers found them at Philadelphia. The archive was sold off and is now divided among collections in Cairo, London, Florence, the University of Michigan and elsewhere. About 2,000 documents survive, dating from about 261 to 229 BC.

They are letters, accounts, contracts, petitions and memoranda, in Greek, with a few in demotic. They show the estate's vineyards, olive groves and wheat fields, irrigation works and imported Greek crops and livestock; Egyptian farm workers complaining of unpaid wages or striking; officials, tax collectors and travellers; and Zenon's own interests, including his greyhound and the education of young athletes. They reveal the close-run, profit-minded management of Ptolemaic Egypt, and the meeting of Greek settlers and Egyptian villagers.
