<!-- field: summary -->
A marble Nike shown mid-descent, wind pressing her drapery against her body, set on a nine-metre pedestal at Olympia by the sculptor Paionios of Mende.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its base inscription makes it one of the few major Classical Greek statues that can be securely dated, attributed to a named sculptor, and tied to a specific historical event, rather than reconstructed from Roman copies and guesswork.

<!-- field: body -->
Visitors to Olympia would have seen the statue from a striking distance below: the pedestal alone stood nearly nine metres, putting Nike far above eye level, apparently just touching down after flight. Only her torso and part of the drapery survive intact, but enough remains to show wind-blown cloth clinging to and pulling away from the body in the same figure -- a technical display as much as a religious dedication.

The base inscription is unusually informative. It names the dedicators, the Messenians and Naupactians, and the sculptor, Paionios of Mende, who is otherwise attested as the sculptor of pedimental figures at Olympia's Temple of Zeus. Scholars connect the dedication to the Athenian-allied capture of the Spartan garrison on Sphacteria in 425 BC during the Peloponnesian War, in which Messenian and Naupactian troops served alongside Athens -- though the inscription itself does not name the battle, and the link rests on the coincidence of date and the two cities' known involvement.

Its display at Olympia, a Panhellenic sanctuary shared by all Greeks including Sparta, made the dedication a pointed public statement: a monument to a Spartan defeat, standing permanently within Sparta's own religious world.
