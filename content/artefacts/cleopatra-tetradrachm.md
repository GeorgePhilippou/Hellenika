<!-- field: summary -->
Coins struck by Cleopatra VII showing a hook-nosed, sharp-chinned ruler — nothing like the later legend of her beauty.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only portraits of Cleopatra made in her lifetime with her own approval — and they look nothing like the legend.

<!-- field: body -->
Silver coins issued by Cleopatra VII, the last Ptolemaic ruler of Egypt, between 51 and 30 BC, struck at Alexandria and at other mints, including Ascalon and Antioch. Examples are in the British Museum and many other collections.

Her coin portraits show a strong hooked nose, a prominent chin, a firm mouth and large eyes, with her hair tied back in a bun and a royal diadem. They closely resemble the coin portraits of earlier Ptolemaic queens. The effect is of a ruler asserting her place in a long dynasty, not of a beauty: on money, looking like a legitimate monarch mattered more than looking attractive. Plutarch, writing more than a century later, says much the same — that her beauty was not in itself remarkable, but her presence, voice and conversation were irresistible.

Some coins struck in the eastern Mediterranean show Cleopatra on one side and Mark Antony on the other, their profiles cut in a similar style, advertising their political partnership against Octavian.

The later image of Cleopatra as a dangerous seductress comes largely from Roman propaganda after her defeat at Actium in 31 BC, and from later drama and film, not from the evidence of her own coins.
