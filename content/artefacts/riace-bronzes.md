<!-- field: summary -->
Two life-size Greek bronze warriors recovered from the sea in 1972 — original Classical bronzes, not copies.

<!-- field: body -->
Found by a snorkelling tourist off Riace in Calabria in 1972, probably jettisoned or accidentally lost overboard from a Roman-era ship carrying looted Greek art toward Italy, and left undisturbed on the seabed for roughly two thousand years until their chance rediscovery.

They matter above all because they are genuine Greek originals rather than later copies. Almost everything modern scholarship knows about Classical Greek sculpture comes filtered through Roman-era marble copies made centuries after the originals, and those copies are strikingly different from the real thing now that it can actually be compared directly. The bronze surface, freshly cast, would have shown as a warm gold-brown colour rather than the green patina visible today. Lips and nipples were inlaid in reddish copper, teeth in silver, and eyes in ivory and coloured glass paste, together producing a startlingly lifelike effect no plain marble copy could ever convincingly reproduce. Statue A's expression in particular, with slightly parted lips and inset eyes looking a little to one side, has a psychological specificity and immediacy that feels genuinely different in kind from the blanker gaze of marble sculpture.

Following their recovery the two statues underwent a long, painstaking restoration process in Florence, during which conservators found and carefully documented preserved internal core material, remnants of the clay and support structure used in the original ancient casting process, still sealed inside the hollow bronze bodies after twenty-five centuries. Altogether, the bronzes demonstrate concretely what was lost across antiquity whenever bronze statues were melted down for their metal, whether for coinage, weapons, or later building material: not merely individual artworks, but an entire visual register of ancient sculpture — polychrome, subtly glinting with inlaid materials, and considerably closer to the appearance of a living human body than plain white marble, itself once brightly painted too, ever really suggested to modern eyes used to seeing it bare.
