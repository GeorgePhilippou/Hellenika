<!-- field: summary -->
Pheidias' colossal seated statue of Zeus in gold and ivory, made for the god's temple at Olympia — one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most admired cult statue of antiquity, said to have changed how people imagined the king of the gods.

<!-- field: body -->
After completing the Athena Parthenos in Athens, the sculptor Pheidias went to Olympia, where in the 430s BC he made the cult statue for the new Temple of Zeus. Zeus sat on an elaborate throne of cedar inlaid with ebony, ivory, gold and precious stones, holding a figure of Victory in his right hand and a sceptre topped by an eagle in his left. The figure was about 12 m tall, its flesh of ivory and its robe and hair of gold, built over a wooden framework. It nearly filled the temple: ancient writers joked that if Zeus stood up he would go through the roof.

Pausanias, who saw it in the 2nd century AD, described it in detail, and its image appears on coins of Elis. It was counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, and ancient writers said that to see it was to forget one's troubles. Keeping the ivory from cracking in Olympia's damp climate required constant care with oil.

The workshop where Pheidias built the statue, just west of the temple, was excavated in the 1950s; it held tools, clay moulds for the gold drapery and a cup inscribed "I belong to Pheidias".

The statue's fate is uncertain. According to later Byzantine sources it was taken to Constantinople and destroyed in a fire in the 5th century AD.
