<!-- field: summary -->
A striding god caught mid-throw, one of the very few large Greek bronzes to survive as an original rather than a Roman copy, pulled from a shipwreck off Euboea in two pieces decades apart.

<!-- field: significance -->
Alongside the Riace Bronzes, it is direct physical evidence for what Classical Greek bronze casting actually looked like, rather than what marble copyists later approximated -- and its identity is still argued over precisely because the one detail that would settle it, the object once held in the missing hand, never surfaced.

<!-- field: body -->
Sponge divers found the first fragments off Cape Artemision in 1926; further pieces, including the head and torso, came up only in 1928, meaning the statue was assembled from a delayed, two-stage recovery of the same wreck. It shows a bearded god at the height of a throw, weight fully back on the right leg, left arm extended for aim, right arm cocked behind the shoulder -- a pose of total, coiled control rather than strain.

Whatever he once held in his right hand is gone, and with it the surest way to name him. A thunderbolt would make him Zeus; a trident would make him Poseidon. Both readings have serious defenders, and the debate itself is a useful reminder of how much meaning in Greek sculpture depended on now-missing attributes rather than the pose alone.

The wreck that carried it seems to date centuries after the statue's own casting, suggesting it was old cargo -- part of the same current of looted or purchased Greek art moving toward Italy that later swept up the Riace Bronzes -- rather than a contemporary shipment. Both wrecks preserve, by accident, exactly the kind of large-scale original bronze that peacetime Greek and Roman recycling otherwise destroyed almost without exception.
