A Corinthian olpe carrying the earliest detailed depiction of hoplites fighting in formation.

<!-- field: significance -->
The earliest detailed picture of hoplites fighting in formation, and a masterpiece of Corinthian miniature painting.

<!-- field: body -->
A painted pottery jug (olpe) 26 cm tall, made in Corinth around 650–640 BC. It was found in 1881 in an Etruscan tomb at Monte Aguzzo near Veii, north of Rome, on the estate of Prince Mario Chigi, and is now in the National Etruscan Museum at the Villa Giulia, Rome.

It is painted in the Protocorinthian miniature style, with figures only a few centimetres high, in several colours rather than plain silhouette. Its most famous band shows two lines of heavily armed infantrymen — hoplites — advancing on each other with round shields, crested helmets, greaves and spears held overhand, while a piper plays to keep them in step. A second group is still running up to join the line. Other bands show a lion hunt, a hare hunt, horsemen and a scene often read as the Judgement of Paris.

The vase is the key image in the debate over the hoplite phalanx: whether this close-packed way of fighting appeared suddenly in the 7th century BC as a "hoplite reform", or developed gradually. It shows that something like a formation of armoured infantry existed by the mid-7th century.

Its findspot in an Etruscan tomb shows how Greek luxury goods were reaching Italy through trade.
