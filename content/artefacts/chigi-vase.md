<!-- field: summary -->
A Corinthian olpe carrying the earliest detailed depiction of hoplites fighting in formation.

<!-- field: body -->
Painted in the miniaturist Protocorinthian style with figures a few centimetres high, in outline and polychrome rather than pure silhouette.

The main frieze shows two lines of hoplites advancing towards each other with round *aspis* shields, thrusting spears held overhand, crested helmets and greaves. A piper plays to keep them in step — exactly as Thucydides later describes the Spartans doing. One group is still arriving, spears held differently, which suggests a phalanx forming rather than a static line.

This is the primary visual evidence for hoplite warfare in the seventh century BC, and it is a central exhibit in the argument about whether the phalanx arrived suddenly as a "hoplite reform" or developed gradually.

The famous battle scene is only one register among several bands of decoration circling the vessel: separate friezes show a lion hunt and what appears to be a judgement scene, along with a frieze of grazing animals, so the small olpe packs a considerable variety of narrative and decorative subjects onto a single object only a few centimetres tall, a demonstration of Protocorinthian miniaturist skill as remarkable as the historical content of its most celebrated scene.
