<!-- field: summary -->
A solid gold burial casket embossed with the Macedonian star, holding cremated royal bones and a gold oak wreath.

<!-- field: significance -->
The centrepiece of the richest unlooted royal tomb in Greece, and possibly the casket of Philip II, father of Alexander the Great.

<!-- field: body -->
A solid gold casket (larnax) weighing about 11 kg, made in Macedon around 336–316 BC. Manolis Andronikos found it in 1977 inside a marble sarcophagus in the main chamber of Tomb II, beneath the Great Tumulus at Vergina, ancient Aigai, the old royal capital of Macedon. It is now in the Museum of the Royal Tombs of Aigai at Vergina.

The lid is embossed with a sixteen-rayed star, the emblem of the Macedonian royal house. Inside were cremated bones wrapped in a cloth of purple and gold, with a gold wreath of oak leaves and acorns. A second, smaller gold larnax in the antechamber held the bones of a young woman, wrapped in a similar cloth with a gold diadem.

Whose bones they are is debated. Philip II, assassinated in 336 BC, is the leading candidate, supported by the tomb's date, its wealth, and small ivory portrait heads found nearby. Others argue for Alexander's half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus, buried in 316 BC. Studies of the skeletons have reached opposite conclusions.

The star on the lid has become a powerful national symbol in Greece and in the dispute over the name "Macedonia".
