<!-- field: summary -->
A marble statue of the god Hermes holding the baby Dionysus, found at Olympia in 1877 and attributed to Praxiteles.

<!-- field: significance -->
Possibly the only surviving original by one of the greatest Greek sculptors — and the classic example of 4th-century grace and softness.

<!-- field: body -->
In 1877 German excavators at Olympia found a marble statue of Hermes lying in the Temple of Hera, exactly where Pausanias had seen "a Hermes of stone carrying the infant Dionysus, a work of Praxiteles" in the 2nd century AD. It is now in the Archaeological Museum of Olympia.

The god stands with his weight on one leg in a relaxed S-curve, leaning on a tree trunk over which his cloak is draped. On his left arm he holds the infant Dionysus, whom, in myth, he is carrying to be raised by the nymphs; his missing right hand probably held up a bunch of grapes, teasing the child. The marble is finely polished to suggest soft flesh, and the face is dreamy and gentle.

If by Praxiteles, made around 340–330 BC, it would be the only surviving original by one of the most famous Greek sculptors, whose other works, such as the Aphrodite of Knidos, are known only from copies. But some scholars argue that details of its carving, the back and the support point to a Hellenistic or Roman work, perhaps by a later sculptor named Praxiteles or a copy. The debate is unresolved.

Whoever made it, the statue is the finest surviving example of the graceful, relaxed style of 4th-century BC sculpture, very different from the grandeur of the Parthenon.
