<!-- field: summary -->
Lifelike painted portraits of men, women and children of Roman Egypt, placed over their mummies in the 1st–3rd centuries AD.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only large group of ancient panel paintings to survive, and the faces of the Greek-Egyptian society of the Roman period.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek painters such as Apelles and Zeuxis were the most admired artists of antiquity, but their panel paintings have all perished. The closest we can come to their art is a group of about a thousand portraits from Roman Egypt, found mainly in the cemeteries of the Fayum, the fertile basin west of the Nile, and at other sites such as Antinoopolis.

They were painted on thin wooden panels in hot wax (encaustic) or in tempera, in the Greek naturalistic tradition, and then wrapped into the linen bandages of the mummy so that the face looked out — a fusion of Egyptian burial custom with Greek and Roman portraiture. The sitters, men, women and children, wear Roman hairstyles, jewellery and clothes, and many have Greek names. They were probably descendants of the Greek settlers of the Ptolemaic period who had intermarried with Egyptians. Some portraits may have been painted in life, others at death.

In 1887 the Viennese dealer Theodor Graf acquired many found at er-Rubayat, and in 1887–88 and 1911 Flinders Petrie excavated more at Hawara, recording their contexts. The custom faded in the 3rd century AD.
