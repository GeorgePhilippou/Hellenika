<!-- field: summary -->
A priestly decree of 238 BC in hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek, honouring Ptolemy III and Berenice II and reforming the calendar.

<!-- field: significance -->
A fuller trilingual text than the Rosetta Stone, it confirmed the decipherment of hieroglyphs and records an early attempt at a leap year.

<!-- field: body -->
In March 238 BC the priests of Egypt met in synod at Canopus, near Alexandria, and passed a decree honouring Ptolemy III and Queen Berenice II for their gifts to the temples, the return of sacred statues carried off by the Persians, and their relief of a famine with imported grain. They established a new class of priests in their honour and deified their young daughter Berenice, who had just died.

The decree also reformed the calendar. The Egyptian year of 365 days slipped against the seasons by a day every four years; the priests ordered an extra day every fourth year — the idea behind the leap year. The reform was not kept, and it was the Romans under Augustus who made a leap year last in Egypt.

Like the Rosetta Stone, the decree was inscribed on stone in hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek and set up in temples. In 1866 the German Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius found a complete copy at Tanis in the Delta, now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo; other copies have been found since. Its long, nearly undamaged texts confirmed that Champollion's method of reading hieroglyphs, worked out from the Rosetta Stone, was correct.
