<!-- field: summary -->
The oldest complete manuscript of Homer's Iliad, copied in 10th-century Constantinople, with ancient scholarly notes in its margins.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most important surviving witness to the text of the Iliad and to the work of the Alexandrian scholars who edited it.

<!-- field: body -->
Venetus A is a large parchment book of 327 leaves, copied in Constantinople around the middle of the 10th century, a time of renewed interest in classical texts. It contains the whole *Iliad*, with extensive notes (*scholia*) written around the text in smaller script. These notes are excerpts from ancient commentaries, going back to the Alexandrian scholars Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium and especially Aristarchus, who edited Homer in the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC. The manuscript even preserves the critical signs Aristarchus used in the margins to mark lines he doubted.

The manuscript belonged to Cardinal Bessarion, who left his library to Venice in 1468, and it is now in the Biblioteca Marciana as *Marcianus Graecus* Z. 454 (= 822). Its importance was recognised in 1788, when the French scholar Villoison published its scholia. They showed that the ancient text of Homer had varied widely, which helped the German scholar F. A. Wolf argue in 1795 that the Homeric poems were composed orally and written down only later, launching the modern "Homeric Question".

Since 2007 the Homer Multitext project has made complete digital images of it freely available.
