<!-- field: summary -->
The longest Greek law code to survive, inscribed on stone at Gortyn in Crete in the 5th century BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
A unique first-hand record of Greek law in practice, especially on marriage, inheritance and slavery.

<!-- field: body -->
The Gortyn code was carved around 480–450 BC on the curved wall of a public building at Gortyn, a leading city of central Crete. In the Roman period the blocks were reused in a later building, the Odeion, where they survive. The Italian archaeologist Federico Halbherr found most of the text in 1884.

The inscription runs to twelve columns and over 600 lines in the local Doric dialect and early Cretan alphabet. It is written *boustrophedon*, "as the ox ploughs": one line runs right to left, the next left to right.

It is not a complete law code but a collection of laws, chiefly on family and property. It sets out rules on marriage and divorce, the property rights of women, inheritance, the marriage of heiresses, adoption, rape and adultery, and debt. It distinguishes carefully between free citizens, serfs and slaves, setting different penalties and rights for each; a woman could own property and keep it on divorce.

Because almost all other Greek law is known only from speeches and later writers, the Gortyn code is precious evidence of how a Greek city actually regulated family life and status. It is still in place at Gortyn.
