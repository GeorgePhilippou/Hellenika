<!-- field: summary -->
A drinking cup from a Greek colony in Italy inscribed with a joke that assumes the audience knows Homer.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the earliest Greek inscriptions and the earliest witness to the Homeric epics — a joke that assumed its readers knew the Iliad.

<!-- field: body -->
A small pottery drinking cup made in Rhodes and inscribed around 730–720 BC. Giorgio Buchner found it in 1954 in a child's cremation grave at Pithekoussai, one of the earliest Greek trading settlements in the west, on the island of Ischia off the Bay of Naples. It is now in the island's archaeological museum.

Scratched on its side, in three lines in the Euboean form of the alphabet, mostly in hexameter verse, is a joke: "I am the cup of Nestor, good to drink from. Whoever drinks from this cup, desire for beautiful-crowned Aphrodite will seize him at once." In the *Iliad*, Nestor owns a massive gold cup so heavy that only he can lift it full; this cup is small, cheap clay, mock-heroically claiming the same name.

The joke only works if drinkers knew the Homeric story. So around 725 BC, Greek settlers far from the Aegean were literate enough to write playful verse, and familiar enough with epic poetry to enjoy a parody of it. Whether the reference is to the *Iliad* itself or to the wider tradition behind it is debated.

Why a cup carrying an adult drinking-party joke was buried with a child is unknown.
