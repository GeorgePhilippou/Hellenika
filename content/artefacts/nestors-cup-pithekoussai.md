<!-- field: summary -->
A drinking cup from a Greek colony in Italy inscribed with a joke that assumes the audience knows Homer.

<!-- field: body -->
Found in a child's cremation burial at Pithekoussai, one of the earliest Greek trading settlements anywhere in the western Mediterranean, on the island of Ischia off the Italian coast. Scratched onto the cup in three lines, largely in hexameter verse, is a small joke: "I am the cup of Nestor, good to drink from. Whoever drinks from this cup, desire for beautifully-crowned Aphrodite will seize him instantly."

The joke depends entirely on the audience already knowing the Iliad. Nestor's cup in that poem is a massive golden vessel, so heavy that Homer specifically notes only Nestor himself could lift it easily when full — an old warrior's oversized heirloom. This object is, by contrast, a small, plain clay drinking cup of the kind anyone at a party might use, wittily claiming grand Homeric status it obviously does not have.

The implications of the object are genuinely large for such a small cup. Around 720 BC, Greek settlers on a small island far from mainland Greece were not merely literate but confident enough in that literacy to write playful verse for entertainment, and could rely on a shared, casual knowledge of Homeric material spreading among their audience — evidence that pushes the diffusion of both epic poetry and the alphabet itself earlier and further from the Aegean than scholars once assumed likely. That such a witty, adult drinking-party joke was buried specifically with a child, rather than an adult who might plausibly have owned or used it at a symposium, adds a poignant detail nobody has satisfactorily explained.
