<!-- field: summary -->
A merchant ship that sank off southern Turkey around 1300 BC, its cargo the richest record of Late Bronze Age trade ever found.

<!-- field: significance -->
A time capsule of the interconnected Bronze Age world, showing the long-distance trade on which the Mycenaean palaces depended.

<!-- field: body -->
In 1982 a Turkish sponge diver reported seeing metal "biscuits with ears" on the seabed off Uluburun, near Kaş on the south coast of Turkey. They were copper ingots from a ship that had sunk around 1300 BC. From 1984 to 1994 the Institute of Nautical Archaeology excavated the wreck at depths of 44–61 m, in over 22,000 dives.

The ship was about 15 m long, built of cedar. Its cargo was extraordinary: about ten tonnes of copper in some 350 ingots, mostly from Cyprus, and a tonne of tin — the right proportion to make bronze; ingots of blue glass; ebony and ivory; ostrich eggs; amber from the Baltic; resin, spices and wine in Canaanite jars; Cypriot pottery; gold and silver jewellery, including a gold scarab of the Egyptian queen Nefertiti; weapons; and a folding wooden writing tablet. Goods came from at least seven cultures, including Mycenaean Greece.

The ship was probably sailing west from the Levant or Cyprus towards the Aegean, perhaps carrying a royal shipment. Its cargo shows the scale of the exchange of raw materials and luxury goods that linked the palace states of Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus, Anatolia and Mycenaean Greece — the world that collapsed a century later.

The finds are displayed in the Bodrum Museum of Underwater Archaeology.
