<!-- field: summary -->
A giant bronze statue of the sun god Helios set up at Rhodes around 280 BC — one of the Seven Wonders, which stood for only 54 years.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous of the lost Wonders, a symbol of Hellenistic ambition and engineering, and the origin of the word "colossal".

<!-- field: body -->
After Rhodes survived the year-long siege of Demetrius Poliorcetes in 305–304 BC, the Rhodians sold the siege engines he had left behind and used the money to build a thank-offering to their patron god, Helios, the Sun. The sculptor Chares of Lindos, a pupil of Lysippos, spent about twelve years on it, finishing around 280 BC.

The statue was about 33 m tall, roughly the height of the Statue of Liberty from its feet to its crown. It was built of bronze plates over an internal framework of iron bars, weighted with stone, on a marble base. Its exact pose and location are unknown. The popular image of a giant standing astride the harbour mouth, with ships passing between its legs, is a later invention: a statue that size could not have been built that way, and it probably stood near the harbour or on the acropolis.

Around 226 BC an earthquake broke it at the knees and it fell. The Rhodians, citing an oracle, did not rebuild it. Its huge fragments lay where they fell for some eight centuries; Pliny says few people could get their arms around its thumb. In AD 654 Arab forces raiding Rhodes reportedly sold the bronze for scrap.

It was counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, and its name gave us the word "colossal".
