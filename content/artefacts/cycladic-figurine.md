<!-- field: summary -->
Slender marble figures with folded arms and blank faces — abstract to modern eyes, and originally brightly painted.

<!-- field: significance -->
The signature art of the Early Bronze Age Cyclades — and a lesson in how looting erases meaning, since most known figurines have no recorded findspot.

<!-- field: body -->
Marble figurines made in the Cycladic islands of the Aegean around 2800–2300 BC, during the Early Bronze Age. The classic type is a standing nude woman, a few centimetres to over a metre tall, with arms folded right under left across the stomach, and a flat, tilted-back face with only a ridge for a nose. They were shaped with emery, a hard abrasive found on Naxos, and polished with pumice.

Today they look white and abstract, but they were painted. Traces of red and blue pigment survive, and "paint ghosts" — differences in weathering — show where eyes, hair and patterned markings once were. Most cannot stand: their feet point downward, so they were made to lie flat, which fits their usual findspot in graves. Many were broken in antiquity, sometimes deliberately at the neck, perhaps as part of funeral rites. Whether they represent goddesses, ancestors, servants or the dead themselves is unknown.

The first excavated examples with known contexts came from Christos Tsountas's work in Cycladic cemeteries such as Chalandriani on Syros in 1898–99. In the 20th century their simple forms appealed to modern artists such as Brancusi, Modigliani and Henry Moore. The resulting collectors' market drove massive looting of graves, and forgeries entered collections; most figurines now in museums have no recorded archaeological context.
