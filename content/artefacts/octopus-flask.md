<!-- field: summary -->
A 27-centimetre terracotta flask from Palaikastro, its surface filled by octopuses and seabed motifs — an iconic work of Minoan Marine Style.

<!-- field: body -->
This painted terracotta flask was found at Palaikastro on the eastern coast of Crete and dates to approximately 1500–1450 BC, during the Minoan New Palace period. Now in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum, it stands about 27 centimetres high. Its rounded body and paired handles provide a continuous surface on which the marine composition unfolds.

Large octopuses dominate the decoration. Although their bodies face the viewer, they appear to swim diagonally, while their tentacles extend, curl and overlap across the vessel's curved surface. Smaller sea urchins, triton shells, rocks and seaweed occupy the spaces between them. This dense arrangement—sometimes described by the art-historical term *horror vacui*, or fear of empty space—makes the pot itself seem like an underwater environment rather than a neutral support for a flat picture.

The vessel belongs to the Marine Style, a distinctive phase of Late Minoan pottery in which sea life was adapted closely to the shapes of ceramic vessels. The imagery reflects the importance and familiarity of the sea to communities on Crete, but its precise meaning and the flask's original use cannot be established from the decoration alone. It is therefore safer to read the design as evidence for artistic observation and ceramic practice than as proof of a particular religious symbolism.

The museum attributes the flask to the so-called Marine Style Master. A closely related stirrup jar from Gournia—with similar octopus imagery—is attributed to the same painter. That comparison suggests shared artistic practice across eastern Crete, although the surviving objects do not reveal exactly how the painter or workshop was organised.
