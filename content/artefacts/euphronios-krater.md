A red-figure krater showing the death of Sarpedon — a masterpiece of Greek vase painting, and the object that changed museum acquisition ethics.

A masterpiece of early red-figure painting — and the looted object whose return changed how museums acquire antiquities.

<!-- field: body -->
A red-figure calyx krater (wine-mixing bowl), made in Athens around 515 BC and signed by the painter Euphronios and the potter Euxitheos. It is now in the National Etruscan Museum at Cerveteri, Italy.

Its main scene comes from *Iliad* Book 16: the winged figures of Sleep and Death lift the body of the Trojan ally Sarpedon, son of Zeus, from the battlefield, while Hermes watches. Blood runs from Sarpedon's wounds. Euphronios belonged to the "Pioneer Group" of Athenian painters who explored the new red-figure technique, in which figures were left in the red of the clay and details drawn with a brush. That freedom let him show muscles, twisting poses and foreshortening in a way the older black-figure silhouettes could not.

The krater was looted from an Etruscan tomb near Cerveteri in 1971. In 1972 the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York bought it for $1 million, then a record for any ancient work, with a false story that it came from an old family collection. After an Italian investigation into the antiquities trade, the Met returned it to Italy in 2008.

The case, with others, led museums to adopt much stricter rules on the provenance of the antiquities they buy.
