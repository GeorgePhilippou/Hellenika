<!-- field: summary -->
An Attic black-figure krater carrying over 270 figures in six friezes, signed by both its potter and its painter.

<!-- field: significance -->
A picture-book of Greek myth as it stood around 570 BC, and the masterpiece of early Athenian black-figure painting.

<!-- field: body -->
A large black-figure krater (wine-mixing bowl), 66 cm tall, made in Athens around 570–565 BC and signed twice each by the potter Ergotimos and the painter Kleitias. It was exported to Etruria, found by Alessandro François in 1844 in a tomb near Chiusi, and is now in the National Archaeological Museum, Florence.

Six bands of decoration carry more than 270 human and animal figures, over 120 of them labelled with their names. The central scene is the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, attended by the gods; around it run the Calydonian boar hunt, the funeral games for Patroclus, Achilles pursuing the Trojan prince Troilus, Theseus and the young Athenians dancing after their escape from the Minotaur, and the battle of the Lapiths and centaurs. The vase is thus an illustrated encyclopaedia of myth, preserving versions of stories before later poets fixed them differently.

It was made for drinking parties, not display: a bowl for mixing wine with water at a banquet. Its export to an Etruscan family shows the prestige of Athenian painted pottery in Italy.

In 1900 a museum guard smashed it into 638 pieces with a stool. It was reassembled, and restored again in 1973 using better methods.
