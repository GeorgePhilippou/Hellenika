<!-- field: summary -->
A colossal bronze wine-mixing vessel from a Macedonian tomb, its cast and hammered reliefs of Dionysus and his followers ranked among the finest surviving Greek metalwork.

<!-- field: significance -->
It shows a level of technical mastery in large-scale bronze casting and relief work that few other surviving objects can match, and demonstrates the wealth flowing through Macedonian aristocratic circles in Alexander's own generation.

<!-- field: body -->
Found by chance in 1962 during road construction near Derveni, north of Thessaloniki, the krater was already an heirloom by the time it was buried: an inscription identifies an earlier owner from Larissa in Thessaly, decades before it ended up holding the ashes of a Macedonian noble couple in their tomb. At over 90 cm tall and weighing more than 40 kilograms of bronze, it dwarfs the everyday grave goods buried alongside it.

Its decoration is exceptional even by the standards of the period: a running frieze in high relief shows Dionysus seated among an ecstatic company of maenads and satyrs, cast and worked with an anatomical fluency that has led some scholars to associate the workshop, speculatively, with major sculptural traditions of the fourth century BC. Smaller figures — animals, further Dionysiac attendants — decorate the neck and volutes.

It was recovered from the same cluster of tombs, and the same 1962 excavation, that produced the carbonised Derveni Papyrus — an accidental double discovery that makes the Derveni tombs one of the richest single archaeological finds from the Macedonian world of Alexander's own lifetime.
