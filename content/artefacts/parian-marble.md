A marble chronicle inscribed on the island of Paros in 264/3 BC, listing the dates of events in Greek history from mythical times.

<!-- field: significance -->
The oldest surviving Greek chronological table, showing how Greeks themselves dated their past — myth and history alike.

<!-- field: body -->
The Parian Marble (*Marmor Parium*) is a chronicle carved on a marble slab on the island of Paros in the Cyclades in 264/3 BC. Its author is unknown. It survives in pieces: the upper part was bought in Smyrna in 1627 for the Earl of Arundel and taken to London, where part was lost in the Civil War, and given to Oxford University in 1667; it is now in the Ashmolean Museum. A further fragment was found on Paros in 1897.

The inscription lists events from the reign of Cecrops, the legendary first king of Athens, dated by the Greeks to 1581 BC in our terms, down to 299/8 BC, counting each as so many years before 264/3 BC. It dates both what we would call myth and history: the flood of Deucalion, the arrival of Cadmus, the fall of Troy (1209/8 BC by its reckoning), the first Olympiad, the lives of poets such as Homer, Hesiod and Sappho, the battles of Marathon and Salamis, the deaths of Socrates and Alexander.

Its dates are mostly given by Athenian archons, and it shows a particular interest in poets, music and festivals. Some of its dates differ from those of other ancient chronographers, such as Eratosthenes.

The Parian Marble is invaluable evidence for ancient Greek chronology and for how Greeks constructed a single timeline out of legend and recorded history.
