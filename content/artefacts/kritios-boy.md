<!-- field: summary -->
A marble youth from around 480 BC, the first Greek statue with true contrapposto, buried by the Athenians themselves in the rubble left by the Persian sack of the Acropolis.

<!-- field: significance -->
It marks, more precisely than almost any other single object, the physical joint between Archaic and Classical Greek sculpture -- and the fact that it was buried as building rubble, not destroyed, is itself a direct trace of the Persian Wars.

<!-- field: body -->
Archaic kouroi stand rigidly frontal, weight distributed evenly on both legs, faces fixed in the stylised "Archaic smile". The Kritios Boy breaks that formula: his weight rests on his left leg, his right knee bends and trails, and his hips, torso and head all shift very slightly out of alignment in response -- the first Greek sculpture to render a standing body the way a body actually stands. The face, correspondingly, drops the Archaic smile for a calm, almost severe expression, a shift art historians label the "Severe Style".

Its torso and head were recovered decades apart, in 1865-66 and 1888, in excavations on the Acropolis's south slope -- a separation that itself reflects how the statue survived. It had been broken up and buried, along with a great deal of other sculpture, in the fill the Athenians used to rebuild and level the Acropolis after the Persians burned it in 480/479 BC. That burial, rather than destruction of the stone itself, is why the statue survives at all, and it gives it an unusually secure upper date limit: whatever it depicts, it was carved before the sack that buried it.

The attribution to the sculptor Kritios rests entirely on stylistic comparison with other works associated with him, not on any inscription or ancient text naming this specific statue -- a reminder that even a securely dated, well-preserved piece like this one can still carry an entirely modern name.
