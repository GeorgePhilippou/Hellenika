<!-- field: summary -->
The black conical stone worshipped as Aphrodite at her Paphos sanctuary, an aniconic cult image with no human features at all.

<!-- field: significance -->
It shows that even Aphrodite's most famous Greek sanctuary had, at its ritual core, an image with nothing Greek or anthropomorphic about it -- a reminder that Greek religion absorbed and reinterpreted much older Cypriot and Near Eastern cult practice rather than inventing everything itself.

<!-- field: body -->
Visitors expecting a statue of the goddess found something stranger: a smooth, dark, roughly conical stone standing on a base, with no carved face, limbs or drapery. Tacitus, describing the sanctuary in his account of Titus's visit in AD 69, found it notable enough to record in detail -- "the image of the goddess is not in human form... it is a rounded mass, broader at the base and rising like a turning-post to a small circumference" -- evidently as puzzled by it as any other Roman visitor would have been.

The stone's cult standing long outlasted any single building phase of the sanctuary: it appears on Roman provincial coinage from Cyprus, shown standing within a shrine flanked by attendants, centuries after the sanctuary's Late Bronze Age origins. That continuity, across a site rebuilt and administered by Cypriot kings, Ptolemies and Roman governors in turn, suggests the stone itself -- not any particular temple structure around it -- was the real, unbroken object of the cult.

Aniconic stone worship of this kind has closer parallels in Levantine and Near Eastern religion than in mainland Greek practice, fitting Cyprus's position as a cultural crossroads between the Aegean and the Near East. The Paphos baetyl is usually cited as the clearest surviving case of it within the Greek-speaking world, precisely because Greek and Roman visitors kept describing how unlike a normal cult statue it was.
