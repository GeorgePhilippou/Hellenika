<!-- field: summary -->
A libation vessel of carved rock crystal, reassembled from over 300 fragments — the finest surviving Minoan stone vessel.

<!-- field: significance -->
The finest stone vessel to survive from Minoan Crete, and a product of the only Minoan palace found unlooted.

<!-- field: body -->
A ritual pouring vessel (rhyton) carved from a single block of rock crystal, about 16 cm high, made on Crete around 1500–1450 BC. It was found by Nikolaos Platon's excavations, begun in 1961, in the treasury of the palace at Zakros on Crete's far eastern coast. Crushed into more than 300 fragments when the palace collapsed, it was reassembled and is now in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum.

Rock crystal is a very hard, transparent quartz that could not be cut with bronze tools. The vessel had to be ground into shape with abrasives such as emery, using drills and rubbing tools, with no way to correct a mistake. Its body is left plain to show off the stone. The neck ring is made of gilded faience, and the handle is a row of crystal beads threaded on bronze wire, found still in its original order.

Zakros, with a good harbour facing Egypt and the Levant, was the smallest of Crete's major palaces. Destroyed around 1450 BC, it was never resettled or robbed, so its shrine treasury was found almost as its owners left it, filled with vessels of stone. A second famous rhyton from the same palace, of green chlorite, is carved with a picture of a mountain sanctuary.
