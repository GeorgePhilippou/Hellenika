<!-- field: summary -->
Two bees holding a honeycomb between them, in gold — a technical showpiece of Minoan metalwork.

<!-- field: significance -->
The finest piece of jewellery to survive from Bronze Age Crete, and a showcase of Minoan goldsmiths' mastery of granulation.

<!-- field: body -->
A gold pendant 4.6 cm across, made on Crete around 1800–1700 BC. It was found in 1930 by the French School at Athens in Chrysolakkos, the elite burial complex of the palace town of Malia on Crete's north coast, and is now in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum.

Two insects face each other, their curved bodies meeting around a granulated disc — perhaps a drop of honey or a honeycomb. Their wings are made of fine wire, three small discs hang below, and a tiny cage above may once have held something. The goldsmith combined three demanding techniques: repoussé (hammering the sheet from behind), filigree (fine soldered wire) and granulation (fusing tiny gold beads to the surface without visible solder). Minoan craftsmen had mastered granulation by about 2000 BC; after the palace age it vanished from the Aegean for centuries.

The insects are usually called bees, though some argue their narrow waists suggest a wasp. Bees mattered: honey was the only concentrated sweetener, and later Linear B tablets record honey offered to the gods.

Chrysolakkos — "the pit of gold" — was robbed by local diggers from the 1880s. The British Museum's "Aegina Treasure", bought in 1892, is widely thought to have been looted from it. The pendant survived because it was found in a recorded excavation.
