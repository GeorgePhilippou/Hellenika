<!-- field: summary -->
Journey's end: twenty years after he left for Troy, Odysseus returns home in disguise to find his palace occupied by suitors besieging his wife.

<!-- field: myth -->
The Phaeacians leave Odysseus asleep on a beach he does not at first recognise — Athena has disguised both him and the island's landmarks. Disguised as a beggar, he learns that over a hundred suitors have occupied his palace for years, consuming his household while pressuring his wife Penelope to remarry and his son Telemachus to accept it. Recognised only by his old nurse (by a childhood scar) and his dying dog Argos, he strings his own great bow — a feat no suitor can manage — and, with Telemachus, kills every suitor in the hall. Penelope tests him with a final trick about their marriage bed before finally accepting he is really her husband.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Odyssey Books 13 through 23.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Unlike almost every other stop, Ithaca is not itself in geographical doubt — it is a real, named Greek island. What is genuinely disputed is whether the modern island of Ithaki matches Homer's own description of it (which some ancient and modern readers have found a poor fit, fuelling proposals that Homeric "Ithaca" was instead neighbouring Kefalonia or Lefkada) — a real scholarly debate, unlike the fantastical stops earlier in the voyage.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Excavations on Ithaki and neighbouring Kefalonia have found genuine Mycenaean-period remains, including a substantial site at Agios Athanasios on Ithaki sometimes promoted as "Odysseus's palace" — an identification not accepted by most specialists, but resting on real Late Bronze Age archaeology rather than pure invention.
