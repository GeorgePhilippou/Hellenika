<!-- field: summary -->
How the Greeks made a living: farming, crafts, trade by sea, slavery and the spread of coined money.

<!-- field: significance -->
Greek trade networks and coinage knit the Mediterranean together and spread Greek goods, wine, olive oil and culture far beyond Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Most Greeks lived by farming. Greece's thin soils and dry summers suited the "Mediterranean triad" of wheat or barley, olives and grapes, along with sheep and goats. Most farms were small family holdings, as Hesiod's *Works and Days* describes, though the rich owned larger estates worked by slaves or tenants.

Because good land was limited, Greek cities looked to the sea. From the 8th century BC colonies and trading posts — Al Mina in Syria, Pithekoussai in Italy, Naukratis in Egypt — linked Greece with the Near East, Egypt, Italy and the Black Sea. Greeks exported wine, olive oil, fine pottery and metalwork, and imported grain, timber, metals and slaves. Transport jars (*amphorae*), stamped with their city's mark, let archaeologists trace this trade. By the 5th century BC Athens depended on grain shipped from the Black Sea, which made control of the Hellespont a matter of survival.

Coinage, invented in Lydia in the late 7th century BC, spread rapidly through the Greek cities, making trade, taxation and the payment of soldiers and officials easier. Athens' silver owls, minted from the Laurion mines, became the most trusted currency of the Classical world.

Craftsmen, many of them resident foreigners or slaves, worked in small workshops. Markets (*agorai*), harbours such as Piraeus, banks and maritime loans developed. In the Hellenistic period royal economies, such as that of Ptolemaic Egypt, operated on a far larger scale.
