<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek city-state: a self-governing community of citizens with its town, its territory and its gods — the basic unit of Greek political life.

<!-- field: significance -->
Almost everything distinctively Greek — citizenship, democracy, political philosophy, the hoplite army — grew within the polis, and the word lives on in "politics".

<!-- field: body -->
A polis was a self-governing community of citizens: a town, usually with an acropolis and an agora (public square), together with the farmland around it. It took shape across the Greek world during the 8th century BC, the same period that saw the first monumental temples, the alphabet and the spread of colonies. By the Classical period there were well over a thousand poleis, from the Black Sea to Spain.

Most were small. A typical polis had a few hundred to a few thousand adult male citizens; Athens and Sparta, with tens of thousands, were exceptional. Each had its own laws, magistrates, coinage, calendar, army and patron gods, and fiercely guarded its independence (*autonomia*). Government ranged from kingship and narrow oligarchy to the democracy of Athens, but in nearly all, citizens shared in decisions through assemblies and councils.

The polis defined identity. Citizenship was usually inherited and jealously limited; women, slaves and resident foreigners lived in the city without full membership. Citizens fought for it as hoplites or rowers, and worshipped its gods together at its festivals. Aristotle called the human being a "political animal" — a creature made to live in a polis.

The independence of the poleis was also their weakness: they fought each other constantly. After Philip II and Alexander, real power passed to kings and then to Rome, though the polis survived as the framework of city life across the Hellenistic East.
