<!-- field: summary -->
The revival of Greek oratory and literature under the Roman empire, about AD 50–250, when star speakers recreated the language and themes of classical Athens.

<!-- field: significance -->
It shaped how the Roman empire, and later Byzantium, understood Greek culture: as the classical heritage of Athens, preserved in pure Attic Greek.

<!-- field: body -->
In the 230s AD the writer Philostratus wrote the *Lives of the Sophists*, in which he traced a "second sophistic" back to the orator Aeschines, but chiefly described the star speakers of his own and recent times. Modern scholars use the term for the whole Greek literary culture of the early Roman empire.

Its heroes were sophists — professional orators who travelled between the great cities, especially Athens, Smyrna and Ephesus, giving display speeches before huge audiences. They improvised on set themes, often drawn from classical history ("Should the Athenians bury their dead after Marathon?"), and spoke and wrote in an artificial Attic Greek modelled on Plato and Demosthenes, avoiding everyday koine. Famous sophists such as Dio Chrysostom, Polemon, Aelius Aristides and Herodes Atticus were celebrities, rich, honoured by emperors and fiercely competitive.

The same backward-looking pride in the classical past inspired Plutarch's *Lives*, Pausanias' guide to the monuments of Greece, Arrian's history of Alexander and the satire of Lucian. Under Roman rule, Greeks defined themselves by their culture, education (*paideia*) and history.
