<!-- field: summary -->
The tradition of rational enquiry into nature, knowledge, ethics and politics that began in Ionia in the 6th century BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The origin of Western philosophy and science: its questions, methods and schools shaped Roman, Islamic, medieval and modern thought.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek philosophy began in the Ionian city of Miletus in the early 6th century BC, when Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes tried to explain the world in terms of natural substances and processes rather than the actions of gods. These early thinkers, the Presocratics, offered bold theories: Heraclitus' world of constant change, Parmenides' unchanging being, Pythagoras' cosmos of numbers, Empedocles' four elements and the atoms of Leucippus and Democritus.

In the 5th century BC attention turned to human affairs. The Sophists taught rhetoric and questioned whether morality was natural or merely conventional. Socrates, in Athens, cross-examined his fellow citizens about justice, courage and the good life, and was executed in 399 BC.

His pupil Plato founded the Academy around 387 BC and wrote dialogues developing the theory of Forms and the ideal state of the *Republic*. Plato's pupil Aristotle founded the Lyceum in 335 BC and wrote on logic, nature, ethics, politics and poetry, laying foundations for many later sciences.

After Alexander, philosophy became a guide to living well. The Stoics of Zeno taught virtue and acceptance of fate; Epicurus taught a calm life free from fear, based on atomism; the Sceptics, following Pyrrho, suspended judgement; and the Cynics, such as Diogenes, rejected social conventions. These schools continued into the Roman world and, with Plato and Aristotle, shaped philosophy for two thousand years.
