<!-- field: summary -->
The painted pottery of ancient Greece, from abstract Geometric patterns to the black-figure and red-figure scenes of myth and daily life.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-surviving Greek pictorial art: thousands of painted vessels preserve images of myth, ritual, war, sport and daily life that would otherwise be lost.

<!-- field: body -->
Pottery survives far better than painting on walls or wood, so painted vases are the main record of Greek pictorial art. Fine pottery was made in many places, but Corinth and above all Athens led production.

After the Bronze Age, Protogeometric pottery (c. 1050–900 BC) and then Geometric pottery (c. 900–700 BC) were decorated with circles, meanders and zigzags; by the 8th century BC human figures returned, in scenes such as the funeral on the Dipylon Amphora. Contact with the Near East brought an "Orientalising" style in the 7th century BC, with animal friezes and monsters, led by Corinth.

In the black-figure technique, developed at Corinth and perfected in Athens, figures were painted in a slip that fired black, with details scratched through to the red clay and touched up in red and white. Masters such as Kleitias and Exekias made black-figure the dominant Athenian style until about 530 BC. Then Athenian potters invented red-figure, reversing the scheme: the background was painted black and the figures left in the red of the clay, with details drawn in fine lines. This freedom let painters such as Euphronios show anatomy, movement and emotion.

Many vases were made for the symposium and for funerals, and many were signed by their painters and potters. Athenian vases were exported widely, especially to Etruria. Production of fine figured pottery declined in the 4th century BC.
