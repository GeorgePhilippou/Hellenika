<!-- field: summary -->
How the works of Homer, Plato, the tragedians and the historians were copied, selected and preserved in Byzantium and passed to Renaissance Europe.

<!-- field: significance -->
Without Byzantine copyists and the scholars who brought their books to Italy, most of the Greek authors in this atlas would be lost.

<!-- field: body -->
Ancient books were written on papyrus rolls, which last a few centuries at most, so every text had to be recopied again and again to survive. In late antiquity texts were transferred from rolls to parchment books (codices), written in capital letters (uncial script). Works that were not chosen for copying, or for school reading, slowly disappeared.

In the 9th century, after the end of Iconoclasm, Byzantine scholars adopted a smaller, faster lowercase script, minuscule, and recopied much of classical literature into it; the earliest dated example is from 835. Scholars such as Photius and Arethas of Caesarea commissioned copies of Plato, Euclid and Aristotle, many of which still exist, and the *Iliad* manuscript known as Venetus A preserves ancient commentaries. A second revival in the Palaiologan period, around 1300, produced new editions of the poets.

Much had already been lost, especially in the Fourth Crusade of 1204. But what survived began to move west. From 1397 Manuel Chrysoloras taught Greek in Florence, and in the 15th century Byzantine scholars and refugees such as Bessarion brought or sold manuscripts to Italy. Printing completed the rescue: from 1495 Aldus Manutius in Venice printed first editions of Aristotle, Plato, the tragedians and many others.
