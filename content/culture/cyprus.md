<!-- field: summary -->
A copper-rich island facing the Levant, divided among small Greek–Phoenician city-kingdoms and later secured as one of the Ptolemies' most valuable possessions.

<!-- field: body -->
Cyprus sits close enough to the Levantine coast to trade with it constantly and far enough offshore to develop on its own terms. Its copper mines supplied the Bronze Age Mediterranean on a scale that shaped the island's whole economy and external relationships; the Latin word for the metal, *cuprum*, derives from the island's own name rather than the other way round, a linguistic trace of just how closely the two were associated in the ancient world.

Through the Archaic and Classical periods it was never one state but around ten small city-kingdoms — Salamis, Paphos, Kition, Kourion and others — with mixed Greek and Phoenician populations, nominally subject in turn to Assyria, then Egypt, then Persia, while their own kings struck their own coins and fought each other as readily as any outside power. That persistent fragmentation made the island a strategic prize repeatedly fought over rather than a unified power projecting force of its own. Cypriot scribes developed a distinctive syllabic script of their own, unrelated in form to the Linear scripts of Crete and Greece or to the alphabet, and used it to write both Greek and an unrelated, still poorly understood local Eteocypriot language side by side for centuries, a linguistic layering that has made bilingual Cypriot inscriptions valuable evidence for scholars working on both languages. Paphos, on the island's southwest coast, was one of the most important cult centres of Aphrodite anywhere in the Greek world, tradition holding that the goddess had risen from the sea foam just offshore.

After Alexander's death it was fought over repeatedly by his successors. Demetrius Poliorcetes broke Ptolemaic control of the island at the naval battle of Salamis in 306 BC, but Ptolemy I recovered it by the 290s BC, and his dynasty held it as a core possession — a naval base and a steady supply of copper and timber — for over two centuries. Rome annexed Cyprus outright in 58 BC in a controversial seizure orchestrated partly by Cato the Younger; Ptolemaic rule was briefly restored from 48 to 30 BC, largely at Julius Caesar's and then Mark Antony's initiative, before the island returned permanently to direct Roman control after Actium.
