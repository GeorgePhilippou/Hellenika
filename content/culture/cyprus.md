<!-- field: summary -->
A copper-rich island facing the Levant, divided among small Greek–Phoenician city-kingdoms and later secured as one of the Ptolemies' most valuable possessions.

<!-- field: body -->
Cyprus sits close enough to the Levantine coast to trade with it constantly and far enough offshore to develop on its own terms. Its copper mines supplied the Bronze Age Mediterranean; the Latin word for the metal, *cuprum*, derives from the island's name rather than the other way round.

Through the Archaic and Classical periods it was never one state but around ten small city-kingdoms — Salamis, Paphos, Kition, Kourion and others — with mixed Greek and Phoenician populations, nominally subject to Assyria, then Egypt, then Persia, while their own kings struck coins and fought each other. That fragmentation made the island a prize rather than a power in its own right.

After Alexander's death it was fought over repeatedly. Demetrius Poliorcetes broke Ptolemaic control of it at Salamis in 306 BC, but Ptolemy I recovered the island by the 290s BC, and his dynasty held it as a core possession — naval base, copper and timber supply — for over two centuries. Rome annexed Cyprus in 58 BC; Ptolemaic rule was restored from 48 to 30 BC before the island returned permanently to Roman control.
