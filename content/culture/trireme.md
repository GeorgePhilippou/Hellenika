<!-- field: summary -->
The fast, oared warship with three banks of rowers that dominated Greek naval warfare from the late 6th century BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The ship that won Salamis, built Athens' empire and gave its poorest citizens, who rowed it, a decisive voice in the democracy.

<!-- field: body -->
The trireme (*triērēs*) was a long, narrow warship about 37 m long and 5 m wide, rowed by around 170 men sitting in three levels on each side, with a small crew of officers, sailors, archers and marines — about 200 men in all. It carried sails for cruising but was rowed in battle. At its bow was a bronze ram at the waterline, the ship's main weapon: triremes fought by driving into the side or oars of an enemy ship. Skilled crews could also sweep past an enemy to shear off its oars.

The type spread through the Greek and Phoenician world in the later 6th century BC. In 483 BC Themistocles persuaded Athens to spend a windfall of silver on some 200 triremes, and in 480 BC that fleet won the battle of Salamis in the narrow straits off Attica. Afterwards the fleet became the instrument of Athenian power, policing the Delian League and keeping grain flowing from the Black Sea.

Rowing a trireme took training but not wealth, so the rowers of Athens were mostly its poorer citizens, the *thetes*, along with resident foreigners and some slaves. Ancient writers linked their importance in war to their power in the democratic Assembly.

A full-size reconstruction, the *Olympias*, built by the Hellenic Navy and launched in 1987, showed that such a ship could reach about 9 knots under oar. Its original was lost when Athens' last fleet was destroyed at Aegospotami in 405 BC.
