<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek cities of southern Italy and, in a wider sense, Sicily — the rich western frontier of the Greek world.

<!-- field: significance -->
Wealthy cities that rivalled mainland Greece, produced philosophers and scientists, and passed Greek culture to the Etruscans and Romans.

<!-- field: body -->
"Magna Graecia" (Great Greece) was the Roman name for the Greek cities of southern Italy, and is often extended to include Sicily. Greek settlers came from the mid-8th century BC: Euboeans to Pithekoussai and Cumae near Naples, Achaeans to Sybaris and Croton, Spartans to Taras (Taranto), Corinthians to Syracuse, and many others. The land was fertile and the cities grew rich; Sybaris became a byword for luxury.

The western Greeks made major contributions to Greek culture. Pythagoras founded his community at Croton; Parmenides and Zeno taught at Elea; Empedocles came from Akragas in Sicily; and Archimedes lived at Syracuse. The Doric temples of Paestum, Akragas and Selinous are among the best preserved in the Greek world. Syracuse, under its tyrants, became one of the great powers of the Mediterranean and defeated the Athenian invasion of 415–413 BC.

The western Greeks fought constantly with each other, with the Italian peoples around them, and in Sicily with Carthage. Through trade and contact, they passed the alphabet, gods, art and ideas to the Etruscans and to Rome.

In the 3rd century BC Rome absorbed the cities of southern Italy, after defeating Pyrrhus of Epirus, who had come to help Taras, and took Sicily from Carthage. Syracuse fell in 212 BC. Greek remained widely spoken in the region for centuries.
