<!-- field: summary -->
The largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from the Aegean to the Indus, and the defining external power in Greek history.

<!-- field: body -->
Founded by Cyrus the Great around 550 BC, the Achaemenid Empire eventually governed perhaps 25 million people — a substantial fraction of humanity alive at the time — across the Balkans, Egypt, Anatolia, Mesopotamia, Iran and Central Asia, making it by a wide margin the largest state the world had yet seen. Cyrus set the tone for how it would rule from the start: the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay proclamation issued after his conquest of Babylon, records him permitting displaced peoples, including Judean exiles held in Babylon, to return to their homelands and rebuild their own temples — a policy of restoring rather than erasing conquered peoples' institutions that some modern commentators have controversially compared to an early human-rights charter, and that in any case gave the empire's early conquests real practical legitimacy in the eyes of those it absorbed.

Its administrative achievement was genuine and durable. Darius I divided the empire into satrapies, each with a fixed tribute obligation and a Persian governor typically balanced by independent royal inspectors the Greeks called "the King's Eyes and Ears," standardised a gold coin called the daric, and built the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, its relay stations allowing couriers on the royal messenger system to cover roughly 2,700 kilometres in about a week — a speed of long-distance communication nothing else in the ancient Mediterranean world matched. Local languages, religions, legal codes and even local rulers were generally left in place under this system rather than replaced with Persian equivalents; the empire governed through administered diversity rather than enforced uniformity, an approach that let it hold together dozens of previously independent kingdoms and cultures for two centuries. Its military reflected the same scale: the elite infantry corps the Greeks called the "Immortals," after the Persian custom of instantly replacing any casualty so the unit's paper strength of 10,000 never visibly changed, became one of the most recognisable images of Persian power in Greek accounts of the wars between the two.

Those Greek accounts are, however, almost the entirety of what popular memory of Persia is actually built on, and they are systematically hostile, written by people with an obvious interest in explaining and dignifying a Greek military victory against overwhelming numerical odds. Persian administrative sources tell a considerably less lurid story: the Persepolis Fortification tablets, an enormous cache of everyday bureaucratic records rather than royal propaganda, show ordinary wages paid in grain, wine and other rations to workers of both sexes on the palace's own payroll, including specific extra rations recorded for women who had recently given birth — the routine texture of an administered state rather than the despotic caricature that dominates Herodotus and later Greek tradition.

Alexander the Great destroyed the empire's political independence in a single sustained campaign between 334 and 330 BC, defeating Darius III in three major battles and burning the ceremonial capital of Persepolis, but he then governed much of the conquered territory using the Achaemenid empire's own administrative machinery largely intact, retaining Persian satraps and Persian methods of taxation and communication rather than replacing them wholesale — an implicit acknowledgment that the system Cyrus and Darius had built worked well enough to be worth keeping, even for the man who had just destroyed the dynasty that built it.
