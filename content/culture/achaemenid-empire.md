<!-- field: summary -->
The largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from the Aegean to the Indus, and the defining external power in Greek history.

<!-- field: body -->
Founded by Cyrus the Great around 550 BC, the Achaemenid Empire eventually governed perhaps 25 million people — a substantial fraction of humanity at the time — across the Balkans, Egypt, Anatolia, Mesopotamia, Iran and Central Asia.

Its administrative achievement was real. Darius I divided it into satrapies with fixed tribute, standardised the gold daric, and built the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa with relay stations allowing couriers to cross 2,700 km in about a week. Local languages, religions and legal systems were generally left alone; the empire governed through diversity rather than against it.

Greeks defined themselves against it, and the surviving accounts are overwhelmingly theirs — hostile, and interested in explaining a Greek victory. Persian sources present a different and less lurid picture: the Persepolis tablets record wages paid in grain and wine to workers including women, with extra rations for new mothers.

Alexander destroyed it between 334 and 330 BC and then governed much of it using its own machinery.
