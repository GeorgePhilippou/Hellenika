<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek drinking party: an evening of wine, poetry, music, games and conversation among male citizens reclining on couches.

<!-- field: significance -->
The setting for much of Greek poetry, philosophy and vase painting — and a key institution of aristocratic and citizen social life.

<!-- field: body -->
A symposium (*symposion*, "drinking together") was an evening gathering of men in the *andrōn*, the men's dining room of a private house, usually with seven to fifteen couches arranged around the walls. After dinner the guests reclined, garlanded, while a host or elected "symposiarch" decided how strong the wine should be. Greek wine was always mixed with water in a large bowl, the *kratēr*; drinking it neat was thought the habit of barbarians.

The evening followed rituals: libations to the gods, songs, toasts, riddles and games such as *kottabos*, flicking wine dregs at a target. Guests took turns singing or reciting, and much Archaic lyric poetry — by Alcaeus, Anacreon, Theognis and others — was composed for these occasions. Hired musicians, dancers and *hetairai* (educated female companions) might entertain; respectable citizen women did not attend.

The symposium was also a place of friendship, politics and love between men, where political factions formed and young men learned the values of their class. Plato's *Symposium* and Xenophon's dialogue of the same name portray philosophical conversation at such parties.

The symposium shaped Greek art. Many of the finest Athenian painted vessels — mixing bowls, wine jugs and drinking cups — were made for it, and they often show symposium scenes, as does the painted Tomb of the Diver at Paestum. Exported to Etruria, such vessels spread the custom of the banquet across the Mediterranean.
