<!-- field: summary -->
The kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world that, under Philip II and Alexander, conquered Greece and the Persian Empire.

<!-- field: significance -->
The power that ended the age of independent city-states and carried Greek culture from the Balkans to India.

<!-- field: body -->
Macedon lay in the plains and mountains around the Thermaic Gulf, north of Thessaly and Mount Olympus. Its lowlands were rich in grain and horses, its uplands in timber and, later, gold and silver. Unlike the southern city-states, it remained a kingdom, ruled from about 700 BC by the Argead dynasty, which claimed descent from Heracles and from Argos. The capital was Aigai (Vergina), later moved to Pella around 400 BC; Aigai remained the royal burial place.

Southern Greeks often looked down on the Macedonians as semi-barbarian, though their kings took part in Greek culture: Alexander I competed at Olympia, and Archelaus invited Euripides and other artists to his court. Whether the Macedonian language was a dialect of Greek is debated; recent inscriptions favour a Greek dialect. Macedonian society was dominated by a warrior aristocracy, the king's Companions.

For long a weak kingdom troubled by neighbours, Macedon was transformed by Philip II (359–336 BC), who built a professional army armed with the long sarissa and mastered Greece at Chaeronea in 338 BC. His son Alexander the Great conquered the Persian Empire. After Alexander's death, Macedon itself was fought over until the Antigonid dynasty secured it in the 270s BC.

Rome defeated Macedon in a series of wars and, after Pydna in 168 BC, abolished the monarchy. It became a Roman province in 148 BC.
