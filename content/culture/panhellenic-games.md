<!-- field: summary -->
The four great athletic and religious festivals open to all Greeks — the Olympic, Pythian, Isthmian and Nemean Games.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Games brought rival cities together under a shared truce, gave Greece a common calendar and created the ideal of athletic excellence that the modern Olympics revived.

<!-- field: body -->
Four festivals formed the "circuit" (*periodos*) of games open to Greeks from every city. The Olympic Games, for Zeus at Olympia, were held every four years from, by tradition, 776 BC. The Pythian Games, for Apollo at Delphi, were held every four years from 586 BC and included music and poetry contests. The Isthmian Games, for Poseidon near Corinth, and the Nemean Games, for Zeus at Nemea, were held every two years. Their prizes were simple wreaths — olive, laurel, pine and celery.

The festivals were religious occasions, with sacrifices and processions as well as competition. Events included foot races, the pentathlon, wrestling, boxing and the brutal *pankration*, and chariot and horse races. Competitors were free Greek men, who competed naked. Women were barred from competing, though they could win the chariot races as owners of the horses, as the Spartan princess Cynisca did.

Although the prize was only a wreath, victory brought fame and reward at home: statues, free meals for life, and victory odes by poets such as Pindar. A sacred truce let athletes and spectators travel safely to the games, even through cities at war. Counting years by Olympiads gave the Greeks a shared way of dating events.

The Olympic Games continued under Roman rule until they were ended around AD 393. They were revived as the modern Olympic Games in Athens in 1896.
