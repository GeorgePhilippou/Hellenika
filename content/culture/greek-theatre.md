<!-- field: summary -->
The tragedy and comedy created in Athens for the festivals of Dionysus, performed by masked actors and a chorus in open-air theatres.

<!-- field: significance -->
The beginning of Western drama: the plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes are still performed, and their forms underlie theatre to this day.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek drama grew out of the worship of Dionysus at Athens. At the City Dionysia each spring, and at the smaller Lenaia, playwrights competed before audiences of thousands seated on the slope below the Acropolis. By tradition, Thespis staged the first tragedy around 534 BC; comedy was added to the festival in 486 BC.

A tragedy combined spoken scenes between actors with songs and dances by a chorus of twelve or fifteen. All performers were male citizens wearing masks, and two or three actors played all the speaking parts. Each tragic poet presented three tragedies and a comic satyr play. Wealthy citizens paid for the choruses as a public duty. Tragedies drew on myth — the families of Agamemnon and Oedipus, the Trojan War — to explore justice, fate, the gods and the conflicts of the city. Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides are the only tragedians whose plays survive complete, about 32 in all.

Comedy was very different. The Old Comedy of Aristophanes mocked living politicians, philosophers and poets by name, with fantastic plots, obscene jokes and sharp political satire. By the late 4th century it had given way to the New Comedy of Menander, gentle plays of everyday family life and romance that shaped Roman comedy and, through it, later European drama.

Stone theatres, such as those at Athens and Epidaurus, spread across the Greek world. Aristotle's *Poetics* remains the first great analysis of drama.
