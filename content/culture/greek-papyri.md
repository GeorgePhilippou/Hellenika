<!-- field: summary -->
The tens of thousands of Greek papyri preserved in the dry sands of Egypt, from the 4th century BC to the 8th century AD.

<!-- field: significance -->
They have restored lost works of Greek literature and give the fullest picture of everyday life anywhere in the ancient world.

<!-- field: body -->
Papyrus, made from a Nile reed, was the paper of the ancient world. It rots in damp conditions, so almost all ancient books outside Egypt have perished; what survived was copied and recopied in the Middle Ages. In Egypt, however, papyrus survived in the dry desert margins: in the rubbish mounds of towns, in abandoned villages of the Fayum, and as cartonnage, the papier-mâché used to wrap mummies.

From the Ptolemaic conquest until after the Arab conquest of AD 641, Greek was the language of government and of much private life in Egypt, so most papyri are Greek. Since the 1770s, and especially since the 1890s, tens of thousands have been found. The greatest site is Oxyrhynchus, where Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt dug from 1896 to 1907; its papyri are still being published more than a century later.

Most papyri are documents: tax receipts, contracts, petitions, school exercises, private letters. But a tenth or so are literary, and they have given back Aristotle's *Constitution of the Athenians*, the odes of Bacchylides, whole plays of Menander, new poems of Sappho and Archilochus, and early texts of the New Testament. The study of them, papyrology, has become a discipline of its own.
