<!-- field: summary -->
One-man rule seized outside the traditional constitution, common across the Greek cities in the 7th and 6th centuries BC and later in Sicily.

<!-- field: significance -->
A stage through which many Greek cities passed on the way from aristocratic rule to broader citizen government — and the origin of the word "tyrant".

<!-- field: body -->
A *tyrannos* was a ruler who took sole power outside the city's traditional constitution, usually by force or popular support. The word was not originally an insult: many tyrants governed effectively and were remembered with some gratitude. Only later, especially in democratic Athens, did "tyrant" come to mean a cruel and lawless despot.

Tyranny spread through the Greek cities from the mid-7th century BC, when growing wealth, colonisation and the rise of hoplite armies strained the power of the old aristocracies. Among the first was Cypselus of Corinth, around 657 BC; others included Pheidon of Argos, Polycrates of Samos, Cleisthenes of Sicyon and Peisistratus of Athens. Many tyrants weakened rival noble families, sponsored temples, harbours, aqueducts and festivals, and attracted poets and artists to their courts.

Few tyrannies outlasted a second generation. Their sons were often harsher and less able, and were overthrown — as Hippias was at Athens in 510 BC, with Spartan help. The fall of tyranny opened the way, in some cities, to democracy.

In Sicily, where war with Carthage favoured strong leaders, tyranny lasted much longer: Gelon and Hieron at Syracuse in the 5th century BC, Dionysius I in the 4th, and Agathocles at its end. Greek political thinkers from Herodotus to Plato and Aristotle treated tyranny as the worst of constitutions.
