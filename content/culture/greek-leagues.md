<!-- field: summary -->
The alliances and federations through which Greek city-states combined for war and, later, shared government.

<!-- field: significance -->
Leagues shaped the great wars of Greece and, in the Hellenistic federal states, produced one of antiquity's most advanced experiments in shared government.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek city-states prized their independence but constantly joined together for security. Most leagues were military alliances (*symmachiai*) led by a strong city. The Peloponnesian League, formed under Spartan leadership in the 6th century BC, bound most Peloponnesian cities to follow Sparta in war. The Delian League, founded in 478 BC to fight Persia, was led by Athens and became an Athenian empire. Their rivalry produced the Peloponnesian War. In the 4th century BC Athens founded a second, milder league, and Philip II united the Greek states in the League of Corinth in 337 BC under his leadership.

Other leagues were regional federations of neighbouring communities sharing an identity. The Boeotian League, dominated by Thebes, had a federal council and army. The Thessalian and Arcadian Leagues were similar.

In the Hellenistic period, two federal states became major powers: the Aetolian League in central Greece and the Achaean League in the Peloponnese. They had a shared citizenship, federal assemblies and elected generals, while member cities kept local self-government. Under leaders such as Aratus and Philopoemen, the Achaean League united much of the Peloponnese, and the historian Polybius, himself an Achaean statesman, admired it. The framers of the United States Constitution studied these federations.

Rome first allied with and then broke up the leagues. After the Achaean revolt of 146 BC, Rome destroyed Corinth and dissolved the League.
