<!-- field: summary -->
The system by which the citizens of Athens governed themselves directly, through an open Assembly, a Council chosen by lot and mass citizen juries, from 508 to 322 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first well-documented democracy in history, and the model — admired and criticised — behind every later idea of government by the people.

<!-- field: body -->
Athenian democracy (*dēmokratia*, "power of the people") was created by Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7 BC, building on Solon's earlier changes, and deepened in the 460s and 450s BC by Ephialtes and Pericles. It lasted, with two brief oligarchic interruptions in 411 and 404 BC, until Macedon abolished it in 322 BC.

It was direct democracy. The Assembly (*ekklēsia*), open to every adult male citizen, met about forty times a year on the Pnyx hill and voted on war, peace, laws and finance; any citizen could speak. A Council of 500, chosen each year by lot from the ten tribes, prepared its business. Most officials were also chosen by lot for one year, on the principle that ordinary citizens could govern; only military generals and some financial officers were elected. Mass juries of hundreds of citizens, also chosen by lot, decided cases in the law courts, and ostracism could exile a dangerously powerful man. From the 450s citizens were paid for jury service and later for attending the Assembly, so the poor could take part.

Its limits were sharp. Only free adult men of citizen birth — perhaps 30,000 in the 4th century BC — had political rights. Women, resident foreigners and a large enslaved population were excluded, and the democracy ruled an empire of subject allies.

Ancient critics such as Plato thought it rule by the ignorant mob; its defenders, like Pericles in Thucydides, saw it as the source of Athens' greatness.
