<!-- field: summary -->
The fertile region of central Greece north-west of Attica, dominated by Thebes and the scene of many of Greece's decisive battles.

<!-- field: significance -->
A rich farming land and the home of Hesiod and Pindar, whose plains were the battlefield of Greece from Plataea to Chaeronea.

<!-- field: body -->
Boeotia is the region of central Greece lying between Attica to the south-east and Phocis to the west, with fertile plains around Thebes and the great lake of Copais (drained only in modern times). In the Bronze Age it was home to the powerful Mycenaean centres of Thebes, Orchomenos and the vast fortress of Gla, which helped drain the Copais basin.

In the historical period its cities were joined in the Boeotian League, a federation usually dominated by Thebes, with a federal council and army. Other Boeotian cities, such as Plataea and Orchomenos, often resented Theban control; Plataea sided with Athens and fought beside it at Marathon.

Athenians mocked Boeotians as dull farmers, but the region produced the poets Hesiod, whose *Works and Days* describes life at Ascra, and Pindar of Thebes, as well as the poet Corinna. Its open plains made it the battlefield of Greece, which Epaminondas reportedly called "the dancing floor of Ares": Plataea (479 BC), Delium (424 BC), Leuctra (371 BC) and Chaeronea (338 BC) were all fought there.

Under Epaminondas and Pelopidas, Boeotia under Thebes became the leading power in Greece after Leuctra. Its independence ended with Philip II's victory at Chaeronea and Alexander's destruction of Thebes in 335 BC.
