<!-- field: summary -->
The heavily armed citizen infantry of the Greek city-states, fighting shoulder to shoulder in the close formation called the phalanx.

<!-- field: significance -->
The way of fighting that defined Greek warfare for three centuries — and tied military duty, and political rights, to the citizen who could afford his own armour.

<!-- field: body -->
A hoplite was a heavily armed foot soldier, named after his equipment (*hopla*) and above all his large round shield, the *aspis*, about a metre across, held on the left arm. He also wore a bronze helmet, a breastplate of bronze or linen, and greaves, and fought with a thrusting spear about 2.5 m long and a short sword. The full set was expensive, and hoplites usually bought their own, so they came from the middling and wealthier farmers of each city.

Hoplites fought in the phalanx, a formation usually eight ranks deep in which each man's shield partly covered the man to his left. Battles between phalanxes were short and brutal: the lines met, pushed and stabbed until one side broke and ran. Images such as the Chigi Vase of around 650 BC show the formation already in use, though whether it appeared suddenly in a "hoplite reform" or developed gradually is debated.

Because the city's defence rested on its hoplites, many historians link their rise to the decline of aristocratic rule and the spread of broader citizen government. Sparta made hoplite fighting a professional way of life and was long thought invincible, until Epaminondas broke its phalanx at Leuctra in 371 BC.

Hoplites beat Persian infantry at Marathon and Plataea, but from the 4th century BC they were overtaken by light troops, mercenaries and finally the Macedonian pike phalanx armed with the sarissa.
