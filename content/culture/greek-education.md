<!-- field: summary -->
How Greek boys — and occasionally girls — were brought up and taught, from the reading teacher and the wrestling school to the gymnasium and the philosophical schools.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Greek ideal of education (paideia) — a balance of mind and body, letters and music — became the foundation of education in the Roman world and, through it, Europe.

<!-- field: body -->
In most Greek cities education was a private matter for families who could afford it. In Classical Athens a boy from a well-off family went from about seven to a *grammatistēs*, who taught reading, writing and arithmetic and made him learn Homer by heart; to a *kitharistēs*, who taught the lyre and lyric poetry; and to a *paidotribēs*, who trained him in wrestling, running and other sports at a wrestling ground (*palaistra*). A slave called a *paidagōgos* escorted him. Girls were usually taught at home, mainly household skills.

Sparta was the great exception: from about seven, citizen boys entered the *agoge*, a harsh state training in obedience, endurance and fighting. Spartan girls also exercised in public.

For older youths and men the centre of education was the *gymnasion*, a public exercise ground with tracks, baths and colonnades, which became a place for conversation and teaching. Socrates questioned young men there, and the Sophists offered paid courses in rhetoric. Plato's Academy and Aristotle's Lyceum were founded at Athenian gymnasia, and grew into the first lasting schools of higher learning. From the late 4th century, Athens trained its young men as *ephebes* for two years of military service.

In the Hellenistic world the gymnasium became the symbol of Greek life. New cities from Egypt to Afghanistan — Ai-Khanoum among them — built gymnasia, where Greek-speaking youths were trained in Greek letters and athletics.
