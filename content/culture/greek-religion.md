<!-- field: summary -->
The beliefs and rituals through which Greeks honoured their many gods — a religion of practice, festivals and sacrifice rather than doctrine.

<!-- field: significance -->
Religion ran through every part of Greek life, from household and city to theatre, athletics and war; its gods and myths remain the best-known legacy of ancient Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek religion had no founder, no holy book, no creed and no church. It was a matter of practice: honouring the gods in the right way so that they would favour the community. Its roots reach back to the Bronze Age — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon and Dionysus are already named on Linear B tablets — and its stories were given lasting shape by Homer and Hesiod.

The Greeks worshipped many gods. The twelve Olympians — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes and Hestia or Dionysus — were joined by countless local gods, nymphs and heroes. Each city had its own patron deities and calendar of festivals, such as the Panathenaia at Athens.

The central ritual was animal sacrifice at an outdoor altar, followed by a shared meal of the meat, which bound the community to its gods and to each other. People also offered prayers, libations, first fruits and dedications, from cheap figurines to marble statues and whole treasuries. Temples were houses for the gods' images, not places of congregation. Priests and priestesses served particular cults, often as a civic office.

Greeks sought the gods' guidance through oracles, above all at Delphi and Dodona, and through omens. Panhellenic sanctuaries such as Olympia and Delphi brought the scattered Greek cities together. For many, mystery cults such as those of Eleusis offered a more personal hope for the afterlife.

Greek religion slowly gave way to Christianity and was banned under Theodosius I in the AD 390s.
