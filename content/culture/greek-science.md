<!-- field: summary -->
The mathematics, astronomy, medicine and natural science of the Greeks, which reached its height in Hellenistic Alexandria.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Greeks created the idea of science as explaining nature by reasoned argument and proof; their geometry, astronomy and medicine were the foundation of science until the 17th century.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek science grew out of the same Ionian curiosity as philosophy. In the 6th century BC Thales and Anaximander sought natural explanations of the world, and Pythagoras and his followers found mathematical order in music and the heavens.

Medicine became rational in the 5th century BC with Hippocrates of Kos and the writings collected under his name, which explained illness by natural causes such as diet, climate and the balance of bodily fluids, and stressed careful observation of patients. Aristotle, in the 4th century BC, founded systematic biology through his study of animals.

The great age of Greek science was the Hellenistic period, centred on the Museum and Library of Alexandria. Euclid organised geometry into a deductive system; Archimedes calculated areas and volumes, founded hydrostatics and built war machines; Apollonius wrote on conic sections. Aristarchus proposed that the Earth orbits the Sun; Eratosthenes measured the Earth; Hipparchus catalogued the stars and discovered the precession of the equinoxes. At Alexandria Herophilus and Erasistratus dissected human bodies, discovering the nerves and studying the brain and heart. Engineers such as Ctesibius built water clocks and pumps, and the Antikythera Mechanism shows how sophisticated Greek instruments could be.

Greek science was largely theoretical, with little systematic experiment. But through Ptolemy and Galen in the Roman period, and Arabic and Latin translators afterwards, it shaped the science of the Islamic world and Europe until the Scientific Revolution.
