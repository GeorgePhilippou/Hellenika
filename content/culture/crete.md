<!-- field: summary -->
The largest Greek island, home of Europe's first palace civilisation and a crossroads between the Aegean, Egypt and the Levant.

<!-- field: body -->
Crete is 260 km long, mountainous, and positioned where the sea routes from Egypt, the Levant and the Aegean converge. That position explains most of its history.

It was settled by around 7000 BC by farmers who brought livestock and crops by boat — one of the earliest maritime colonisations known. The palaces emerged around 1900 BC and dominated the Aegean until the destructions of 1450 BC.

After the Bronze Age it became comparatively marginal: a land of many small, frequently feuding poleis, known for skilled archers, mercenaries hired out across the wider Greek world, and, in the standard Greek stereotype, a reputation for lying. The "Cretan paradox" — a Cretan saying all Cretans are liars — enters logic as a foundational puzzle about self-reference and eventually turns up quoted in the New Testament's Epistle to Titus. The island's rugged, mountainous interior sustained isolated highland communities with their own dialects and customs well after Bronze Age palatial society had collapsed elsewhere on the island, a fragmentation that persisted through the Archaic and Classical periods even as the rest of the Aegean world consolidated into more familiar, better-known city-states.

Rome took the island in 69–67 BC after a difficult campaign against determined local resistance.
