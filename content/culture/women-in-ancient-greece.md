<!-- field: summary -->
The lives, roles and restrictions of women in the Greek world, which varied greatly between cities and periods.

<!-- field: significance -->
Half of the population, largely missing from political history, yet central to religion, the household and much of Greek literature.

<!-- field: body -->
Most of what survives about Greek women was written by men, and most of it concerns Athens, where their position was especially restricted. An Athenian citizen woman could not vote, hold office or own significant property in her own name, and was legally under the guardianship (*kyrieia*) of her father, husband or nearest male relative. Girls married young, often at fourteen or fifteen, to husbands chosen for them, and respectable women were expected to manage the household (*oikos*) and stay largely out of public view. Poorer women, however, worked in markets, fields and crafts.

Religion was the great exception. Women served as priestesses — the priestess of Athena Polias at Athens and the Pythia at Delphi were among the most important religious figures in Greece — and took part in festivals, some of them, such as the Thesmophoria, for women only.

Elsewhere things differed. Spartan women owned and inherited land, exercised in public and were known for speaking their minds. Some women became famous in their own right: the poet Sappho on Lesbos, Aspasia of Miletus in Athens, the philosopher Hipparchia, and Cynisca of Sparta, the first woman to win at Olympia.

In the Hellenistic kingdoms royal women such as Olympias, Arsinoe II and Cleopatra VII wielded real political power. Greek drama, from Antigone to Medea, repeatedly put women's voices and conflicts at its centre.
