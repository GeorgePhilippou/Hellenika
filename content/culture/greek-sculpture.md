<!-- field: summary -->
The sculpture of the Greek world, from stiff Archaic kouroi to the idealised figures of the Classical age and the drama of Hellenistic art.

<!-- field: significance -->
Greek sculptors invented the naturalistic, idealised human figure that dominated Western art from Rome to the Renaissance and beyond.

<!-- field: body -->
Large-scale stone sculpture appeared in Greece in the 7th century BC, influenced by Egypt. The Archaic period produced the *kouros*, a standing nude youth, and the *korē*, a clothed young woman, used as grave markers and offerings to the gods. They stand stiffly, left foot forward, with the faint "Archaic smile".

Around 480 BC sculptors broke with this formula. Works such as the Kritios Boy shift their weight onto one leg (contrapposto), and the body responds naturally. The bronze-casting of the Severe Style, seen in the Charioteer of Delphi and the Riace Bronzes, led to the High Classical art of Pheidias and the Parthenon sculptures, and Polykleitos' *Doryphoros*, built on ideal proportions. In the 4th century BC Praxiteles, Scopas and Lysippos made figures softer, more emotional and more varied; Praxiteles' Aphrodite of Knidos was the first monumental female nude.

Hellenistic sculpture after Alexander explored drama, suffering, old age and portraiture, in works such as the Pergamon Altar, the Dying Gaul, the Winged Victory of Samothrace and the Laocoön.

Two facts change how Greek sculpture should be imagined. It was painted, often brightly, and the white marble look comes from paint loss. And most famous bronzes were melted down; they survive mostly through Roman marble copies. Originals such as the Riace and Artemision bronzes show how much was lost.
