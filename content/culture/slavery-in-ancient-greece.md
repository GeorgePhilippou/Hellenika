<!-- field: summary -->
The ownership and forced labour of human beings, built into the economy and society of nearly every Greek city.

<!-- field: significance -->
The hidden foundation of Greek civilisation: the leisure of citizens, the silver of Athens and the farms of Sparta all rested on unfree labour.

<!-- field: body -->
Slavery existed throughout Greek history, from the palace workers recorded on Linear B tablets to the households of the Hellenistic kingdoms. In most cities it took the form of chattel slavery: slaves were property who could be bought, sold, punished and freed by their owners. They came mainly from war captives, piracy and the slave trade with peoples beyond the Greek world, such as Thracians and Scythians; Greeks enslaved other Greeks after wars, as Athens did at Melos.

In Classical Athens slaves worked in households, workshops and farms, as public servants and police, and in brutal conditions in the silver mines of Laurion. Estimates of their numbers vary, perhaps 60,000 to 80,000 in Attica at its height. Some skilled slaves lived and worked independently and could save to buy their freedom.

Sparta was different. Its helots, mainly the conquered people of Messenia, were not owned by individuals but bound to the land as a subject population, farming it for Spartan citizens. They far outnumbered their masters, and Sparta held them down by violence; their liberation in 369 BC ended Spartan power.

Few Greeks questioned slavery itself. Aristotle argued that some people were slaves by nature; a few Sophists and later Stoics suggested that slavery was a matter of law, not nature. Modern historians stress that Greek freedom and democracy for citizens existed alongside, and depended on, the unfreedom of others.
