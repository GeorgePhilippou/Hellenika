<!-- field: summary -->
The first writing system to represent both consonants and vowels — adapted from Phoenician by repurposing unneeded signs.

<!-- field: body -->
Phoenician script recorded consonants only. That works for Semitic languages, where roots are consonantal and vowels are predictable, but it fails for Greek, where vowels carry essential distinctions.

The Greek solution was to take Phoenician letters for sounds Greek did not have — the glottal stop *aleph*, the pharyngeal *ayin*, *he*, *yod* — and reassign them as alpha, omicron, epsilon and iota. The letter names came along untranslated: *aleph* means "ox" and *beth* means "house" in Phoenician, and mean nothing at all in Greek. That is itself proof of the borrowing.

The result could represent any Greek word unambiguously and could be learned in weeks rather than years. Local alphabets varied at first — Athens used a different set from Corinth or Euboea — until Athens adopted the Ionic standard in 403 BC.

The Euboean variant travelled to Italy and became Etruscan, then Latin, and so the alphabet used for most of the world's writing today.
