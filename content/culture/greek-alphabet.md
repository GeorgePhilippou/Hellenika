<!-- field: summary -->
The first writing system to represent both consonants and vowels — adapted from Phoenician by repurposing unneeded signs.

<!-- field: body -->
Phoenician script recorded consonants only. That works for Semitic languages, where roots are consonantal and vowels are largely predictable from context, but it fails badly for Greek, where vowel quality carries essential grammatical and lexical distinctions a reader cannot simply infer.

The Greek solution was to take Phoenician letters for sounds Greek did not have — the glottal stop *aleph*, the pharyngeal *ayin*, *he*, *yod* — and reassign them as alpha, omicron, epsilon and iota. The letter names came along untranslated: *aleph* means "ox" and *beth* means "house" in Phoenician, and mean nothing at all in Greek. That mismatch is itself strong proof of the borrowing, since no one inventing a script from scratch names its own letters with foreign words for unrelated objects.

Among the earliest surviving examples of the new script are the Dipylon oinochoe, a small jug from an Athenian grave dated to around 740 BC and scratched with a line of hexameter verse about a dancing contest, and the roughly contemporary "Nestor's Cup" from Pithekoussai off the Italian coast, whose inscription jokingly invokes the far grander drinking cup of the Homeric hero Nestor. Early Greek writing had no fixed direction: inscriptions could run right to left in the Phoenician manner, left to right, or boustrophedon — literally "as the ox turns" while ploughing — alternating direction with every line, sometimes even mirroring each letter's orientation to match. Local city alphabets also varied for generations in which extra signs they kept or dropped, including now-obsolete letters like digamma, qoppa and san, until Athens formally adopted the Ionic 24-letter standard in 403 BC, a decision that gradually became the model followed everywhere else.

The result could represent any Greek word unambiguously and, unlike Linear B or Egyptian hieroglyphs before it, could be learned by a child in weeks rather than by a scribe over years of specialised training — a difference in accessibility that scholars increasingly credit with helping to seed the unusually broad, non-priestly literacy of later Greek society. The western, Euboean variant of the alphabet travelled to Italy through colonies like Cumae and became Etruscan, then Latin, and so, by a long chain of transmission from a Semitic script devised for entirely different languages, the alphabet used for most of the world's writing today.
