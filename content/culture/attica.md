<!-- field: summary -->
The triangular peninsula of central Greece that formed the territory of Athens.

<!-- field: significance -->
The homeland of Athenian democracy, whose silver mines, harbours and farms made Athens the leading power of Classical Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Attica is the triangular peninsula of central Greece pointing south-east into the Aegean, about 2,500 square kilometres, bounded by the mountains of Kithairon and Parnes on the north and by the sea on its other sides. Unusually for Greece, the whole region formed a single city-state: Athens. Athenians credited the hero Theseus with uniting its villages into one state.

Its landscape was mountainous, with small plains around Athens, Eleusis and Marathon, thin soil and dry summers better suited to olives, vines and figs than to grain. Its mountains supplied the fine marble of Pentelikon and Hymettos, used for the Parthenon, and the clay of its potters. In the south, the silver mines of Laurion financed Themistocles' fleet in 483 BC and the coinage of Athens, worked by thousands of slaves.

Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7 BC divided Attica into about 140 local communities, the demes, grouped into city, coast and inland zones, which became the basis of citizenship. Many Athenians lived in these rural demes rather than in the city itself.

Attica's famous places include the battlefield of Marathon, the sanctuary of Eleusis, the temple of Poseidon at Sounion and the harbour of Piraeus. In the Peloponnesian War Spartan armies repeatedly ravaged its countryside, forcing its people behind the walls of Athens.
