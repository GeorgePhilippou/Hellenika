<!-- field: summary -->
The highland interior of the Peloponnese, home of Pan and of poets' idealised pastoral life, and the base of the Arcadian League of 370 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
A mountain region that Greeks imagined as an unspoiled pastoral land and that became a counterweight to Sparta.

<!-- field: body -->
Arcadia occupies the centre of the Peloponnese, a landlocked plateau of mountains and high basins. Its people claimed to be older than the moon, and spoke a dialect closely related to Cypriot Greek, a relic of Mycenaean speech. Cities such as Tegea, Mantinea and Orchomenos governed small territories, and the region was never part of Dorian Sparta's lands.

Pan, a god of herds, was worshipped there, and on Mount Lykaion stood an old sanctuary of Zeus where a festival took place. Poets such as Virgil later made Arcadia the home of an idealised shepherd life, and the word came to mean an imagined rural paradise. The land was poor, and many men sought service as mercenaries.

After Leuctra in 371 BC, with Theban support, the Arcadians formed a League with a federal assembly, the Ten Thousand, and a new capital at Megalopolis. The League was short-lived, but Arcadian cities later joined the Achaean League. The region stayed relatively isolated.
