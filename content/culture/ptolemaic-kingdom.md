<!-- field: summary -->
Greek-ruled Egypt under Alexander's general Ptolemy and his descendants — the wealthiest and longest-lasting successor kingdom.

<!-- field: body -->
The Ptolemies governed Egypt for 275 years as a Greek-speaking elite over an Egyptian population, presenting themselves as pharaohs to Egyptians and as Hellenistic kings to Greeks. Temple reliefs show them in pharaonic dress making offerings to Egyptian gods; their coins show them in Greek royal diadems.

The economy was tightly controlled. Grain, oil and papyrus were state monopolies, and Egypt's agricultural surplus made the kingdom enormously rich — wealth that funded the Library, the Museum and the Pharos.

The dynasty practised sibling marriage, following Egyptian royal precedent, which produced a genealogy of extraordinary complexity and a great deal of murder within the family.

Cleopatra VII was the last. Her defeat at Actium and death in 30 BC ended the kingdom and the Hellenistic age with it.
