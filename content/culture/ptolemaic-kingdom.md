<!-- field: summary -->
Greek-ruled Egypt under Alexander's general Ptolemy and his descendants — the wealthiest and longest-lasting successor kingdom.

<!-- field: body -->
The Ptolemies governed Egypt for 275 years as a Greek-speaking elite over an Egyptian population, presenting themselves as pharaohs to Egyptians and as Hellenistic kings to Greeks. Temple reliefs show them in pharaonic dress making offerings to Egyptian gods; their coins show them in Greek royal diadems.

The economy was tightly controlled. Grain, oil and papyrus were state monopolies, and Egypt's agricultural surplus made the kingdom enormously rich — wealth that funded the Library, the Museum and the Pharos.

The dynasty practised sibling marriage, following Egyptian royal precedent, which produced a genealogy of extraordinary complexity and a great deal of murder within the family. Fourteen kings in direct succession were all named Ptolemy, distinguished today only by modern numbering and by epithets ancient writers or later historians attached to them — Ptolemy III "Euergetes," the Benefactor, against Ptolemy VIII "Physcon," the Fatty, whose own reign is remembered as one of the family's most violently self-destructive: he had his ten-year-old son by his first wife, Cleopatra II, killed and dismembered, and reportedly sent the pieces to her as a birthday gift, in the course of a decades-long civil war between rival branches of the same family for the throne.

Cleopatra VII was the dynasty's last ruler, and by most accounts one of its most capable, holding Egypt's independence together through alliances first with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony against the growing power of Rome. Her and Antony's defeat at the naval battle of Actium in 31 BC, followed by both their deaths the following year as the victorious Octavian's forces closed in on Alexandria, ended Ptolemaic Egypt after two hundred and seventy-five years and, with it, brought the entire Hellenistic age to a formal close — the last of Alexander's successor kingdoms finally absorbed into what would shortly become the Roman Empire.
