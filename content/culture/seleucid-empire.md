<!-- field: summary -->
The largest successor state, inheriting most of Alexander's Asian conquests from Anatolia to Central Asia.

<!-- field: body -->
Seleucus I took Babylonia in 312 BC — the year from which the Seleucid Era counts, making it the first continuous numbered year-count in history and the ancestor of all such systems, including AD dating.

The empire's problem was its size and diversity. It governed Greeks, Babylonians, Persians, Jews, Bactrians and dozens of other peoples, and held them together by founding Greek cities as anchors of control — Antioch, Seleucia-on-the-Tigris, Apamea, Laodicea and many more.

It lost territory steadily: the far east to the Mauryans and then to the Parthians, Bactria to a breakaway Greek kingdom, and Anatolia to Rome after Magnesia in 190 BC. The attempt to impose Greek practice in Judaea under Antiochus IV provoked the Maccabean revolt.

Pompey annexed what remained in 63 BC.
