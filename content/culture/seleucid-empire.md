<!-- field: summary -->
The largest successor state, inheriting most of Alexander's Asian conquests from Anatolia to Central Asia.

<!-- field: body -->
Seleucus I took Babylonia in 312 BC — the year from which the Seleucid Era counts, making it the first continuous numbered year-count in recorded history and, through a long chain of borrowed conventions, an ancestor of the AD dating system still used worldwide.

The empire's fundamental problem was its own enormous size and diversity. It governed Greeks, Babylonians, Persians, Jews, Bactrians and dozens of other peoples spread across a territory stretching from the Aegean to the borders of India, and held them together primarily by founding dozens of new Greek cities as anchors of military and administrative control — Antioch, Seleucia-on-the-Tigris, Apamea, Laodicea and many more, each populated with Greek and Macedonian settlers loyal to the crown in regions that otherwise had little natural attachment to it.

It lost territory steadily rather than all at once: the far eastern provinces first to the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta, then further territory to the nomadic Parthians, who eventually took Mesopotamia itself; Bactria broke away entirely as an independent Greek kingdom; and Anatolia was lost outright to Rome after the decisive defeat at Magnesia in 190 BC, a battle that forced the Seleucids to abandon essentially all their territory west of the Taurus mountains under the harsh terms of the subsequent Treaty of Apamea. Domestically, Antiochus IV's aggressive attempt to impose Greek religious practice on the Temple in Jerusalem, including reportedly erecting an altar to Zeus within it, provoked the Maccabean revolt, a Jewish uprising that eventually won effective independence for Judaea.

Pompey annexed what little remained of the once-vast empire as a Roman province in 63 BC, ending it after nearly two and a half centuries of slow, uneven contraction from its original scale.
