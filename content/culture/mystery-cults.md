<!-- field: summary -->
Secret religious rites, open to those who chose to be initiated, that promised a closer bond with the gods and a better life after death.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most personal side of Greek religion, offering individual hope beyond death; the Eleusinian Mysteries drew initiates for a thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Alongside the public worship of the city's gods, many Greeks sought initiation into "mysteries" (*mystēria*), rites revealed only to those who had been initiated (*mystai*). Initiates swore to keep the rites secret, and they largely did, so much about them remains unknown.

The most famous were the Eleusinian Mysteries of Demeter and her daughter Persephone, celebrated at Eleusis near Athens from at least the 7th century BC. Their founding story, told in the Homeric *Hymn to Demeter*, is Persephone's seizure by Hades and her return each year. Each autumn initiates walked in procession from Athens to Eleusis and, inside the great hall of initiation, saw sacred things revealed by torchlight. Initiation was open to men and women, free and slave, Greek speakers of any city, and was believed to bring a happier lot after death. Its rites continued until the Christian emperor Theodosius I suppressed pagan cult in AD 392.

Other mysteries included those of the Great Gods on Samothrace, believed to protect sailors, and of Dionysus. The Orphic movement, linked to the mythical singer Orpheus, taught that the soul was immortal and could be purified through a special way of life; gold leaves buried with the dead give instructions for the journey to the underworld, and the Derveni Papyrus preserves a commentary on an Orphic poem.

In the Roman period mystery cults of Isis, Mithras and others spread widely.
