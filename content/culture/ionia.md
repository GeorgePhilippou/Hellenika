<!-- field: summary -->
The central west coast of Asia Minor and its islands, settled by Greeks after the Bronze Age — birthplace of Greek philosophy and science.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most advanced region of the Archaic Greek world: the home of Homeric epic, the first philosophers and the revolt that began the Persian Wars.

<!-- field: body -->
Ionia was the central part of the west coast of Asia Minor, modern Turkey, with the nearby islands of Chios and Samos. Greek speakers settled it in the centuries after about 1050 BC, in the movement later remembered as the Ionian migration. Twelve Ionian cities — including Miletus, Ephesus, Samos, Chios, Phocaea and Colophon — formed a religious league that met at the Panionion sanctuary.

Ionia sat at the meeting point of the Greek world and the kingdoms of Anatolia and the Near East, and in the Archaic period it was the most prosperous and creative part of the Greek world. The Homeric epics probably took shape here. Ionian cities founded colonies around the Black Sea and as far as France. At Miletus in the 6th century BC Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes began Greek philosophy and science, followed by Heraclitus of Ephesus; Hecataeus pioneered geography and history; and Ionian architects built the first great Ionic temples.

The Ionian cities fell under Lydian rule and, after 546 BC, under Persia. Their revolt in 499 BC, supported by Athens, led to the Persian invasions of Greece. Freed after 479 BC, they joined the Delian League, but were handed back to Persia by the King's Peace of 387 BC. Alexander freed them again in 334 BC, and Ionian cities such as Ephesus flourished in the Hellenistic and Roman periods.
