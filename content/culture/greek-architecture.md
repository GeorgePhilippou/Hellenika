<!-- field: summary -->
The temples, theatres, stoas and cities of the Greeks, built in the Doric, Ionic and later Corinthian orders.

<!-- field: significance -->
Greek temple design — columns, entablature and pediment, built on rules of proportion — became the basis of classical architecture from Rome to the present.

<!-- field: body -->
Monumental Greek architecture began in the 7th century BC, when temples built of mud brick and timber started to be rebuilt in stone. The basic form was the temple: a rectangular room (*cella*) housing the god's statue, often surrounded by a colonnade and topped by a pitched roof with triangular pediments at each end. Temples faced east, towards an altar outside, where sacrifices were made.

Two systems, or orders, developed in the 6th century BC. The Doric order, used mainly on the mainland and in the west, has sturdy fluted columns with plain cushion capitals and no base, and a frieze of alternating triglyphs and metopes. The Ionic order, from the islands and Ionia, has slenderer columns on moulded bases, with scroll-shaped capitals and a continuous frieze. The Corinthian order, with capitals of acanthus leaves, first appears at Bassae in the late 5th century BC and became popular in the Hellenistic and Roman periods.

Builders fitted stone blocks precisely without mortar, using metal clamps, and refined their designs with subtle curves, as in the Parthenon. Buildings and their sculptures were brightly painted.

Beyond temples, Greeks built theatres, stoas (long colonnaded halls), council houses, gymnasia, fountain houses and fortifications. From the 5th century BC new cities were often laid out on regular grids, a practice associated with Hippodamus of Miletus. The Romans adopted Greek forms wholesale, and through them Greek architecture shaped European building for two thousand years.
