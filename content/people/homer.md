<!-- field: summary -->
The name attached to the Iliad and the Odyssey — the foundational poems of Greek literature, and the closest thing the Greek world had to scripture.

<!-- field: significance -->
Homeric epic supplied Greeks with a shared heroic past, a common poetic language, and the moral vocabulary that tragedy and philosophy argued with for centuries.

<!-- field: body -->
Homer is the name the Greeks gave to the poet of the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*, the epics that took shape in the 8th and early 7th centuries BC, probably in Ionia on the coast of Asia Minor. Nothing certain is known about him. Seven cities claimed to be his birthplace, and tradition made him a blind singer from Chios or Smyrna. Whether one poet composed both epics, or either, is still debated.

What is clear is that the poems come from a long tradition of oral poetry. In the 1930s Milman Parry showed that their repeated phrases — "swift-footed Achilles", "the wine-dark sea" — are the working tools of singers who composed as they performed, and confirmed it by recording oral epic singers in Yugoslavia who worked the same way. The modern debate about how the poems were made, the "Homeric Question", began with Friedrich August Wolf's *Prolegomena ad Homerum* of 1795.

The poems' world is layered. Bronze Age objects, such as the boar's-tusk helmet and the tall body shield, appear beside later Iron Age customs, such as cremation, and a society closer to the poet's own time. They are best read not as a record of the Mycenaean age but as a memory of it, reshaped over centuries of retelling.

For the Greeks, Homer was simply "the poet": the foundation of education, the source of their shared heroic past, and the text that later poets and philosophers argued with. The Homeric Hymns were also credited to him in antiquity, but are the work of later poets.
