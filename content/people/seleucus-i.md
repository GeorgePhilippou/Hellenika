<!-- field: summary -->
Founder of the Seleucid Empire, the largest of Alexander's successor states, stretching from Anatolia to Central Asia.

<!-- field: significance -->
Ruled the greatest territorial share of Alexander's conquests and planted Greek cities across Mesopotamia, Iran and beyond.

<!-- field: body -->
Seleucus was a relatively minor infantry commander under Alexander, notable mainly for having remained married, alone among the senior officers, to the Sogdian noblewoman Apama after the mass marriages Alexander staged at Susa in 324 BC — a loyalty to a political match most others abandoned that later gave his dynasty a claim to genuine Persian as well as Macedonian legitimacy. He was made satrap of Babylonia in 321 BC, driven out by rival Antigonus, and returned in triumph in 312 BC — a date the Seleucid Era counts from, making it the first continuous numbered year-count in history and the direct ancestor of later systems including, ultimately, the Christian calendar's own logic of counting onward from a fixed epoch.

He pushed east as far as the Indus valley and there met the Indian king Chandragupta Maurya. Rather than fight a costly war, he ceded the easternmost provinces in exchange for 500 war elephants, a trade that proved decisive a few years later at Ipsus in 301 BC, where the elephants blocked the battlefield and separated Antigonus from his own cavalry. Seleucus went on to found dozens of new cities across his territory, populating a huge and previously loosely governed region with Greek and Macedonian colonists: sixteen were named Antioch after his father Antiochus, nine Seleucia after himself, and six Laodicea after his mother, a naming scheme that broadcast dynastic continuity across a landscape as effectively as any coin or inscription.

Late in his reign his son and co-ruler Antiochus I fell into a wasting illness that court physicians could not explain, until Erasistratus reportedly diagnosed it as lovesickness by observing Antiochus's pulse quicken whenever his young stepmother Stratonice entered the room; Seleucus, on hearing this, gave Stratonice to his son in marriage rather than lose him to grief, a story ancient writers told as an example of exceptional paternal devotion. Seleucus was assassinated in 281 BC by Ptolemy Keraunos, a disaffected son of Ptolemy I whom he had sheltered, just as he was finally moving to take Macedon itself — the one prize among Alexander's former territories that eluded him to the end.
