<!-- field: summary -->
Student of Socrates, founder of the Academy, and author of the dialogues that set the agenda for Western philosophy.

<!-- field: significance -->
Whitehead's remark that European philosophy is footnotes to Plato overstates the case, but not by much: metaphysics, epistemology, ethics and political theory all take their initial shape here.

<!-- field: body -->
Plato founded the Academy around 387 BC in a grove outside Athens sacred to the hero Academus — arguably the first institution of higher learning in Europe, and one that, under a long line of successive heads, continued operating in some form for roughly nine centuries after his death, making it one of the longest-lived intellectual institutions in Western history before its final closure. His journeys were not confined to Athens: he travelled at least twice to Syracuse in Sicily, drawn by the ambition of shaping the tyrant Dionysius I, or later his son Dionysius II, into something resembling his ideal of a philosopher-king, ventures that ended badly enough that, according to a persistent and plausible ancient tradition, Plato was actually sold into slavery on one visit before being recognised, ransomed and returned safely to Athens.

His central doctrine is the theory of Forms: the changing, imperfect things people perceive with their senses are merely partial instances of perfect, unchanging realities that can only be grasped by reason rather than observation. The *Republic* builds an imagined just city as a working model for the structure of a just individual soul, argues at length for rule by philosopher-kings uniquely qualified by education to see the truth, and contains the Allegory of the Cave, in which ordinary people are compared to prisoners mistaking shadows on a wall for reality itself. His concrete political proposals in that dialogue are genuinely authoritarian by most modern standards — strict censorship of poetry and music, a rigid hereditary-seeming class structure softened only by exceptional individual promotion, and state control over reproduction and child-rearing among the guardian class — and treating him as a straightforward proto-democrat requires ignoring a great deal of what the text actually says.

Unusually for any ancient author, his complete body of work survives intact, alongside a handful of additional dialogues and letters, including the disputed *Seventh Letter*, whose authenticity as Plato's own genuine autobiographical account remains debated among specialists.
