<!-- field: summary -->
The young Spartan king of c. 244–241 BC who tried to restore Sparta through debt cancellation and land reform, and was executed.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first of Sparta's revolutionary kings, whose failed reforms inspired Cleomenes III.

<!-- field: body -->
By the 3rd century BC Sparta had fallen far from its classical power. Land had become concentrated in a few hands, many Spartans were in debt, and the number of full citizens had fallen to about 700, of whom perhaps 100 owned land.

Agis IV, of the Eurypontid royal house, became king around 244 BC at about the age of twenty. Inspired by the idealised picture of Lycurgus' Sparta, he proposed to cancel all debts and to divide the land into 4,500 equal lots for Spartans, including new citizens drawn from the perioikoi and foreigners, and 15,000 for perioikoi. He gave up his own wealth, and his mother and grandmother, among the richest people in Sparta, supported him.

He was opposed by the other king, Leonidas II, whom his supporters had deposed. The debts were cancelled and the records of loans burned in the marketplace, but the redistribution was delayed. While Agis was away campaigning with the Achaean League, his opponents recovered. On his return Leonidas was restored, and in 241 BC the ephors had Agis strangled in prison, with his mother and grandmother — the first Spartan king put to death by his own people. Our main source is Plutarch's *Life of Agis*.
