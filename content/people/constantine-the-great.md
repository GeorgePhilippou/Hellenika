<!-- field: summary -->
The Roman emperor (306–337) who made Christianity the favoured religion of the empire and founded Constantinople.

<!-- field: significance -->
His conversion and his new capital began the Christian Roman empire of the east, which historians call Byzantine.

<!-- field: body -->
Constantine (c. 272–337) was the son of Constantius I, one of four co-emperors of the Tetrarchy. When his father died at York in 306, the army proclaimed him emperor, and he fought his way to power through a series of civil wars. In 312, before defeating his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome, he had a vision or dream, later described by Christian writers, that led him to fight under a Christian sign. In 313 he and his colleague Licinius issued the Edict of Milan granting toleration to Christians.

Constantine gave the Church money, buildings and privileges, and intervened in its disputes. In 325 he summoned the first general council of bishops at Nicaea in Asia Minor, which condemned the teaching of Arius and agreed the first form of the Nicene Creed.

After defeating Licinius in 324 he became sole emperor and chose the old Greek city of Byzantion as his new capital, rebuilding it on a grand scale and dedicating it as Constantinople in 330. He stripped temples across the Greek world of statues to adorn it, including the bronze serpent column from Delphi, which still stands in Istanbul. He was baptised on his deathbed in 337. The Orthodox Church honours him as a saint.
