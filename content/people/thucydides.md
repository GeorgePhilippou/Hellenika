<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian general turned historian of the Peloponnesian War, and the founder of critical, evidence-based historical method.

<!-- field: significance -->
Stripped the gods and the marvellous out of historical explanation, replacing them with power, interest and human nature — the model for political history ever since.

<!-- field: body -->
Thucydides came from a wealthy Athenian family with mining rights and property connections in Thrace, an inheritance that mattered enormously once his career turned against him: commanding an Athenian fleet in 424 BC, he failed to reach the strategically important city of Amphipolis before the Spartan general Brasidas took it, and was exiled for twenty years as a result. He used the exile productively rather than bitterly, travelling on the Peloponnesian side of the war as his own Thracian connections allowed and interviewing participants from both Athens and Sparta — which is precisely why the finished work reads as something closer to an even-handed inquiry than a partisan Athenian account of its own war.

He states his method explicitly and pointedly against Herodotus's example: he rejects poetic embellishment and traditional storytelling in favour of cross-checking eyewitness reports against one another, and warns his readers plainly that the result will be less immediately entertaining because of it. He intends the finished history, in his own famous phrase, as "a possession for all time" rather than a prize composition for one-off recitation. His analysis of how prolonged war corrodes ordinary language and morality — most notably his account of the vicious civil war (stasis) that tore apart Corcyra, where he observes that words themselves changed their accepted meanings to justify atrocities — is still routinely cited by modern political scientists and historians of civil conflict, and his broader account of how a rising power's growth and the fear it provokes in an established one can make war difficult to avoid has lent its name to the modern phrase "Thucydides trap," used in discussions of great-power rivalry today.

The history breaks off abruptly mid-sentence in the events of 411 BC, seven years before the war actually ended, and it fell to the historian Xenophon, among others, to pick up the narrative where Thucydides had left it.
