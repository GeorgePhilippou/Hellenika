<!-- field: summary -->
Epicurus's closest friend and first great disciple, who defended the Garden's teaching in writing.

<!-- field: significance -->
Metrodorus shows the Garden's ideal of friendship in practice, and was honoured as the second founder in the school's cult of its heroes.

<!-- field: body -->
Metrodorus came from Lampsacus on the Hellespont, where Epicurus taught for a time before moving to Athens in 306 BC. He followed him, and stayed with him for the rest of his life. The Epicureans later ranked him next to Epicurus himself, along with Hermarchus and Polyaenus, as one of the four "leaders" of the school.

Epicurus's will, quoted by Diogenes Laertius, shows the depth of the tie. He asks that the children of Metrodorus be raised with the help of the school's funds, and orders that Metrodorus's memory, along with his own, be marked with a yearly feast. The images of the two men were preserved together, and several double herms survive.

Metrodorus wrote to defend the school against rivals. Titles include works against the physicians, the dialecticians and the sophists, against Timocrates, his own brother who left the school, and a treatise On Wealth. He has a reputation for taking Epicurus's position on pleasure at its crudest: a line preserved by Plutarch has him saying that "the belly" is the source of all good. Whether he meant it literally, or a more moderate point about bodily pleasure as the root of all others, is disputed.

Fragments of his work have been identified among the carbonised papyri from Herculaneum, together with quotations in Philodemus, Plutarch and Seneca. He died at fifty-three, and Epicurus's letters of consolation to his family are recorded.
