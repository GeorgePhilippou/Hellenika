<!-- field: summary -->
King of Macedon who destroyed the Achaemenid Empire in a decade of campaigning, reached the Indus, and died at 32 without an heir.

<!-- field: significance -->
Redrew the political map from the Adriatic to the Punjab and made Greek the language of government and learning across the Near East for a thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Alexander's education was as deliberate an investment as any his father made. Philip II hired Aristotle himself to tutor the boy from around age thirteen, and whatever else the philosopher's lessons in logic, ethics and natural science gave him, Alexander kept a annotated copy of the Iliad, supposedly a gift from Aristotle, with him on campaign for the rest of his life and slept with it under his pillow alongside a dagger. The other formative story from his youth is the taming of Bucephalus, a horse considered too wild for anyone else to ride, which the twelve-year-old Alexander managed by noticing the animal was startled by its own shadow and simply turning it to face the sun — a small anecdote later writers treated as proof that the qualities that made him a conqueror were visible from childhood.

He became king at twenty in 336 BC, immediately after his father's assassination, in circumstances murky enough that ancient and modern historians alike have wondered how much Alexander or his mother Olympias knew in advance. He moved fast to secure the throne, having rival claimants killed, and just as fast to suppress revolts in Greece that broke out at the news of Philip's death, making an example of Thebes by destroying the city and enslaving its surviving population while sparing, according to tradition, only the house of the poet Pindar. He crossed into Asia in 334 BC with a combined Macedonian and allied Greek force and won three set-piece battles against the Achaemenid Persian Empire in succession — the Granicus, Issus, and finally Gaugamela in 331 BC, where Darius III's much larger army broke and its king fled the field for the last time. Along the way Alexander took the island fortress of Tyre only by building an artificial causeway out to it under constant bombardment, was crowned pharaoh in Egypt without a fight, and burned the Persian ceremonial capital of Persepolis, an act ancient sources disagree on describing as calculated reprisal for the Persian sack of Athens a century and a half earlier or as a drunken accident that got out of hand.

At Gordion, in Phrygia, Alexander is said to have encountered a wagon whose yoke was fastened by an impossibly complex knot, tied to no visible end, which local prophecy held would be undone only by the future ruler of Asia. Rather than working out how to untie it, he simply cut it through with his sword — a story almost certainly embellished or invented after the fact, but one that stuck precisely because it captured something real about how he preferred to solve problems. A similar instinct for self-presentation shows in his detour into Egypt's western desert to consult the Oracle of Zeus-Ammon at the remote Siwa oasis in 331 BC, an exhausting journey he made specifically so that the oracle's answer, whatever exactly it was, could be reported back home as confirmation that he was the god's own son — a claim Alexander leaned on increasingly as his campaigns went on, and one later Hellenistic kings imitated in their own royal cults.

The campaign did not end at Persepolis. Alexander pushed on through Bactria and Sogdiana in modern Central Asia, over the Hindu Kush, and down into the Punjab, where he defeated the Indian king Porus and his war elephants at the Hydaspes river in 326 BC during a battle fought in monsoon rain that made the ground treacherous for cavalry and infantry alike. It was at the Hyphasis river shortly afterward that his own army finally refused to go any further east, exhausted by years of continuous campaigning and unwilling to face whatever larger kingdoms supposedly lay beyond the Ganges; Alexander reportedly sulked in his tent for three days before accepting the mutiny and turning back. The return march through the Gedrosian desert, undertaken partly to outdo a legendary earlier crossing by the Persian queen Semiramis, killed thousands of soldiers and camp followers to heat and thirst — arguably the single costliest stretch of the entire expedition in terms of ordinary lives lost.

Alexander was never simply a Greek conqueror imposing Macedonian rule wholesale. He retained Persian administrators in the satrapies he took, adopted elements of Persian royal dress and court ceremonial, and married first the Bactrian noblewoman Roxana and later a daughter of Darius III, part of a deliberate policy of fusing Macedonian and Persian elites that climaxed in 324 BC with mass weddings at Susa between his officers and Persian noblewomen. That same policy provoked real resistance: his attempt to require proskynesis, a Persian gesture of prostration his own companions regarded as fit only for worshipping a god, met open Macedonian refusal and had to be quietly dropped, and at Opis later that year his veteran troops mutinied outright when he moved to discharge them and replace them with Persian recruits, forcing a public reconciliation banquet before the crisis passed. His temper had already turned lethal well before then: he killed his friend and former commander Cleitus with a spear in a drunken argument at a banquet in 328 BC, and had the general Parmenion, one of his father's most trusted lieutenants, executed on suspicion of conspiracy after Parmenion's son had already been condemned.

Alexander died in Babylon in June 323 BC after an illness of about eleven days, spiking fevers that ancient sources describe in enough clinical detail that modern physicians have argued over the diagnosis ever since — poison, malaria, typhoid fever, and complications from chronic wounds and heavy drinking have all had serious advocates, along with rarer proposals like Guillain-Barré syndrome, and no single explanation commands consensus. He was thirty-two, left no adult heir, and when his marshals reportedly asked on his deathbed who should inherit the empire, he is said to have answered only "to the strongest" — an answer, true or invented, that turned out to be exactly what happened. His empire began fragmenting within days of his death and was fought over for decades afterward by his former generals in the wars of the Diadochi, but the political and cultural map those wars eventually settled into — Greek-speaking kingdoms stretching from Egypt to Central Asia, Greek established as the common administrative and intellectual language of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East for the next thousand years — was Alexander's most durable legacy, built in barely a decade of campaigning and outlasting the empire that produced it by many centuries.
