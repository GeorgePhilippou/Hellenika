<!-- field: summary -->
Molossian princess, wife of Philip II, mother of Alexander, and a ruthless political operator in the succession wars after his death.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the few women in Greek history who exercised open political and military power, and was executed for it.

<!-- field: body -->
Olympias came from the royal house of Epirus and claimed descent from Achilles — a lineage Alexander took seriously enough to visit Troy early in his campaign and honour Achilles' tomb personally. Ancient sources describe her involvement in ecstatic Dionysiac religion, including handling live snakes during ritual, though these accounts come almost entirely from writers hostile to her and eager to paint her as exotic and unsettling. She reportedly encouraged, or at least never firmly denied, stories that Alexander's true father was not Philip at all but Zeus himself, visiting her in the form of a serpent — a claim that flattered Alexander's own later pretensions to divine ancestry and that Alexander seems to have found useful rather than embarrassing.

After Philip took another wife, the Macedonian noblewoman Cleopatra, in a match that threatened to produce a rival, fully Macedonian heir, Olympias was politically sidelined, and after Philip's assassination in 336 BC she was widely suspected, then and since, of some hand in it, not least because she was seen publicly honouring the assassin's tomb. During the wars of the Diadochi following Alexander's own death, she returned from exile in Epirus to invade Macedon in 317 BC, defeated the forces of the rival regent Eurydice, and had both Eurydice and her husband Philip III Arrhidaeus — Alexander's own mentally unfit half-brother, who had briefly shared the throne — put to death, forcing Eurydice to take her own life by sword, noose or poison rather than executing her outright. Cassander besieged her in turn at Pydna; she surrendered on terms guaranteeing her life, and was killed anyway in 316 BC after a staged assembly condemned her, reportedly by relatives of those she had ordered killed, since Cassander's own soldiers refused to carry out the execution themselves.
