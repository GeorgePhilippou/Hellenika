<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek geographer of the Augustan age whose 17-book Geography describes the whole known world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most complete geographical work to survive from antiquity, and a mine of information on the cities and peoples of the Greek world.

<!-- field: body -->
Strabo (c. 64 BC–after AD 21) was born into a wealthy Greek family at Amaseia in Pontus, northern Asia Minor. He studied in Asia Minor, Rome and Alexandria, travelled from Armenia to Italy and up the Nile, and lived through the transition from the Roman Republic to the empire of Augustus.

His *Geography*, in 17 books, survives almost complete. After two books on the theory of geography — in which he discusses and criticises Eratosthenes, Polybius and others — he describes the inhabited world region by region: Spain, Gaul and Britain, Italy, the Balkans and Greece (three books), Asia Minor, the Near East, India, Egypt and Libya. He combines physical geography with history, myth, economy and customs, and often quotes Homer, whom he regarded as the first geographer.

His books on Greece describe many cities and sanctuaries, often in decline under Rome, and are a key source for ancient topography. He also wrote a history, now lost, continuing Polybius. Written for statesmen and administrators of the new Roman empire, the *Geography* shows how the Greek East looked at the dawn of Roman imperial rule.
