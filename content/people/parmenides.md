<!-- field: summary -->
Philosopher of Elea whose poem argued that change and plurality are illusions and only unchanging being is real.

<!-- field: significance -->
Parmenides began the argument about what can be said to exist, and every later Greek cosmologist, Plato among them, had to answer him.

<!-- field: body -->
Parmenides lived at Elea, a Phocaean colony on the coast of southern Italy, and probably flourished in the early fifth century BC. His dates are uncertain: Diogenes Laertius places his prime in 504–501 BC, while Plato's dialogue, which has him about sixty-five when Socrates was young, points to a birth around 515. The two do not agree.

His poem opens with a chariot ride to the house of a goddess who reveals two paths of inquiry. The Way of Truth argues, step by step, that what exists cannot have come into being, since it would have to arise from what is not, and that it cannot perish, divide or change. On this argument the world of the senses, with its motion and multiplicity, cannot be what is really there. The Way of Opinion then gives a cosmology of light and night, presented as the best account of appearances.

Readers still disagree on what Parmenides meant. Some take him to deny plurality and change outright; others read him as making a claim about how we must speak and reason about what is. What no one disputes is that the argument was new, that it was strict, and that it could not be ignored. His pupil Zeno defended it with paradoxes of motion, and the atomists, Empedocles and Plato all built their systems in response.

The poem is fragmentary, and all of it comes down through later quotation. Every summary of it, including this one, is an interpretation.
