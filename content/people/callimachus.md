<!-- field: summary -->
The scholar-poet of Hellenistic Alexandria who catalogued the Library and championed short, learned, polished poetry.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most influential Hellenistic poet, whose ideal of refined, learned verse shaped Roman poets from Catullus to Ovid.

<!-- field: body -->
Callimachus (c. 310–240 BC) was born in Cyrene in Libya and moved to Alexandria, where he worked under Ptolemy II and Ptolemy III at the court and the Library. He never became head librarian, but he compiled the *Pinakes* ("Tables"), a catalogue of Greek literature in 120 books, listing authors by genre with their works — the first great library catalogue and a foundation of literary scholarship.

He was also a prolific poet. His surviving works include six hymns to the gods and over sixty epigrams; much else, such as the *Aetia* ("Causes"), a long poem explaining the origins of customs and rites, survives in papyrus fragments. In *The Lock of Berenice* he tells how a lock of hair dedicated by Queen Berenice II became a constellation.

Callimachus stood for a new poetic ideal: short, highly polished, learned poems full of rare myths and allusions, rather than long epic. "A big book is a big evil," he is said to have declared, and in the *Aetia* he says Apollo told him to keep his Muse slender and to drive his chariot along untrodden paths. Ancient tradition imagined a feud with Apollonius of Rhodes over epic, though this may be invented.

Roman poets — Catullus, Propertius, Virgil and Ovid — took him as their model, and Catullus translated *The Lock of Berenice*.
