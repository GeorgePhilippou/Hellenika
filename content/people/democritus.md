<!-- field: summary -->
Philosopher from Abdera who, with his teacher Leucippus, proposed that the world is made entirely of indivisible atoms moving in empty space.

<!-- field: significance -->
Atomism offered a fully mechanistic account of nature with no role for divine purpose — atoms and void, nothing else — that later became the physical foundation of Epicurean philosophy and, much later, an ancestor of the modern atomic theory of matter, though the resemblance is conceptual rather than a direct scientific lineage.

<!-- field: body -->
Democritus is usually paired with his teacher Leucippus, about whom almost nothing else is known, as co-founder of atomism. Their claim was that everything is composed of atomos — literally "uncuttable" — particles, infinite in number, differing from one another only in shape, size, order and position, moving through an infinite void. Combination and separation of atoms accounts for coming-into-being and passing away; there is no other kind of change. Qualities we perceive as colour, taste or temperature are, on this account, effects of atomic arrangement on our senses rather than real properties of the atoms themselves.

This was a strictly mechanistic physics, without gods directing events or any built-in purpose to nature — a position later generations found either liberating or alarming. Democritus was reportedly also an ethical writer, urging a tranquil cheerfulness (euthymia) reached through moderation and inner contentment rather than external success, themes that anticipate Epicurus directly.

Ancient tradition remembers him as "the laughing philosopher," amused by human folly rather than despairing of it — a deliberate contrast drawn against the gloomier Heraclitus. Of his large output, reportedly some seventy titles, only fragments survive, mostly through quotation.
