<!-- field: summary -->
The aristocratic poet of Mytilene on Lesbos, contemporary of Sappho, whose songs of politics, war and wine were sung at the symposium.

<!-- field: significance -->
A leading lyric poet of the Archaic age, whose songs give an inside view of aristocratic feuds and tyranny on Lesbos.

<!-- field: body -->
Alcaeus was born around 625 BC into an aristocratic family of Mytilene, the chief city of Lesbos, and was a contemporary of Sappho. His life was taken up with the violent politics of his city. He and his brothers fought against the tyrant Myrsilus, and when Pittacus, once their ally, took power with popular support, Alcaeus turned his bitter verse against him, calling him "base-born" and mocking his looks. He spent years in exile, and later tradition says Pittacus finally pardoned him.

His poems, in the Aeolic dialect of Lesbos, were written for singing at the symposium among his companions. They celebrate wine ("let us drink — why wait for the lamps?"), describe a hall gleaming with weapons, praise the gods and lament exile. He used the image of the storm-tossed ship for a city in civil strife, the first known "ship of state". Like Archilochus, he confessed to losing his shield in battle.

Ancient scholars collected his work in ten books; it now survives only in quotations and papyrus fragments.

The stanza named after him, the Alcaic, was adopted by the Roman poet Horace, who saw Alcaeus and Sappho as his models.
