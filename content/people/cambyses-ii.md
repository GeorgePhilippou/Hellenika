<!-- field: summary -->
Son and successor of Cyrus the Great, who conquered Egypt for Persia in 525 BC and died amid a succession crisis three years later.

<!-- field: significance -->
His conquest of Egypt made the Achaemenid Empire span from the Aegean to the Nile, the largest political unit the ancient Near East had yet seen, and his sudden death opened the crisis that brought Darius I to power.

<!-- field: body -->
Cambyses inherited an empire from Cyrus that already stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia, and completed its dominance of the Near East by adding Egypt. He defeated Pharaoh Psamtik III at Pelusium in 525 BC after Egypt's former Greek and Phoenician allies withdrew their support, and took Memphis shortly after, ending the 26th Dynasty and folding Egypt into the empire as a satrapy that endured for nearly two centuries.

Herodotus and other Greek sources depict him as increasingly erratic and cruel during his time in Egypt — desecrating temples, mocking Egyptian religion, and, most famously, stabbing the sacred Apis bull, an act supposedly followed by a wasting illness and madness. Modern historians treat this tradition skeptically: contemporary Egyptian evidence, including a properly buried Apis bull from his reign, contradicts the story, and the hostile portrait likely reflects the resentment of Egyptian priesthoods whose temple income Cambyses had reduced.

He died in 522 BC on the march back from Egypt, reportedly from an accidental wound, just as news arrived of a usurper — Gaumata, claiming to be Cambyses' own brother Bardiya, who had in fact been killed in secret years earlier — seizing the throne in his absence. The crisis that followed brought Darius I, a more distant relative, to power.
