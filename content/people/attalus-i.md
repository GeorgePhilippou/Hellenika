<!-- field: summary -->
The ruler of Pergamon from 241 to 197 BC who defeated the Galatians, took the title of king and made Pergamon Rome's ally in the east.

<!-- field: significance -->
Founder of the Attalid kingdom, whose victory monuments created one of the most famous styles of Hellenistic sculpture.

<!-- field: body -->
Attalus I (269–197 BC) inherited the small principality of Pergamon in north-western Asia Minor in 241 BC. The Galatians — Celtic tribes who had crossed into Asia Minor in 278 — were extorting tribute from the Greek cities. Attalus refused to pay, and around 238 defeated them near the sources of the river Caicus. After the victory he took the title of king and the name Soter, "saviour".

He celebrated his victories over the Galatians and the Seleucids with bronze monuments on the acropolis of Pergamon. Marble copies made for the Romans, including the Dying Gaul in the Capitoline Museums and the Gaul killing himself and his wife in Palazzo Altemps, are generally thought to reproduce figures from them, showing the defeated enemy with dignity and pathos.

Attalus saw Philip V of Macedon as the main threat and became one of Rome's first allies in the Greek east, fighting beside Rome in the First and Second Macedonian Wars. In 205/204 BC he helped Rome bring the sacred black stone of the Great Mother, Cybele, from Asia Minor to Rome. He collapsed while addressing a meeting at Thebes in 197 and died soon after. His son Eumenes II succeeded him.
