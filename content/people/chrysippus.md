<!-- field: summary -->
Third head of the Stoa, whose voluminous writing turned Zeno's teaching into a complete philosophical system.

<!-- field: significance -->
Chrysippus's work on logic and on fate and freedom made Stoicism the dominant philosophy of the Hellenistic and Roman world.

<!-- field: body -->
Chrysippus was born at Soli in Cilicia and came to Athens as a young man. He studied with Cleanthes, Zeno's successor, and also with sceptical members of the Academy, which gave him an unusual skill in arguing against his own positions. When Cleanthes died about 230 BC, Chrysippus took charge of the Stoa.

Diogenes Laertius lists more than 700 books and adds that he wrote as many as 500 lines a day. All of them are lost, and the tradition depends on quotation. Yet the scale of the work explains the ancient saying that without Chrysippus there would be no Stoa: he elaborated Zeno's ideas into a coherent account of logic, physics and ethics, and defended them against Academic and Epicurean objections.

His most lasting contribution was in logic. Where Aristotle's syllogistic reasons about terms, Chrysippus worked out a logic of propositions, with rules for combining "if", "and" and "or", and modern logicians regard this as a real precursor of the propositional calculus. In physics he argued that the cosmos is a single rational whole, permeated by a breath he called pneuma, and that everything happens by fate. In ethics he faced the problem of freedom: he argued that we are responsible for our actions because they follow from our own character, even though that character is itself part of the causal chain.

The old anecdote that he died laughing at a donkey eating figs, after telling its owner to give it wine to wash them down, may be a joke made at the expense of a famously serious man.
