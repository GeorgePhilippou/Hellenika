<!-- field: summary -->
Pythagorean from southern Italy whose cosmos, with a central fire and a moving earth, broke with the idea that the earth is the centre.

<!-- field: significance -->
Philolaus's system is the earliest Greek account that set the earth in motion, and our best witness to Pythagorean thought before Plato.

<!-- field: body -->
Philolaus was a Pythagorean, born at Croton or Taras, who in the fifth century BC left Italy, perhaps after the political attacks on the Pythagorean communities, and taught at Thebes. According to Plato's Phaedo, two of the people present at Socrates's death, Simmias and Cebes, had been his pupils. Ancient tradition calls him the first Pythagorean to write his teaching down.

Aristotle describes his cosmology. At the centre of the universe is a fire, not the sun. The earth is one of ten bodies revolving around it: the sphere of the fixed stars, the five planets, the sun, the moon, the earth and a hidden "counter-earth", probably added to bring the number up to ten, the perfect number. It is not a heliocentric model, and it was not based on observation, but it removes the earth from the centre and puts it in motion, and later astronomers, including Aristarchus, sometimes cited it.

The surviving fragments say that everything consists of "limiters" and "unlimiteds" fitted together by harmony, and that number makes things knowable. Their authenticity is disputed. Walter Burkert regarded them as forgeries of the Hellenistic age, and other scholars, led by Carl Huffman, defend most of them as genuine. The question matters, because on Huffman's view Philolaus supplies the only reliable early evidence for Pythagorean philosophy in the writers' own words.
