<!-- field: summary -->
Soldier, student of Socrates and author of the Anabasis — the first-person account of ten thousand Greek mercenaries fighting their way out of Persia.

<!-- field: significance -->
Wrote the earliest surviving military memoir, and provides the main continuation of Greek history after Thucydides breaks off.

<!-- field: body -->
Xenophon joined a large mercenary army hired by the Persian prince Cyrus the Younger in a bid to overthrow his own brother, the reigning Persian king Artaxerxes II. Cyrus was killed at the battle of Cunaxa in 401 BC, and shortly afterward the senior Greek generals were treacherously seized under a supposed truce and executed by the Persian satrap Tissaphernes. Left leaderless and stranded roughly a thousand miles inside hostile Persian territory with no safe route home, the surviving Greek soldiers elected an entirely new set of commanders — Xenophon prominent among them despite having no prior senior command experience — and fought and marched their way north through a brutal Armenian winter toward the Black Sea coast. Their collective shout of "Thalatta! Thalatta!" — "the sea, the sea!" — on finally sighting open water from a mountain pass is among the most famous single moments in all of Greek literature, a spontaneous eruption of relief after months of danger.

He later fought openly for Sparta, including at one point against his own native Athens, a choice of loyalty that led directly to his formal exile from the city; Sparta compensated him with a country estate at Scillus in the Peloponnese, where he lived for years hunting, writing, and entertaining guests before political upheaval eventually forced him to leave that too. He wrote widely and in strikingly varied genres: the *Hellenica*, continuing Greek history from precisely where Thucydides's own account breaks off; several Socratic memoirs offering a more conventional, less philosophically demanding portrait of Socrates than Plato's; a practical treatise on horsemanship; and another on the management of a private estate. His vivid first-hand demonstration that a disciplined Greek force could fight its way clean across the heart of the Persian Empire and survive was not lost on later readers, including, by most accounts, Philip II and Alexander themselves.
