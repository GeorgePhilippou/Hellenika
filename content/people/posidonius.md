<!-- field: summary -->
Stoic polymath of Rhodes who studied the tides, measured the earth and wrote a world history, and taught Cicero.

<!-- field: significance -->
Posidonius shows the Stoics at their most empirical, and his lost books were a main source for Roman writers on geography, astronomy and history.

<!-- field: body -->
Posidonius was born at Apamea in Syria about 135 BC, studied in Athens under the Stoic Panaetius, and settled at Rhodes, where he led the city's government and ran a school. Cicero and Pompey both visited him there.

His interests were wide, and he treated them as parts of one Stoic system. In natural philosophy he held that the cosmos is bound together by a sympathy of its parts, which made it reasonable to link the tides to the moon. On a journey to Spain he watched the tides at Gades, present-day Cádiz, and recorded how they followed the lunar cycle. He also wrote a large history that continued Polybius, studies of peoples he encountered, including the Gauls, and works on ethics and the emotions, in which he returned to Plato's idea of a divided soul and criticised Chrysippus.

Two measurements are attributed to him. One was the circumference of the Earth, which Strabo and Cleomedes report in different values, so it is unclear which he chose. Cicero, in On the Nature of the Gods, describes a sphere "recently constructed by our friend Posidonius" that reproduced the motions of the sun, moon and planets. It has led some to think of the Antikythera Mechanism, which may have been made at Rhodes, although nothing ties Posidonius directly to it.

None of his books survives. Much of what we know comes from Strabo, Cicero, Seneca and Galen, who used him and often disagreed.
