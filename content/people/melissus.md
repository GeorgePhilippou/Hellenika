<!-- field: summary -->
Samian admiral and Eleatic philosopher who defended Parmenides in prose and argued that being is boundless.

<!-- field: significance -->
Melissus gave the Eleatic argument its clearest prose form, and is the rare early philosopher known to have won a naval battle.

<!-- field: body -->
Melissus of Samos is one of the few ancient philosophers who also held military command. Plutarch reports that when Samos revolted from Athens in 440 BC, Melissus led the Samian fleet and defeated an Athenian squadron while Pericles was away, at least for a time. The revolt ended in Athenian victory the following year.

As a philosopher he belongs to the Eleatic tradition. Where Parmenides had written a hexameter poem, Melissus wrote plain prose, and the ten or so fragments that survive in Simplicius read as a chain of short arguments. He argues that what is has no beginning or end, since it would have to come from nothing; that it is therefore unlimited in time; and, unlike Parmenides, who described being as a finite sphere, that it is unlimited in extent. A single, unlimited thing cannot move, since there is nowhere for it to move into, and cannot have a body, since bodies have parts.

He took the conclusion seriously. The world of change that our senses report must therefore be an illusion, or the senses are unreliable. Aristotle thought his arguments were crude compared with Parmenides', but later thinkers, including the atomists, took them as a challenge, and a work critical of him appears among the writings in the Aristotelian corpus.
