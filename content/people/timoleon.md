<!-- field: summary -->
The Corinthian general who freed Syracuse from tyranny in 344 BC, defeated Carthage and refounded Greek Sicily.

<!-- field: significance -->
Remembered in antiquity as the ideal liberator, who gave up power once his work was done.

<!-- field: body -->
Timoleon (c. 411–337 BC) was a Corinthian aristocrat. In the 360s his brother Timophanes tried to make himself tyrant of Corinth, and Timoleon consented to his killing — an act that left him under a cloud for twenty years.

In 344 BC the Syracusans, torn by civil war and threatened by Carthage, appealed to Corinth, their mother city, for help. Timoleon was sent with a small force. He won control of Syracuse, and the tyrant Dionysius II surrendered and was allowed to retire to Corinth. Timoleon pulled down the tyrants' fortress on the island of Ortygia and introduced a more moderate government.

Around 341 BC he defeated a much larger Carthaginian army at the river Crimisus in western Sicily, in a battle fought in a thunderstorm, and then removed the tyrants of other Sicilian cities. Many cities had been ruined or depopulated by decades of war, and he invited tens of thousands of settlers from Greece and Italy to resettle them; archaeology shows a revival of building and farming in Sicily in the following decades.

Timoleon then laid down his power and lived as a private citizen in Syracuse, where he went blind. When he died around 337 BC the city honoured him with a public tomb.
