<!-- field: summary -->
The Alexandrian physician of the early 3rd century BC who founded scientific anatomy through human dissection.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first person known to have dissected human bodies systematically, discovering much of the anatomy of the brain and nerves.

<!-- field: body -->
Herophilus (c. 335–280 BC) was born at Chalcedon on the Bosporus and worked at Alexandria under the first Ptolemies. There, for a brief period unique in antiquity, the dissection of human corpses was permitted, and Herophilus used it to found the systematic study of anatomy. The Roman writers Celsus and Tertullian later claimed that he and his younger contemporary Erasistratus also cut open living criminals provided by the king; whether this is true is debated.

His writings are lost, but later authors, especially Galen, preserve many of his findings. He showed that the brain, not the heart as Aristotle thought, is the centre of the nervous system, and distinguished nerves from tendons and blood vessels, and sensory from motor nerves. He described the ventricles of the brain, the eye and the optic nerve, the liver, the reproductive organs, and the first part of the small intestine, which he named the *duodenum* ("twelve finger-breadths") for its length. He also studied the pulse, timing it with a water clock.

He founded a school of physicians at Alexandria. After his time human dissection was abandoned, and was not practised again systematically until the Renaissance.
