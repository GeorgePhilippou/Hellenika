<!-- field: summary -->
Epicurean philosopher and poet whose library was buried at Herculaneum, and who brought Epicurean thought to Roman Italy.

<!-- field: significance -->
Philodemus's carbonised scrolls are the only philosophical library to survive from antiquity, and are still being read with new imaging techniques.

<!-- field: body -->
Philodemus was born at Gadara in Syria and studied at Athens with the Epicurean Zeno of Sidon. He moved to Italy in the 70s BC and became the client of Lucius Calpurnius Piso, consul in 58 BC and father-in-law of Julius Caesar. Cicero attacked Piso in a speech, and in doing so gave a hostile sketch of his house-philosopher, who is agreeable but, he says, too refined for a rough patron.

He wrote on a range of subjects and gave a unique first-hand view of the Hellenistic schools' quarrels. Titles include On Music, On Poems, On Rhetoric, On Piety, On Signs, and a history of the philosophers. He also wrote light epigrams, about thirty of which survive in the Greek Anthology, and whose subjects are love and wine, in the Epicurean mode of enjoying the day.

In 1752 excavators at Herculaneum, the Roman town buried by the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79, found a villa with a library of more than 1,800 papyrus rolls, carbonised but partly legible. Most are works by Philodemus. The owner of this Villa of the Papyri was probably Piso himself. The scrolls were unrolled with great difficulty in the eighteenth century, and since then infrared imaging, multispectral imaging and other techniques have revealed more text.

As a result we can read a Hellenistic philosopher in his own words, including quotations from earlier Epicureans, such as Metrodorus, whose works are otherwise lost.
