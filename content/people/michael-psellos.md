<!-- field: summary -->
The brilliant 11th-century Byzantine scholar, courtier and historian whose Chronographia portrays the emperors he served.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most learned man of his age, who revived Platonic philosophy in Byzantium and wrote one of its most vivid histories.

<!-- field: body -->
Michael Psellos (1018–c. 1078) was born in Constantinople to a modest family and rose through the civil service by his learning and eloquence. From the 1040s he advised a series of emperors, beginning with Constantine IX Monomachos, who made him chief of the philosophers (*hypatos tōn philosophōn*) in a revived school of higher learning. He survived the frequent palace coups by changing sides with skill, briefly becoming a monk in 1054 when out of favour.

He wrote on almost everything: philosophy, theology, rhetoric, law, medicine, astronomy and the occult, and hundreds of letters survive. He championed the study of Plato and the Neoplatonists such as Proclus, which led to accusations of paganism; he insisted that philosophy served Christianity.

His masterpiece, the *Chronographia*, covers the emperors from Basil II to Michael VII in the 1070s. Unlike traditional chronicles, it is built around psychological portraits of the rulers — the austere Basil, the empresses Zoe and Theodora, the pleasure-loving Constantine IX — and Psellos' own part in events. He was tutor and adviser to Michael VII, whose policies contributed to the disasters after Manzikert, and he disappears from history around 1078.
