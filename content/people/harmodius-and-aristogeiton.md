<!-- field: summary -->
The two Athenians who killed Hipparchus, brother of the tyrant Hippias, in 514 BC, and were honoured as the heroes of Athenian freedom.

<!-- field: significance -->
The founding heroes of Athenian democracy's memory, whose statue group stood in the Agora as a symbol of resistance to tyranny.

<!-- field: body -->
Harmodius and Aristogeiton were Athenian aristocrats and lovers. In 514 BC, at the Panathenaic festival, they attacked the ruling family of Athens. They meant to kill the tyrant Hippias, but killed his brother Hipparchus instead. Harmodius was cut down on the spot; Aristogeiton was caught and executed.

According to Thucydides, their motive was personal. Hipparchus had made advances to Harmodius, been rejected, and in revenge insulted his sister by excluding her from a festival procession. Thucydides tells the story precisely to correct the popular belief that the pair had freed Athens: tyranny in fact lasted four more years, until the Spartans expelled Hippias in 510 BC.

Nevertheless, democratic Athens remembered them as the Tyrannicides, the liberators of the city. Their descendants received special honours, and drinking songs celebrated them. A bronze statue group of the two by the sculptor Antenor was set up in the Agora; when the Persians carried it off in 480 BC, the Athenians commissioned a replacement from Kritios and Nesiotes in 477/6 BC. Alexander the Great later reportedly sent the original back from Persia.

The Kritios and Nesiotes group is known from Roman copies, the best in Naples, showing the two striding forward to strike. It became a symbol of resistance to tyranny.
