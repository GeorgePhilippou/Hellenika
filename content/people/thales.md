<!-- field: summary -->
The first Greek to explain nature without invoking the gods — traditionally the first philosopher and first scientist.

<!-- field: significance -->
Begins the Ionian tradition of natural explanation from which Greek science, and eventually European science, descends.

<!-- field: body -->
Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BC) lived in the wealthy Ionian city of Miletus, on the coast of Asia Minor, and was counted by later Greeks among the Seven Sages and as the first philosopher. He wrote nothing that survives, and what is known of him comes from later writers, above all Aristotle and Herodotus.

He held that everything comes from water, and that the Earth floats on water. The answer is wrong, but the question was new: asking what the world is made of, and answering in terms of natural materials rather than stories about gods, is the founding step of Greek natural philosophy. His successors at Miletus, Anaximander and Anaximenes, carried the enquiry on.

Herodotus says Thales predicted a solar eclipse that halted a battle between the Lydians and the Medes; astronomers date such an eclipse to 28 May 585 BC. How he could have predicted it is unclear. Other stories credit him with measuring the height of the Egyptian pyramids from their shadows and with geometrical theorems, and Aristotle tells how he made a fortune by renting all the olive presses before a good harvest, to show that philosophers could be rich if they wished.
