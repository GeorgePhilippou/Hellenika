<!-- field: summary -->
King of Pontus who mounted the last great challenge to Rome in the Greek East, ordering the massacre of tens of thousands of Romans in one day.

<!-- field: significance -->
Fought Rome for four decades across three wars, and his defeat completed Roman control of Anatolia and the Aegean.

<!-- field: body -->
In 88 BC Mithridates orchestrated the Asiatic Vespers: a coordinated massacre of Roman and Italian residents across the cities of Asia Minor, with ancient estimates of 80,000 killed in a single day. The figure is probably inflated; the coordination is not in doubt.

Many Greek cities, including Athens, sided with him against Roman tax farmers. Sulla besieged and sacked Athens in 86 BC in response, damaging the city severely.

Three wars followed. Pompey finally broke him in 66 BC. Cornered by his own son's rebellion, he tried to poison himself and failed — he had reportedly spent his life taking small doses of poisons to build immunity, which is the origin of the word *mithridatism* — and had a bodyguard kill him instead.

His self-presentation drew on both halves of his heritage: he traced his lineage back to the Persian Achaemenid kings on one side and to Alexander's Macedonian successors on the other, styling himself simultaneously as a legitimate Hellenistic monarch and as the rightful avenger of Greek and Persian grievances against Rome, a dual appeal that helped him rally support across a genuinely diverse set of Anatolian and Greek communities.
