<!-- field: summary -->
Excavator of Knossos, who named the Minoan civilisation and reconstructed its palace in painted concrete.

<!-- field: significance -->
Revealed Europe's earliest civilisation — and shaped how it is seen through reconstructions that are substantially his own interpretation.

<!-- field: body -->
Evans came to archaeology partly by inheritance: his father John Evans was a well-known prehistorian in his own right, whose work identifying worked flint tools alongside extinct animal bones had helped convince Victorian scholars that humanity was vastly older than a literal reading of the Bible allowed. The younger Evans initially made his own name as curator of Oxford's Ashmolean Museum and as a collector of small carved sealstones from Crete bearing an unknown script, a puzzle that drew him to the island years before he ever began digging, in the hope of finding wherever such a script had actually been written down at scale — a hope Schliemann had shared, and had even tried and failed to act on by attempting, unsuccessfully, to buy the Kephala mound himself.

Evans succeeded where Schliemann had not, purchasing the site and beginning excavation in 1900. Within weeks he had uncovered frescoes, storage magazines and clay tablets in two previously unknown scripts, which he would later name Linear A and Linear B. He named the civilisation he had found "Minoan" after the legendary king Minos, identified the sprawling, maze-like complex with the mythical labyrinth of the Minotaur, and devised the Early, Middle and Late Minoan chronological scheme, subdivided further with Roman numerals, that archaeologists studying Bronze Age Crete still use as their basic framework today.

His "reconstitutions" used reinforced concrete, a strikingly modern material for a Bronze Age site, to rebuild columns, staircases and upper storeys as he imagined them, and he commissioned the Swiss father-and-son artists Émile and Émile Gilliéron to restore surviving frescoes from fragments — in some well-known cases, from very few fragments indeed. The famous "Prince of the Lilies" fresco, for instance, combines pieces that may not belong to a single figure at all, or even to figures of the same sex, and much of what tourists now see painted on the walls at Knossos is closer to an early twentieth-century artist's best guess than to any securely recovered Bronze Age original.

Against that necessary caveat, Evans excavated with real care by the standards of his day, published his findings in exhaustive detail across four large volumes, and used his own considerable private wealth to purchase and preserve the site rather than let it be lost to looting or development. He was knighted in 1911 for the discovery. He also insisted, correctly and against most contemporary opinion, that the Linear B tablets were administrative records rather than religious texts — while stubbornly and, as it turned out, wrongly refusing to the end of his life to accept that the language they recorded could possibly be an early form of Greek, a conclusion Michael Ventris would only reach a decade after Evans's own death.
