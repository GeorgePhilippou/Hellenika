<!-- field: body -->
Sir Arthur Evans (1851–1941) was the English archaeologist who excavated Knossos on Crete and named the Minoan civilisation. Son of the prehistorian John Evans, he was keeper of the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford when he became fascinated by small engraved sealstones from Crete carrying an unknown script, and went to the island hoping to find where that script was written.

In 1900 he bought the site of Knossos and began excavating. Within weeks he found the palace, its frescoes and storerooms, and thousands of clay tablets in two unknown scripts, which he named Linear A and Linear B. He called the civilisation "Minoan", after the legendary King Minos, linked the maze of rooms to the myth of the labyrinth, and devised the Early, Middle and Late Minoan system of dating that is still used today. He published his work in the four volumes of *The Palace of Minos* and was knighted in 1911.

His legacy is mixed. In the 1920s he rebuilt parts of the palace in reinforced concrete, and had the artists Émile Gilliéron and his son restore frescoes from fragments, sometimes very few. Much of what visitors see is his reconstruction, shaped by his picture of a peaceful, sophisticated society ruled by priest-kings.

He was right that the Linear B tablets were administrative records, but refused to accept that their language could be Greek. Michael Ventris showed that it was in 1952, eleven years after Evans's death.
