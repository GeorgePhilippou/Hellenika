<!-- field: summary -->
One of Alexander's bodyguards who built a kingdom out of Thrace and western Asia Minor, and was the last of the original successors to die in battle.

<!-- field: significance -->
His death at Corupedium in 281 BC removed the last of Alexander's original companions from the board, closing the first, most violent phase of the wars over the empire he had left behind.

<!-- field: body -->
Lysimachus received Thrace in the division of satrapies after Alexander's death — a difficult, thinly Hellenised assignment on the empire's edge that he spent years subduing rather than a ready-made kingdom. He built up strength steadily rather than dominating events early, and joined the coalition of Ptolemy, Seleucus and Cassander against Antigonus, fighting at Ipsus in 301 BC, where Antigonus was killed and Lysimachus took a large share of Asia Minor as his reward.

His later reign was troubled by his own family: he had his son by an earlier marriage, Agathocles, executed on the accusation of his second wife Arsinoe, a decision that alienated many of his own supporters and pushed some toward his rival Seleucus, the last of the old guard still standing against him.

The two men who had fought together at Ipsus met as enemies at Corupedium in 281 BC. Lysimachus was killed in the battle, and Seleucus, briefly master of nearly all of Alexander's former territory, was assassinated within months — leaving the settled three-kingdom map of Ptolemies, Seleucids and Antigonids that defined the Hellenistic world for the next two centuries.
