<!-- field: summary -->
Pythagorean statesman and mathematician who ruled Taras, solved the doubling of the cube, and was a friend of Plato.

<!-- field: significance -->
Archytas shows the Pythagorean tradition in its most rigorous form, and is the model of the philosopher in politics that Plato admired.

<!-- field: body -->
Archytas lived in Taras, the Spartan colony in southern Italy, and led it in the first half of the fourth century BC. Diogenes Laertius says he was chosen as general seven times, in defiance of a law that barred anyone from holding the office more than a year, and was never defeated.

As a mathematician he is the most important figure between the early Pythagoreans and Euclid. He found a solution to the problem of doubling the cube, which asks for the edge of a cube with twice the volume of a given one, by finding the intersection of three surfaces. It is an ingenious construction in three dimensions and was much admired. He also studied harmonic ratios and proved, according to Boethius, that no mean proportional can exist between numbers in a superparticular ratio, a result important for the theory of musical intervals.

Plato knew him and visited Taras on his trip to Italy. The Seventh Letter, whose authenticity is debated, says that Archytas sent a ship to bring Plato away from Syracuse when the tyrant Dionysius II held him. A late Roman author reports a wooden dove that could fly, powered by compressed air. It is best treated as a legend that grew around a well-known engineer.

Only fragments of his writings survive, some of which may be authentic. His importance lies in the combination of political leadership, mathematical originality and musical theory, which made him for later writers the ideal Pythagorean.
