<!-- field: summary -->
The last tyrant of Athens, son of Peisistratus, expelled in 510 BC, who later guided the Persians to Marathon.

<!-- field: significance -->
His fall in 510 BC opened the way to Athenian democracy; his return with the Persians in 490 BC made Marathon a fight for the democracy's survival.

<!-- field: body -->
Hippias (c. 570–490 BC) was the eldest son of Peisistratus, tyrant of Athens, and succeeded him in 527 BC, ruling with his brother Hipparchus. At first the brothers continued their father's moderate rule, keeping the laws, patronising poets and building temples.

In 514 BC Hipparchus was murdered at the Panathenaic festival by Harmodius and Aristogeiton, in a plot that Thucydides says grew from a personal quarrel. Hippias survived and became suspicious and harsh, executing and exiling opponents. The exiled Alcmaeonid family, with the backing of the Delphic oracle, persuaded Sparta to intervene. In 510 BC King Cleomenes I besieged Hippias on the Acropolis; when his children were captured trying to escape, he surrendered and left Athens. Within three years Cleisthenes had founded the democracy.

Hippias went to Sigeion in the Troad and then to the Persian court, hoping to return to power. In 490 BC, now old, he sailed with the Persian expedition against Athens and, Herodotus says, chose Marathon as the landing place. The Athenian victory there ended his hopes; he died soon after, perhaps on the way back to Asia.

His career shows how tyranny ended in Athens, and how closely the new democracy's survival was tied to the Persian Wars.
