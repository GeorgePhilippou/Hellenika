<!-- field: summary -->
Son of Antigonus I, celebrated as history's greatest besieger of cities despite failing to take the one siege he is best remembered for.

<!-- field: significance -->
His naval victory at Cyprian Salamis triggered the wave of royal title-taking among the successors that made the Hellenistic kingdoms formally, as well as practically, independent monarchies; his failed siege of Rhodes indirectly produced one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

<!-- field: body -->
Demetrius I Poliorcetes (c. 337–283 BC) was the son of Antigonus I, one of Alexander's leading successors, and a dashing commander on land and sea.

In 307 BC he "liberated" Athens from Cassander's governor and was hailed there almost as a god. In 306 BC he destroyed Ptolemy's fleet off Salamis in Cyprus, and his father took the title of king for them both — the first of Alexander's successors to do so. In 305–304 BC he besieged Rhodes with huge machines, including the nine-storey siege tower called the Helepolis, "Taker of Cities". The city held out and he withdrew; the Rhodians sold his abandoned equipment to help pay for the Colossus of Rhodes. The siege earned him the name Poliorcetes, "the Besieger".

At Ipsus in 301 BC his cavalry charge carried him too far from the battle, and his father was killed. Demetrius kept a fleet and some cities, and in 294 BC seized the throne of Macedon. He ruled it for six years before Pyrrhus and Lysimachus drove him out in 288 BC. After a last campaign in Asia Minor he surrendered to Seleucus in 285 BC and died in comfortable captivity two years later.

His marriages, including to Phila, daughter of the regent Antipater, served his alliances. His son Antigonus II Gonatas won Macedon back, and the Antigonid dynasty ruled it until the Roman conquest.
