<!-- field: summary -->
Brilliant, treacherous Athenian aristocrat who commanded for Athens, defected to Sparta, advised Persia, and returned to Athens again.

<!-- field: significance -->
His career is the clearest single illustration of how personal ambition destabilised Athenian democracy during the Peloponnesian War.

<!-- field: body -->
Alcibiades lost his father young and was raised afterward in the household of his guardian Pericles himself, giving him an unusually privileged front-row education in Athenian politics and power from childhood, alongside a personal charisma and good looks ancient sources dwell on repeatedly as central to his entire career. He pushed Athens into the enormously ambitious Sicilian Expedition in 415 BC against more cautious advice. On the eve of the fleet's departure, the city's sacred herms — boundary markers topped with the god Hermes's head — were mutilated overnight in an act of sacrilege that badly unsettled the city; Alcibiades was implicated, recalled from the fleet already at sea to stand trial, and rather than return home to face charges that could easily prove fatal, defected outright to Sparta instead, where he advised the Spartans to permanently fortify Decelea inside Attica itself, a base that harassed Athenian territory continuously for the rest of the war and did the Athenian economy enormous, lasting harm.

He then fell out with his Spartan hosts as well, reportedly after an affair with a Spartan king's wife, and moved on to the Persian satrap Tissaphernes, before eventually negotiating his own dramatic return to Athenian favour and command, winning a string of real naval victories in the Hellespont region that briefly restored Athenian fortunes late in the war. A subordinate's unauthorised defeat during his absence, however, gave his political enemies at home the pretext they needed to remove him from command a second time. He was assassinated in Phrygia in 404 BC, reportedly on Spartan or Persian instigation, his house set on fire and its occupant cut down as he tried to escape.

He was also a close associate of Socrates, who by his own later account saved Alcibiades's life in battle at Potidaea. Plato's *Symposium* gives him a famous, half-drunken speech of genuine, unrequited love and admiration for Socrates delivered at a private dinner party, and this well-known personal association between the philosopher and a man many Athenians blamed for the city's wartime disasters counted materially against Socrates at his own trial years later.
