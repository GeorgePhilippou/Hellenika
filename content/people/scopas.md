<!-- field: summary -->
The sculptor of Paros in the 4th century BC who brought passion and emotion into Greek sculpture.

<!-- field: significance -->
With Praxiteles and Lysippos, one of the three great sculptors of the late Classical age, and a forerunner of Hellenistic drama in art.

<!-- field: body -->
Scopas came from the island of Paros, famous for its marble, and worked across the Greek world in the first half of the 4th century BC. None of his works is securely identified, but ancient writers describe many, and some sculptures from buildings he worked on survive.

He was an architect as well as a sculptor: Pausanias says he designed the temple of Athena Alea at Tegea in Arcadia, and fragments of its pediment sculptures, with deep-set eyes and heads thrown back in strain, are the best guide to his style. Pliny the Elder says he was one of four great sculptors who each carved one side of the Mausoleum of Halicarnassus.

Where Praxiteles was known for grace and calm, Scopas was known for passion. Ancient writers praised his raging Maenad, a follower of Dionysus in ecstatic frenzy, her head flung back and body twisting; a small Roman copy survives in Dresden. His figures expressed intense feeling through the face, with deep-set eyes and parted lips. His style influenced the dramatic sculpture of the Hellenistic age.
