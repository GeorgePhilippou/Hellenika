<!-- field: body -->
Anaximenes (c. 586–526 BC) was a philosopher of Miletus, in Ionia, the third of the Milesian thinkers after Thales and Anaximander, and probably Anaximander's pupil. His writings are lost and known through later summaries.

Like his predecessors he looked for the single basic substance (*arche*) behind the variety of the world. Where Anaximander had proposed an abstract "boundless", Anaximenes chose something familiar: air. His real innovation was a mechanism. Air thins (rarefies) into fire, and thickens (condenses) by stages into wind, cloud, water, earth and stone. The same substance at different densities becomes everything else. This was the first attempt to explain change by a single physical process rather than simply naming a starting material.

He compared the air that holds the world together to the breath (*psyche*) that holds a living body together. He gave natural explanations of earthquakes, lightning and rainbows, and described the Earth as flat, floating on air, with the Sun, Moon and stars as fiery bodies carried around it.

Later Greek writers saw the three Milesians — Thales' water, Anaximander's boundless and Anaximenes' air — as a developing sequence, each answering problems in the last. The idea of explaining the world by condensation and rarefaction of one substance influenced later thinkers even when they rejected air as the answer.
