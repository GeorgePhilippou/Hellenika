<!-- field: summary -->
The Spartan poet of the 7th century BC whose war songs urged citizens to stand and die in the front ranks.

<!-- field: significance -->
The voice of the Spartan ideal of courage, and key early evidence for hoplite warfare and the Spartan constitution.

<!-- field: body -->
Tyrtaeus lived at Sparta in the mid-7th century BC, during the Second Messenian War, when the Messenians rose against Spartan rule. His poems, in elegiac couplets, survive in substantial fragments, mostly quoted by later writers who admired their message.

They urge young Spartans to fight bravely: "It is a fine thing for a brave man to die, falling in the front ranks, fighting for his country." He describes men standing foot to foot, shield pressed against shield, crest against crest — one of the earliest accounts of fighting in a close formation of hoplites. He contrasts the glory of the fallen warrior, honoured by the whole city, with the shame of the coward, and insists that courage, not wealth or athletic skill, is the true measure of a man.

Another poem, the *Eunomia* ("Good Order"), refers to the Spartan constitution granted by Apollo's oracle, with roles for the kings, the elders and the people — an early source for the system later credited to Lycurgus.

Later Athenians claimed Tyrtaeus was a lame Athenian schoolmaster sent to Sparta on the oracle's instructions, a story usually dismissed. Spartan soldiers sang his songs on campaign for centuries.
