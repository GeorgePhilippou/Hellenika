<!-- field: summary -->
Eleatic philosopher whose paradoxes of motion and plurality set a problem about infinity that took two thousand years to resolve.

<!-- field: significance -->
Zeno showed that everyday ideas about space, time and motion lead to contradictions, and forced later thinkers to say more precisely what they meant.

<!-- field: body -->
Zeno (c. 490–430 BC) was a citizen of Elea in southern Italy and a follower of Parmenides. According to Plato, he wrote a book in his youth to defend Parmenides against those who mocked the claim that reality is one. If there are many things, Zeno argued, the consequences are even more absurd than those of monism.

Aristotle preserves the four best-known arguments against motion. In the dichotomy, to reach any goal one must first go half of the way, then half of what remains, and so an infinite number of tasks must be completed. In the Achilles, the fastest runner can never overtake a tortoise with a head start, since he must first reach where it was, by which time it has moved on. In the arrow, a flying arrow occupies at each instant a space equal to itself, and so is at rest at every instant. In the stadium, the same distance seems to take both a time and its double. Aristotle answered them, and later thinkers used Zeno's arguments to press questions about infinity and continuity.

The paradoxes were not intended to prove that motion is impossible for its own sake. They tested the assumption that space and time are infinitely divisible. Mathematicians in the nineteenth century, with the theory of limits, supplied a way to sum an infinite series to a finite value, and many scholars think this answers the first two paradoxes; the arrow and stadium raise more debated questions about the nature of instants.

The ancient biographical tradition reports that Zeno died opposing a tyrant, though the tyrant's name and the manner of his death vary in every account. It is best treated as a legend shaped by the image of the philosopher as a man of courage.
