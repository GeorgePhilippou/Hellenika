<!-- field: summary -->
Achaemenid king who led the greatest invasion of Greece in 480 BC, burned Athens, and lost his fleet at Salamis.

<!-- field: significance -->
His defeat preserved Greek independence at the moment when Classical Athens was about to emerge.

<!-- field: body -->
Xerxes assembled an army and fleet of unprecedented size, bridged the Hellespont with pontoons of lashed ships, and cut a canal through the Athos peninsula — a feat Greeks thought boastful and which modern geophysical survey has confirmed was actually built.

After Thermopylae he took Athens and burned the Acropolis. Then at Salamis the Greek fleet, fighting in narrows chosen by Themistocles, destroyed his naval superiority. He returned to Asia, leaving Mardonius to be defeated at Plataea in 479 BC.

Herodotus portrays him as a study in hubris — whipping the sea for wrecking his bridge — but Persian sources present a conventional, effective king who completed Persepolis. He was assassinated in a palace conspiracy in 465 BC.
