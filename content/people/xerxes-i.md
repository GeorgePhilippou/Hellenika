<!-- field: summary -->
Achaemenid king who led the greatest invasion of Greece in 480 BC, burned Athens, and lost his fleet at Salamis.

<!-- field: significance -->
His defeat preserved Greek independence at the moment when Classical Athens was about to emerge.

<!-- field: body -->
Xerxes I was king of the Persian Empire from 486 to 465 BC. He was the son of Darius I and Atossa, a daughter of Cyrus the Great, which made him the favoured heir over his older half-brothers. He began his reign by crushing revolts in Egypt and Babylon.

In 480 BC he led the largest invasion Greece had ever seen, to complete the punishment of Athens his father had begun. His engineers cut a canal through the Athos peninsula and bridged the Hellespont with boats. Herodotus says that when a storm broke the first bridge, Xerxes had the sea whipped — a story told to show royal arrogance. Herodotus gives his army over five million people; modern estimates are closer to 200,000, still an enormous force.

After breaking through the pass of Thermopylae, where Leonidas and his Spartans died, Xerxes occupied and burned Athens, whose people had left for Salamis and other places. But in the straits of Salamis, chosen by Themistocles, his fleet was badly defeated while he watched from the shore. He returned to Asia, leaving his general Mardonius, who was defeated and killed at Plataea in 479 BC.

Xerxes spent his later years completing his father's buildings at Persepolis, including the Gate of All Nations and the Hall of a Hundred Columns. In 465 BC he was murdered in a palace plot, and his son Artaxerxes I took the throne. To Greek writers he became the classic image of an arrogant eastern despot; to Persia, his reign was one of continued imperial strength.
