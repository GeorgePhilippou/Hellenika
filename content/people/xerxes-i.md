<!-- field: summary -->
Achaemenid king who led the greatest invasion of Greece in 480 BC, burned Athens, and lost his fleet at Salamis.

<!-- field: significance -->
His defeat preserved Greek independence at the moment when Classical Athens was about to emerge.

<!-- field: body -->
Xerxes was Darius I's son by Atossa, daughter of Cyrus the Great, a lineage that in Persian court terms made him a more legitimate heir than any of his older half-brothers born before Darius took the throne. He spent his first two years as king suppressing revolts in Egypt and Babylon rather than looking toward Greece at all, and Herodotus records a long council scene before the invasion in which his uncle Artabanus argued strenuously against it, warning that both land and sea would prove too vast for any army to control — advice Xerxes initially heeded, reversed after a recurring dream he took as divine insistence, and only then committed to war.

The scale of preparation was, even by Herodotus's admittedly inflated numbers, enormous: a canal cut through the Athos peninsula so the fleet would not have to round the promontory where a previous Persian fleet had wrecked, and a double pontoon bridge of lashed ships across the Hellespont at Abydos. When a storm destroyed the first bridge, Xerxes is said to have had the strait whipped three hundred times, branded with hot irons, and cursed as "a turbid and briny river," while the engineers responsible were beheaded — a story Herodotus tells as a set-piece illustration of royal hubris rather than sober reportage, though it captures how Greek writers wanted this king remembered. Modern estimates put the actual invasion force at somewhere around 200,000, still the largest army yet assembled for a single campaign, against Herodotus's claim of over five million combatants and camp followers combined.

After the costly but successful stand at Thermopylae, where a local shepherd named Ephialtes showed his forces a mountain path around Leonidas's position, Xerxes occupied and burned Athens, whose citizens had already evacuated to Salamis and Troezen. He reportedly had a throne set up on a hillside overlooking the strait of Salamis to watch his fleet destroy the Greeks, and instead watched it destroyed, undone by the narrows Themistocles had chosen precisely to neutralise Persian numbers. Xerxes returned to Asia with much of the army, leaving his general Mardonius to be killed at Plataea the following year.

He spent his remaining decade completing his father's building projects at Persepolis, adding the Hall of a Hundred Columns and the Gate of All Nations, before being assassinated in 465 BC in a palace conspiracy led by the courtier Artabanus and the eunuch Aspamitres, who briefly placed a younger son on the throne before Xerxes' surviving son Artaxerxes I had the killers themselves put to death.
