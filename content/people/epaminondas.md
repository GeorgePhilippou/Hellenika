<!-- field: summary -->
Theban general who broke Spartan military supremacy at Leuctra in 371 BC and freed the Messenian helots.

<!-- field: significance -->
Ended Sparta as a great power permanently, and pioneered the tactical asymmetry that Philip II — a hostage in Thebes as a youth — would later develop.

<!-- field: body -->
At Leuctra, Epaminondas massed his left wing fifty ranks deep against the elite Spartan right, deliberately held back the rest of his own line at an angle, and shattered the best Spartan troops before the weaker Theban centre and right had even fully engaged. It was the first clear, deliberate use of an unequal battle line anywhere in Greek warfare, and roughly 400 of the 700 Spartiates present were killed in the process — a demographic wound the already-shrinking Spartan citizen body simply could not absorb or replace.

He then invaded the Peloponnese and carried out what proved to be the single most consequential act of the whole campaign: he liberated Messenia outright, whose enslaved helot population had farmed the land feeding the entire Spartan military system for roughly three centuries, and refounded its chief city, Messene, as a heavily fortified, independent city built with remarkable speed by a coalition of former helots and allied labour. Sparta, stripped permanently of the economic base its whole warrior society had depended on, never recovered its former power. Epaminondas was also celebrated by ancient writers for a personal incorruptibility unusual even by the standards they applied to other generals, reportedly living, and eventually dying, in genuine poverty despite commanding the dominant military power in Greece for most of a decade.

He was killed at Mantinea in 362 BC, struck down at the moment of another tactical victory, and Theban hegemony over Greece effectively died with him almost immediately afterward.
