<!-- field: summary -->
The Jewish philosopher of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–AD 50) who read the Greek Bible through Greek philosophy.

<!-- field: significance -->
The great meeting point of Judaism and Greek thought, whose ideas strongly influenced early Christian theology.

<!-- field: body -->
Philo (c. 20 BC–c. AD 50) belonged to one of the wealthiest Jewish families of Alexandria, then home to the largest Jewish community outside Judaea; his nephew Tiberius Julius Alexander became prefect of Egypt. He was steeped in Greek education and read the Bible in the Septuagint translation.

In dozens of treatises he interpreted the five books of Moses for Greek-educated readers. He used allegory to find philosophical meaning behind the stories: Adam is mind, Eve is the senses, and the patriarchs are stages of the soul's journey to God. He drew on Plato and the Stoics, and gave a central place to the *Logos*, the divine reason through which God created and governs the world — an idea close to the opening of John's Gospel.

In 38 riots broke out in Alexandria between Greeks and Jews, and the Jews lost their civic rights. In 39/40 Philo, by then elderly, led a Jewish embassy to the emperor Gaius in Rome, which he described in his *Embassy to Gaius*. Jewish tradition largely forgot him, but Christian writers such as Clement and Origen of Alexandria valued him, and they preserved his works.
