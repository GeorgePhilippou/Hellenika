<!-- field: summary -->
A mathematician of Roman Alexandria whose Arithmetica studied equations to be solved in rational numbers, and who is often called the father of algebra.

<!-- field: significance -->
His problems gave their name to Diophantine equations and inspired Fermat and modern number theory.

<!-- field: body -->
Almost nothing is known of Diophantus apart from his works. A late epigram, probably a later puzzle, says he lived for 84 years, but gives no dates. He is generally placed in the third century AD in Alexandria, though the second century is possible.

His chief work, the *Arithmetica*, was originally in thirteen books. Six survive in Greek and four more were found in an Arabic translation. It consists of a series of problems, each solved with a specific numerical method, that ask for rational numbers satisfying given conditions. He devised an abbreviated notation for the unknown and its powers, a step toward symbolic algebra.

The work was commented on by Hypatia and others, and was translated into Arabic. In 1621 Claude Bachet published a Latin edition, and Pierre de Fermat famously wrote his conjecture, now Fermat's Last Theorem, in the margin of his copy. Equations whose solutions are required to be whole numbers are now called Diophantine.
