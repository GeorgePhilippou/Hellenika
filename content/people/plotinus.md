<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek philosopher from Egypt (AD 204/5–270), founder of Neoplatonism, who taught that all things flow from a single source, the One.

<!-- field: significance -->
The last great original philosopher of antiquity, whose mystical Platonism shaped Christian, Islamic and Renaissance thought.

<!-- field: body -->
Plotinus (204/5–270) was probably born in Egypt, perhaps at Lycopolis. Our knowledge of him comes from the *Life* by his pupil Porphyry. At twenty-seven he turned to philosophy in Alexandria and studied for eleven years with the Platonist Ammonius Saccas. In 243 he joined the emperor Gordian III's campaign against Persia, hoping to learn the wisdom of the Persians and Indians, but the emperor was killed and he escaped to Antioch. From 245 he taught in Rome, where senators and the emperor Gallienus attended his lectures; he even proposed founding a city in Campania to be governed by Plato's laws.

He began writing only at fifty. After his death, Porphyry arranged his fifty-four treatises into six books of nine, the *Enneads*.

Plotinus saw himself as an interpreter of Plato. He taught that all reality flows from a first principle, the One or the Good, which is beyond being and thought. From it comes Intellect, containing the Forms, and from Intellect the Soul, which gives life to the material world. The human soul can return to its source through philosophy, virtue and contemplation; Porphyry says Plotinus experienced union with the One four times while he knew him. He died in Campania in 270.
