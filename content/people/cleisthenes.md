<!-- field: summary -->
Architect of the Athenian democratic constitution in 508/7 BC, who rebuilt the citizen body from scratch to break the power of regional clans.

<!-- field: significance -->
Created the institutional machinery — ten artificial tribes, the Council of 500, the deme registry — that made mass self-government workable.

<!-- field: body -->
Cleisthenes himself belonged to the powerful, wealthy Alcmaeonid family, and his reforms emerged directly out of an aristocratic power struggle rather than any abstract commitment to equality: finding himself losing a factional contest against a rival noble, Isagoras, he turned to the ordinary Athenian populace for support, promising sweeping changes in exchange for their backing — a calculated political gamble that happened to produce a genuinely transformative constitution. Shortly before this, he and his family are also credited with helping secure Spartan military support and, according to tradition, the Delphic oracle's persistent pressure, in finally expelling the tyrant Hippias from Athens, clearing the way for the reforms that followed.

His central move was ingenious social engineering. He divided Attica into three zones — city, coast and inland — split each into thirty units called *trittyes*, and built ten new artificial tribes by combining exactly one trittys from each of the three zones. Every tribe therefore contained citizens drawn from across the whole of Attica with no pre-existing shared local loyalty, which deliberately severed the traditional link between old aristocratic families and their regional power bases, since no single tribe could any longer be dominated by one clan's local following.

Citizenship was now registered through the *deme*, an individual's local village or city ward, and a man's demotic — his deme name — replaced his father's name in official public use, replacing inherited lineage with residence as the primary mark of civic identity. The new Council of 500, with fifty members chosen by lot from each of the ten tribes, set the agenda for the full citizen assembly and became the practical administrative backbone of the entire Athenian democratic system for the following two centuries.
