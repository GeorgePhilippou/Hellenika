The mathematician and philosopher of Alexandria murdered by a Christian mob in AD 415.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous woman scholar of antiquity, whose murder became a symbol of the end of the classical world.

<!-- field: body -->
Hypatia (c. 355–415) was the daughter of Theon, a mathematician and astronomer of Alexandria who edited Euclid's *Elements* and wrote commentaries on Ptolemy. She surpassed him, teaching mathematics, astronomy and the philosophy of Plato and Plotinus to students who came from across the eastern Mediterranean, both pagan and Christian. One of them, Synesius, later bishop of Cyrene, wrote her letters asking advice on building an astrolabe and a hydrometer. Ancient writers praise her learning, her eloquence and her self-control; she never married.

Her writings, including commentaries on the *Arithmetica* of Diophantus and the *Conics* of Apollonius, do not survive, though she may have revised part of her father's commentary on the *Almagest*.

She was respected by the city's leaders and was an adviser to the Roman prefect Orestes, who was in bitter conflict with the bishop Cyril. In March 415 rumours spread that she was preventing their reconciliation. A Christian mob led by a church reader named Peter seized her in the street, dragged her into a church, and killed her, tearing her body apart with pottery shards. The church historian Socrates Scholasticus condemned the murder. Her death became a symbol, often romanticised, of the clash between Christianity and classical learning.
