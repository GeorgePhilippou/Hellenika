<!-- field: summary -->
The lyric poet of Teos who sang of wine and love at the courts of tyrants in Samos and Athens in the 6th century BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The poet of the symposium par excellence, whose light, graceful songs of wine and love were imitated for two thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Anacreon (c. 570–485 BC) was born at Teos in Ionia. When the Persians conquered Ionia around 545 BC, its people moved to found Abdera in Thrace. Anacreon later became a poet at the court of Polycrates, the tyrant of Samos. After Polycrates was killed around 522 BC, Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, brought him to Athens in a fifty-oared ship. He may have spent his last years in Thessaly, and was said to have died aged 85, choking on a grape seed — a legend fitting a poet of wine.

His poems, written in Ionic Greek for singing at the symposium, are short, witty and elegant, about love for boys and girls, wine, music and the approach of old age. He compares a girl to a Thracian filly who has not yet been broken, and jokingly complains that a girl from Lesbos prefers someone else. The tone is playful rather than intense.

He was hugely popular in Athens, where he appears on vases and a statue of him stood on the Acropolis.

In later antiquity a collection of light poems in his style, the *Anacreontea*, was composed. Rediscovered in the Renaissance, they inspired generations of "Anacreontic" verse about wine and love, and the drinking song "To Anacreon in Heaven" lent its tune to "The Star-Spangled Banner".
