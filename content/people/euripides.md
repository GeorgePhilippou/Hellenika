The most unsettling of the tragedians, who gave voice to women, slaves and the defeated, and questioned the gods openly.

His psychological realism and moral scepticism made him the least successful tragedian in his lifetime and the most read afterwards.

<!-- field: body -->
Euripides (c. 480–406 BC) was the youngest of the three great Athenian tragedians, by tradition born on the island of Salamis in the year of the battle. He wrote around ninety plays and won first prize only four or five times, but nineteen plays survive — more than those of Aeschylus and Sophocles combined — because later readers valued him so highly.

His plays are full of psychological realism, clever argument and doubts about the gods. He gave strong voices to women, slaves and the defeated. *Medea* (431 BC) has a betrayed wife kill her own children and escape unpunished. *Hippolytus*, *Electra* and *The Bacchae* show passion and religious frenzy destroying their victims. *The Trojan Women* (415 BC), staged the year after Athens massacred the people of Melos, shows the sack of Troy through the eyes of its enslaved women, and is often read as a protest against the war.

He was a constant target of the comic poet Aristophanes, who in *Frogs* (405 BC) staged a contest in the underworld between Euripides and Aeschylus over which was the better poet.

Late in life Euripides left Athens for the court of King Archelaus of Macedon at Pella, where he wrote *The Bacchae*, and he died there around 406 BC. His influence on later drama, from Menander to Seneca and beyond, was greater than that of any other Greek tragedian.
