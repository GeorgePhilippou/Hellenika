<!-- field: summary -->
The legendary lawgiver to whom Spartans attributed their entire social and military system.

<!-- field: significance -->
Whether or not he existed, "Lycurgus" is how Spartans explained and legitimised the most distinctive society in Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Plutarch opens his Life by admitting that nothing about Lycurgus can be stated without contradiction — not his dates, his family, or what he did. Ancient estimates of his lifetime span four centuries.

The system credited to him — the *agoge* education, communal messes, the ban on gold and silver coinage, land divided into equal lots, the *krypteia* — was almost certainly assembled over generations. Attributing it to one inspired figure endorsed by Delphi gave it an authority no gradual process could. This is a case where the historical question ("did he exist?") matters less than the functional one ("what did believing in him do?").

The traditional account places Lycurgus's reforms at a moment of acute internal crisis, with Sparta supposedly riven by lawlessness and factional conflict that the new constitution, sanctioned by Delphi, was designed specifically to end — a narrative structure common to Greek lawgiver traditions generally, in which a single wise figure resolves a period of civic breakdown through comprehensive, divinely approved legislation.
