<!-- field: summary -->
The legendary lawgiver to whom Spartans attributed their entire social and military system.

<!-- field: significance -->
Whether or not he existed, "Lycurgus" is how Spartans explained and legitimised the most distinctive society in Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Lycurgus was the lawgiver to whom the Spartans credited their whole way of life. Ancient writers placed him anywhere between the 11th and 8th centuries BC, and Plutarch begins his biography by admitting that nothing about him can be stated without dispute — not his dates, his family or his deeds. Many historians doubt that he existed at all.

Tradition said he had travelled to Crete, Ionia and Egypt, and that his reforms, approved by the oracle at Delphi, ended a period of lawlessness in Sparta. The "Great Rhetra", an oracle-based statement of the constitution quoted by Plutarch, set out the roles of the two kings, the council of elders and the assembly. He was also credited with the *agoge*, the state training of boys; the common messes in which citizens ate; the division of land into equal lots; the ban on gold and silver money; and simple living for all citizens.

In reality this system was almost certainly built up over generations, much of it in the 7th and 6th centuries BC after Sparta's conquest of Messenia. Crediting it all to one wise founder backed by Apollo gave it an authority that gradual change could not.

Lycurgus was worshipped as a hero in Sparta. For later Greek and Roman writers, from Xenophon to Plutarch, he became the model of the wise lawgiver who shapes a whole society.
