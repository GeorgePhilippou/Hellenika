<!-- field: summary -->
The architect who deciphered Linear B in 1952, proving that Mycenaean Greeks wrote Greek five centuries before Homer.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the great intellectual achievements of the twentieth century, and the single most important advance in Aegean prehistory.

<!-- field: body -->
Michael Ventris (1922–1956) was an English architect who deciphered Linear B, the script of the Mycenaean palaces. Gifted at languages from childhood, he heard Arthur Evans lecture in 1936, when he was fourteen, and asked whether the Cretan tablets had ever been read. They had not, and the problem stayed with him. After serving as an RAF navigator in the Second World War, he trained and worked as an architect, working on Linear B in his own time.

He built on the work of the American scholar Alice Kober, who before her death in 1950 had shown that Linear B words changed their endings in regular patterns. Ventris arranged the signs in a grid by shared consonants and vowels, without assuming any sound values, and circulated his working notes to other scholars. In 1952 he tried reading certain words found only at Knossos as Cretan place names — Amnisos, Knossos, Tulissos. The readings worked, and they kept working across other tablets: the language was Greek. He had expected it to be related to Etruscan, and at first resisted his own result.

He announced the decipherment on BBC radio in 1952, and with the Cambridge philologist John Chadwick wrote *Documents in Mycenaean Greek*. A tablet from Pylos found in 1952 and published in 1953, listing tripods beside pictures of them, confirmed the decipherment.

Ventris was killed in a car crash in 1956, aged 34, weeks before the book appeared. His work pushed written Greek back by five centuries.
