<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek biographer and philosopher of the Roman age, author of the Parallel Lives, through whom later Europe came to know the ancient world.

<!-- field: significance -->
Perhaps the most influential ancient writer on later ideas of Greek and Roman history and character, from Shakespeare to the American founders.

<!-- field: body -->
Plutarch (c. AD 46–after 119) was born into a wealthy family at Chaeronea in Boeotia, the town where Philip II had defeated the Greeks in 338 BC. He studied philosophy at Athens, travelled to Egypt and Rome, and gained Roman citizenship, but chose to spend most of his life in his small home town, serving it in local offices. For the last thirty years of his life he was also one of the two priests of Apollo at Delphi.

He wrote prolifically. His *Parallel Lives* are biographies of famous Greeks and Romans, mostly in pairs — Alexander and Caesar, Demosthenes and Cicero, Pericles and Fabius — written to show character and moral example. Writing centuries after his subjects, he drew on many sources now lost, so his Lives are often the fullest surviving account of figures such as Lycurgus, Solon, Themistocles and Pericles. His *Moralia*, more than seventy essays and dialogues, range over ethics, religion, politics, education and much else, including the decline of the oracles.

Plutarch is not a critical historian: he collects anecdotes and moralises. But his vivid storytelling made him one of the most widely read ancient authors. In translation, his Lives shaped Renaissance and Enlightenment views of antiquity; Shakespeare took the plots of his Roman plays from them, and the leaders of the American and French revolutions read him as a guide to civic virtue.
