<!-- field: summary -->
Theban leader who freed his city from Spartan occupation in 379 BC and helped to make it, briefly, the strongest state in Greece.

<!-- field: significance -->
Pelopidas was the political and military partner of Epaminondas, and their cooperation is the foundation of Theban hegemony.

<!-- field: body -->
Pelopidas came from a wealthy Theban family, and after Sparta seized the Cadmea, Thebes's citadel, in 382 BC, he went into exile at Athens. In December 379, he and a few companions slipped back into the city in disguise, killed the leaders of the Spartan party at a banquet, and rallied the citizens. The Spartan garrison, though sizeable, surrendered within days. Thebes was free, and the Boeotian league could be rebuilt around it.

He then took command of the Sacred Band, a corps of trained infantry, which he led at Tegyra in 375 BC to a victory that shocked Sparta, since it was a defeat of a Spartan unit in a fair fight. At Leuctra in 371 he commanded the Band on the left of the line, where Epaminondas's deep column smashed the Spartan right. Pelopidas was more of a diplomat than Epaminondas. He was sent north to Thessaly and Macedon, where he arbitrated a succession dispute and brought back Philip as a hostage. In 367 he went to the Persian court and returned with the King's endorsement of Theban claims.

In 364 he took a small force against Alexander of Pherae, the tyrant who had imprisoned him earlier. His army won at Cynoscephalae in Thessaly, but Pelopidas, charging ahead to kill the tyrant, was killed himself. Thessalian cities honoured him with bronze statues.

Plutarch's life of Pelopidas is the main source, and it is a warm one.
