<!-- field: summary -->
The Alexandrian engineer of the 3rd century BC, inventor of the force pump and the water organ, and the father of pneumatics.

<!-- field: significance -->
The founder of the study of air and water pressure, whose inventions were used for over two thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Ctesibius (active c. 270 BC) was an engineer at Alexandria under Ptolemy II. Later writers say he was the son of a barber, and began by designing a mirror on a counterweight for his father's shop. His own writings are lost, but the Roman architect Vitruvius and the Greek engineers Philo of Byzantium and Hero of Alexandria describe his inventions.

He is regarded as the founder of pneumatics, the science of air and water pressure. He invented the force pump, with two cylinders, pistons and valves, used later as a fire engine and for raising water. He built the *hydraulis*, a water organ in which air was forced through pipes by water pressure — the ancestor of the pipe organ, played at Roman games and festivals. He improved the water clock (*klepsydra*) by keeping the water at a constant level so that it flowed evenly, and added gears to drive pointers and moving figures, making it the most accurate clock until the invention of the pendulum clock. He is also credited with a catapult powered by compressed air.

His work began a tradition of Alexandrian engineering continued by Philo and Hero.
