<!-- field: summary -->
Third head of the Lyceum, nicknamed "the Physicist", who explained the cosmos by natural causes and tested ideas about air and void.

<!-- field: significance -->
Strato moved the Peripatetic school away from Aristotle's purposes in nature towards an experimental, more mechanical science.

<!-- field: body -->
Strato came from Lampsacus on the Hellespont, studied under Theophrastus and took over the Lyceum after him, around 287 BC. Diogenes Laertius says he spent some years at Alexandria as tutor to the future Ptolemy II, and was well paid for it. He led the school until about 269.

His interest was in nature. He rejected the view that the world shows design, and argued that nature itself, with its heat and cold, weight and lightness, is enough to produce order. Cicero complained that he had "dismissed god from the task of making the world". He criticised Aristotle's claims that natural places pull bodies up and down, and argued that falling bodies accelerate.

The writer Hero of Alexandria reports, in his work on pneumatics, that Strato defended the existence of small voids between the particles of matter, and cited experiments with compressed air and with a vessel with a hole to show that air is a body that can be squeezed. The reports come from a much later writer, and scholars debate how far the experiments were Strato's own.

Strato's work is lost, although the titles list studies of motion, void, colour, animals and ethics. The Lyceum lost its vigour after his death, and later heads are hardly known.
