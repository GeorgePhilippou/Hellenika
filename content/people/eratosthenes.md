<!-- field: body -->
Eratosthenes (c. 276–194 BC) was a Greek scholar from Cyrene in Libya, who studied in Athens and around 245 BC was called to Alexandria by Ptolemy III to head the Library. He worked across mathematics, astronomy, geography, chronology and literary criticism.

His most famous achievement was measuring the Earth. He knew that at noon on the summer solstice the sun stood directly overhead at Syene (Aswan), in southern Egypt, while at Alexandria, to the north, an upright rod cast a shadow of about one-fiftieth of a circle. If the Earth is a sphere and the sun's rays are parallel, the distance between the two cities must be one-fiftieth of the Earth's circumference. He multiplied it by fifty and reached about 250,000 stades. Depending on which length of stade he used, the result is within a few per cent of the true figure.

He also drew one of the first world maps with a grid of parallels and meridians, coining the word "geography"; measured the tilt of the Earth's axis; compiled a chronology of Greek history from the fall of Troy, whose date of 1184 BC became standard; and devised the method for finding prime numbers still taught as the Sieve of Eratosthenes.

Rivals nicknamed him "Beta" — second best at everything — a joke that reflects how many fields he worked in.
