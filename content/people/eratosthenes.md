<!-- field: summary -->
Chief librarian at Alexandria who measured the circumference of the Earth using shadows, and effectively invented geography.

<!-- field: significance -->
Demonstrated that a global physical quantity could be determined by measurement and reasoning from a single location.

<!-- field: body -->
Eratosthenes knew, from reports reaching the Library, that at noon on the summer solstice the sun shone straight down a well at Syene (modern Aswan) in southern Egypt, casting no shadow at all. At Alexandria, considerably further north, on that same day and hour a vertical gnomon (a simple upright rod) cast a shadow corresponding to about 1/50th of a full circle, or 7.2 degrees. Reasoning that if the Earth is a sphere and the sun's rays reaching it are effectively parallel, then the north-south distance between the two cities must itself represent 1/50th of the Earth's total circumference, he simply multiplied the known road distance between them by 50 and arrived at a figure of roughly 250,000 stades for the whole planet.

How accurate that final figure actually is depends heavily on exactly which length of "stade" he was using, since several different regional standards existed in his period; on the most plausible value historians have proposed, his result comes out within about 2 percent of the true modern figure for Earth's circumference — a genuinely remarkable outcome given that his entire method rested on a rough measured road distance, a simple upright stick, and careful geometric reasoning rather than any specialised instrument.

He also produced one of the first true world maps built around a systematic grid of parallels and meridians, calculated the tilt of the Earth's axis (the obliquity of the ecliptic) with real precision, compiled a chronological table attempting to date major historical events all the way back to the fall of Troy, and devised the simple, elegant method for identifying prime numbers, the Sieve of Eratosthenes, still taught to students today under his own name. Rival scholars reportedly nicknamed him "Beta," the second letter of the Greek alphabet, implying he was only ever second-best in any given field he worked in — a jab that, given just how many different fields he worked in at a genuinely high level, reads today as a backhanded compliment more than a real insult.
