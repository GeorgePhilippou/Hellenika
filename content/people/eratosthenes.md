<!-- field: summary -->
Chief librarian at Alexandria who measured the circumference of the Earth using shadows, and effectively invented geography.

<!-- field: significance -->
Demonstrated that a global physical quantity could be determined by measurement and reasoning from a single location.

<!-- field: body -->
Eratosthenes knew that at noon on the summer solstice the sun shone straight down a well at Syene, casting no shadow. At Alexandria on the same day a vertical gnomon cast a shadow at about 1/50th of a circle — 7.2°. If the Earth is a sphere and the sun's rays effectively parallel, then the distance between the cities is 1/50th of the circumference. He multiplied by 50 and got roughly 250,000 stades.

How accurate that is depends on which stade he used; on the most likely value the error is under 2%. Either way the method is impeccable, and he was working with a measured road distance and a stick.

He also produced a world map with parallels and meridians, calculated the tilt of the ecliptic, and devised the sieve for finding prime numbers that still bears his name. Rivals nicknamed him "Beta" for being second-best at everything — which, given the range, was a compliment in disguise.
