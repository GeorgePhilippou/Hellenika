<!-- field: summary -->
German businessman who excavated Troy and Mycenae, proved the Bronze Age Aegean was real, and destroyed a great deal of it doing so.

<!-- field: significance -->
Founded Aegean prehistory as a field, and stands as its permanent cautionary tale about method and self-promotion.

<!-- field: body -->
Schliemann taught himself a striking number of languages, by his own account including English, French, Dutch, Spanish, Portuguese, Russian and eventually classical and modern Greek, using an intensive method of memorising and reciting long passages of text aloud — a discipline that served him well later, whatever the exaggerations in how he described his own early poverty and self-education. He made a substantial fortune first in the indigo trade and then during the Californian gold rush, acquiring American citizenship along the way under circumstances later scholars have found murky, before retiring from business to go looking for the real cities of Homer.

At Hisarlik from 1871 he dug a vast trench straight down through the mound, in the process destroying much of the layer that was actually the Homeric-era city, in order to reach a much older, more spectacular hoard of gold and jewellery he named "Priam's Treasure" after the Trojan king of the epic. Having divorced his first wife back in Russia, he had specifically sought a young Greek bride through a matchmaker, marrying the seventeen-year-old Sophia Engastromenos, whom he later claimed, almost certainly falsely, to have had present and photographed wearing the treasure's jewellery at the actual moment of its discovery.

At Mycenae in 1876 he found the shaft graves with their gold funerary masks, and telegraphed the Greek king that he had gazed upon the face of Agamemnon. He had not — the burials are roughly three centuries too early for any plausible Trojan War — but the discovery was genuinely epochal regardless: it proved for the first time that a rich, sophisticated Bronze Age civilisation really had existed on the Greek mainland, rather than being purely a poetic invention of later ages.

His published accounts are demonstrably unreliable in numerous places: he embellished discoveries, backdated some finds to more dramatic moments, and mixed genuine archaeology with self-promotion throughout his career. Much of "Priam's Treasure" itself disappeared from Berlin at the end of the Second World War, seized by advancing Soviet forces, and its fate remained a mystery for decades until it publicly resurfaced in Moscow's Pushkin Museum in the 1990s, where a large part of it remains today amid an unresolved international dispute over ownership. The dig, the find, and Schliemann's own account of both therefore have to be weighed as three separate and not always compatible things.
