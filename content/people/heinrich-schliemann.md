German businessman who excavated Troy and Mycenae, proved the Bronze Age Aegean was real, and destroyed a great deal of it doing so.

<!-- field: significance -->
Founded Aegean prehistory as a field, and stands as its permanent cautionary tale about method and self-promotion.

<!-- field: body -->
Heinrich Schliemann (1822–1890) was a German businessman who became the most famous archaeologist of the 19th century. The son of a poor pastor in Mecklenburg, he made a fortune in trade, including in the indigo business and during the Crimean War, taught himself many languages, and retired from business in his forties to search for the cities of Homer.

In 1871 he began digging at Hisarlik in north-west Turkey, which Frank Calvert had already identified as Troy. He cut a great trench through the mound, destroying much of the evidence, and in 1873 found a hoard of gold he called "Priam's Treasure". It belongs to a level about a thousand years earlier than any Trojan War. He smuggled it out of Ottoman territory; it went to Berlin, was taken by Soviet forces in 1945, and resurfaced in Moscow's Pushkin Museum in the 1990s.

In 1876 he excavated Grave Circle A at Mycenae and found the shaft graves with their gold masks, announcing that he had found the tombs of Agamemnon. He was centuries out, but the find was momentous: it proved that a rich Bronze Age civilisation had existed in mainland Greece. He later excavated at Orchomenos and, with Wilhelm Dörpfeld, at Tiryns.

His accounts are unreliable. He exaggerated, rearranged dates for dramatic effect, and claimed his young Greek wife Sophia was present at finds when she was not. He is remembered both as the founder of Aegean archaeology and as a warning about method and self-promotion.
