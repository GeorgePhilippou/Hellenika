<!-- field: summary -->
Head of the Academy for a quarter of a century, known for his moral gravity, his theory of daimons, and the threefold division of philosophy.

<!-- field: significance -->
Xenocrates turned the Academy's teaching into a tidy system, and is the probable source of the logic-physics-ethics scheme the Stoics took over.

<!-- field: body -->
Xenocrates came from Chalcedon, on the Bosphorus, and joined Plato's Academy as a young man. After Plato's death in 347 BC he left Athens, with Aristotle, for Assos and the court of Hermias, and returned later. When Speusippus died in 339 he was chosen to lead the school, a position he held until 314.

He systematised Plato's later thought. Like Speusippus, he made numbers central, but he identified the Forms with mathematical objects instead of discarding them. He is credited with dividing philosophy into logic, physics and ethics, which later became the standard scheme in Stoic teaching. And he developed a theory of daimons, intermediate beings between gods and humans, which he used to explain why religious stories portray gods acting badly: such acts belong to the less perfect daimons.

Ancient writers admired his character. Anecdotes describe him as grave and incorruptible, said to have kept only a small part of a large gift from Alexander, and unmoved by the charms of the courtesan Phryne, and so respected that an Athenian jury is said to have refused to let him swear an oath. Plutarch reports that he led a delegation to the Macedonian regent Antipater in 322 BC to plead for Athenian prisoners.

Nothing of his many books survives except titles and fragments, preserved by Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus and others.
