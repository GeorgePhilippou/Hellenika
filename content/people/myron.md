<!-- field: summary -->
A leading Athenian bronze sculptor of the mid-5th century BC, famous for the Discobolus and his lifelike animals.

<!-- field: significance -->
A master of figures in motion at the turn from the Severe Style to High Classical sculpture.

<!-- field: body -->
Myron was born at Eleutherae, on the border of Attica and Boeotia, and worked in Athens around 470–440 BC, an older contemporary of Pheidias and Polykleitos. He worked mainly in bronze, and none of his original works survives; they are known from ancient descriptions and Roman copies.

His most famous work is the *Discobolus*, a discus thrower captured at the top of his backswing, his body twisted into two crossing arcs — a solution to the problem of showing violent motion in a still figure. Ancient critics praised his skill at movement and proportion, though they thought his faces lacked emotion.

He also made a group of Athena and the satyr Marsyas for the Acropolis, showing the goddess casting away the flutes she had invented, known from Roman copies, and statues of athletes and gods. His bronze cow on the Athenian Acropolis was so lifelike that dozens of ancient epigrams joked that calves tried to suckle it and herdsmen tried to drive it away.

Pliny the Elder says Myron was more varied and more careful with proportion than Polykleitos, but less concerned with expressing feeling. His work marks the moment when Greek sculptors mastered the human body in action.
