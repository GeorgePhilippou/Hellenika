<!-- field: summary -->
Stoic from Rhodes who brought the school to the Roman elite and whose work On Duty shaped Cicero.

<!-- field: significance -->
Panaetius adapted Stoicism to Roman public life, and through Cicero his ideas about duty entered European moral thought.

<!-- field: body -->
Panaetius was born on Rhodes about 185 BC, studied at Athens and Pergamon, and in the 140s BC travelled to Rome. There he joined the circle around Scipio Aemilianus, which also included the historian Polybius and the statesman Laelius, and accompanied Scipio on an embassy to the eastern Mediterranean. He became head of the Stoic school at Athens around 129 BC.

He reshaped Stoicism to suit men who held public office. He moved the emphasis from the ideal wise man, a figure few could be, to the ordinary person trying to do the right thing, and gave much attention to what is fitting in a given role. His treatise On Duty, now lost, set out four roles that each person plays: as a rational being, as an individual with particular talents, as a person of a given station, and as a person making choices. Cicero, in his own On Duties, says that he followed Panaetius closely, and through that book the Stoic ethics of duty reached medieval and modern Europe.

He also broke with earlier Stoics on several points. He doubted divination and astrology, which Chrysippus had defended, and denied that the world would be destroyed by fire, preferring Aristotle's view of an eternal cosmos. He admired Plato and Aristotle and quoted them often.

His writings are lost. The chief witnesses are Cicero and Seneca, and the later Stoic Posidonius, who was his pupil.
