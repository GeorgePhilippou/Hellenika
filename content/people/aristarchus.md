The first person known to have placed the Sun, not the Earth, at the centre of the cosmos — eighteen centuries before Copernicus.

<!-- field: body -->
Aristarchus (c. 310–230 BC) was an astronomer and mathematician from the island of Samos who worked in Alexandria. He is the first person known to have proposed that the Earth goes round the Sun, about eighteen centuries before Copernicus.

His heliocentric theory survives only because Archimedes summarised it in *The Sand Reckoner*: the Sun and fixed stars do not move, the Earth turns round the Sun, and the sphere of the stars is vastly larger than once thought. That last point was the answer to the obvious objection. If the Earth moved, nearby stars should seem to shift against more distant ones over the year, and no such shift could be seen. Either the stars were unimaginably far away or the Earth stood still; most ancient astronomers reasonably chose the second. Stellar parallax was not measured until 1838.

His one surviving work, *On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon*, uses sound geometry with rough measurements. It puts the Sun about twenty times too close, but shows correctly that the Sun is much larger than the Earth — perhaps the reason he put it at the centre.

His theory found few followers in antiquity, and Ptolemy's Earth-centred system prevailed until Copernicus, who knew of Aristarchus' idea. A bright crater on the Moon is named after him.
