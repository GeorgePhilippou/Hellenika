<!-- field: summary -->
The first person known to have placed the Sun, not the Earth, at the centre of the cosmos — eighteen centuries before Copernicus.

<!-- field: significance -->
Proposed heliocentrism with a coherent supporting argument, and was set aside for reasons that were, on the evidence then available, defensible.

<!-- field: body -->
Aristarchus' heliocentric proposal survives only because Archimedes summarised it in *The Sand Reckoner*. He also argued that the sphere of fixed stars was immensely larger than previously supposed — a necessary consequence, since no stellar parallax could be observed.

That was precisely the objection. If the Earth moves, nearby stars should shift against distant ones over the year. No such shift was detectable, so either the stars were unimaginably far away or the Earth was still. Ancient astronomers reasonably chose the second. Stellar parallax was not measured until 1838.

His surviving treatise on the sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon uses sound geometry with imprecise measurements, and gets the Sun about twenty times too close — but establishes correctly that it is much larger than the Earth.
