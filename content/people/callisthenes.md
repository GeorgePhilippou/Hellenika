<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek historian and relative of Aristotle who recorded Alexander's campaigns and died after opposing his adoption of Persian court ritual.

<!-- field: significance -->
His fall marked the growing tension between Alexander and his Greek and Macedonian followers over the king's divine and Persian claims.

<!-- field: body -->
Callisthenes (c. 360–327 BC) came from Olynthus in northern Greece and was a great-nephew of Aristotle, with whom he compiled a list of victors in the Pythian Games; an inscription at Delphi records the honours they received for it. He wrote a history of Greece and joined Alexander's expedition in 334 BC as its official historian.

His *Deeds of Alexander*, sent back to Greece in instalments, glorified the king: he described the sea off Pamphylia bowing before him, and supported the claim that the oracle of Ammon at Siwa had called Alexander son of Zeus. The work is lost but was used by later historians.

In 327 BC, in Bactria, Alexander tried to introduce *proskynesis*, the Persian custom of bowing down before the king, at his court. Callisthenes refused, arguing that such honour belonged to the gods, and the attempt was dropped. Soon after, some royal pages plotted to kill the king. Callisthenes had been their tutor and was accused of encouraging them. Accounts of his end differ: Ptolemy said he was tortured and hanged; others said he died of illness in captivity. His death embittered Aristotle's school against Alexander.
