<!-- field: summary -->
Spartan regent who commanded the Greek victory at Plataea in 479 BC, then died under suspicion of treasonous dealings with Persia.

<!-- field: significance -->
His command at Plataea ended the Persian invasion of mainland Greece; his downfall a decade later, whatever its true cause, became a cautionary tale in Greek political memory about the corrupting effect of victory and power.

<!-- field: body -->
As regent for his young cousin Pleistarchus, Pausanias held effective command of Sparta and led the largest hoplite army Greece had assembled to Plataea in 479 BC, where the death of the Persian commander Mardonius and the collapse of his army ended the invasion.

His later career is murkier and reported mainly by his political enemies. Thucydides describes him adopting Persian dress and manners after the victory, and both Thucydides and Herodotus report accusations that he corresponded secretly with the Persian king and, later, incited a helot revolt against Sparta itself — charges that, if true, describe a stunning reversal from the man who had just defeated Persia. The ephors moved against him; he took refuge in the sanctuary of Athena Chalkioikos, and was left to starve, dying either inside or as he was dragged out to prevent the sanctuary being polluted by his death.

Ancient and modern historians alike treat the treason narrative with caution, since it survives only through the accounts of the Spartan authorities who destroyed him.
