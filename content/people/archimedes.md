<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest mathematician and engineer of antiquity — founder of hydrostatics, and the man who came closest to inventing calculus.

<!-- field: significance -->
His method of exhaustion anticipates integration by nearly two millennia, and his mechanics remained unsurpassed until the seventeenth century.

<!-- field: body -->
Archimedes (c. 287–212 BC) was a mathematician, physicist and engineer of Syracuse in Sicily, probably the greatest mathematician of antiquity. He may have studied in Alexandria, and corresponded with scholars there such as Eratosthenes.

He calculated π as lying between 3¹⁰⁄₇₁ and 3¹⁄₇ by fitting polygons inside and outside a circle. He found the area and volume of a sphere, showing that it has two-thirds the volume of the cylinder that encloses it — a result he asked to have carved on his tomb. His "method of exhaustion", finding areas by summing ever-smaller pieces, anticipated integral calculus. In physics he founded the study of floating bodies (hydrostatics) and gave the mathematical law of the lever: "give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth". The story of his shouting "Eureka!" in the bath is later legend.

When Rome besieged Syracuse in 213–212 BC, his machines — catapults ranged for every distance and cranes that lifted ships from the water — held the Romans off for two years. The story that he burned ships with mirrors is a later addition. He was killed in the sack of the city in 212 BC, by tradition while studying a diagram in the sand.

In 1906 the scholar J. L. Heiberg identified his works beneath the text of a medieval prayer book, the Archimedes Palimpsest. It preserved *The Method*, in which he explains how he discovered results by mechanical reasoning before proving them by strict geometry.
