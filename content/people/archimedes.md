<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest mathematician and engineer of antiquity — founder of hydrostatics, and the man who came closest to inventing calculus.

<!-- field: significance -->
His method of exhaustion anticipates integration by nearly two millennia, and his mechanics remained unsurpassed until the seventeenth century.

<!-- field: body -->
Archimedes narrowed the value of π to between 3¹⁰/₇₁ and 3¹/₇ using a method of inscribed and circumscribed polygons that anticipates much later approaches to the same problem, found the volume and surface area of a sphere in exact proportion to its circumscribing cylinder — a result he considered his finest achievement and specifically asked to have carved as a diagram on his own tomb — and established both the law of the lever ("give me a place to stand and I will move the earth," as later writers had him say) and the basic principle of buoyancy.

His siege engines defended Syracuse against a besieging Roman fleet and army for two full years: reportedly including grappling cranes capable of lifting enemy ships partway out of the water and dropping them, and catapults and other artillery accurately ranged for every distance an attacker might approach from. The later, much-repeated story that he set Roman ships ablaze using an array of mirrors to focus sunlight is a considerably later addition to his legend and has never been convincingly replicated at any real siege-relevant distance, making it almost certainly more myth than mechanism. Long after his death, tradition also credited him with building sophisticated geared devices to model the movements of the sun, moon and planets; the surviving Antikythera Mechanism, an intricate bronze geared instrument recovered from a shipwreck, shows that this kind of mechanical astronomical modelling was genuinely being built in the Hellenistic world, even if its direct connection to Archimedes himself remains unproven.

He was killed in 212 BC by a Roman soldier during the final sack of Syracuse, reportedly while absorbed in working out a geometric diagram drawn in sand and asking only that the soldier not disturb his circles. In 1906 a medieval Byzantine prayer book was discovered to be a palimpsest, its original pages scraped and reused, written over erased copies of several genuine Archimedes texts, including *The Method*, in which he candidly explains how he first used informal mechanical reasoning and something close to infinitesimals to discover mathematical results privately, only afterward working out how to prove them by fully rigorous classical geometric argument.
