<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian popular leader of the 420s, blamed by Thucydides and mocked by Aristophanes, who won at Sphacteria and died at Amphipolis.

<!-- field: significance -->
Cleon became the type of the demagogue, and the debate over whether he deserves that name is a debate about how to judge Athenian democracy through hostile sources.

<!-- field: body -->
Cleon rose after Pericles's death in 429 BC to become the most influential speaker in the Athenian assembly. Both of our main hostile witnesses, Thucydides and Aristophanes, wrote with personal grievance: Thucydides was exiled after failing to save Amphipolis from Brasidas, and Aristophanes had been prosecuted by him. So the portrait we have, of a loud, violent, self-promoting orator, is heavily coloured.

Two episodes define him. At Mytilene in 427, after the island's revolt was suppressed, Cleon proposed killing every adult man. The assembly agreed, sent out the order, and the next day reconsidered; a second ship arrived just in time. Thucydides gives his speech against mercy as a defence of harsh imperial rule. At Pylos and Sphacteria in 425, a Spartan force was cut off on an island. Cleon, provoked by Nicias's offer to yield him the command, accepted it, and with Demosthenes he captured 292 men, of whom about 120 were Spartiates. It was the most unexpected success of the war.

He was less lucky in 422. Leading an expedition to the northern coast, he camped near Amphipolis, was surprised by Brasidas, and was killed while retreating. Brasidas died in the same fighting. With the two most determined advocates of war gone, the Peace of Nicias followed within a year.

Modern historians have partly rehabilitated him as the first prominent politician not from the old aristocracy, and see much of the criticism as class snobbery. Whether he was a demagogue or a capable public servant depends heavily on how far Thucydides is trusted.
