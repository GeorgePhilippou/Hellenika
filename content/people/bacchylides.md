<!-- field: summary -->
The lyric poet from Ceos, rival of Pindar, whose victory odes and dithyrambs were rediscovered on papyrus in 1896.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the nine canonical Greek lyric poets, recovered almost entire from the sands of Egypt.

<!-- field: body -->
Bacchylides (c. 518–451 BC) was born at Ioulis on the island of Ceos, the nephew of the poet Simonides. Like his uncle and his rival Pindar, he wrote choral songs for paying patrons: victory odes (*epinikia*) for winners at the great games, and dithyrambs and other songs for festivals.

His most important patron was Hiero I of Syracuse. Bacchylides wrote odes for Hiero's horse-race win at Olympia in 476 BC (the same victory celebrated in Pindar's First Olympian), his chariot win at Delphi in 470, and his Olympic chariot victory of 468, which Pindar did not celebrate. His Fifth Ode tells how Heracles met the ghost of Meleager in the underworld. Ancient critics judged him smooth and elegant but less sublime than Pindar.

For centuries his work was known only from scattered quotations. In 1896 the British Museum bought papyrus fragments found in Egypt, and in 1897 Frederic Kenyon published about twenty poems — fourteen victory odes and six dithyrambs. They include Ode 17, in which the young Theseus dives into the sea to prove he is the son of Poseidon, and Ode 18, a dialogue between the king of Athens and a chorus about the approach of Theseus.
