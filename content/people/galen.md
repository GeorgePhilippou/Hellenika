<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek physician from Pergamon (AD 129–c. 216) whose writings dominated medicine in Europe and the Islamic world for fourteen centuries.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most influential doctor in history before modern times, whose anatomy and theory of the humours ruled medicine until the 16th and 17th centuries.

<!-- field: body -->
Galen was born in AD 129 at Pergamon, the son of a wealthy architect. After a vision of the healing god Asclepius came to his father, he trained in medicine at Pergamon, Smyrna and Alexandria. Back home he served as physician to the gladiators of the high priest of Asia, gaining practical experience of wounds. In 162 he moved to Rome, where he won fame with public demonstrations of anatomy, and he became physician to the emperor Marcus Aurelius and later to Commodus and Septimius Severus. He lived through the great Antonine Plague, and died around 216.

Galen wrote prolifically — on anatomy, physiology, diagnosis, drugs, philosophy and his own career — and his surviving works fill some twenty volumes in the standard edition. Since human dissection was no longer permitted, he dissected apes, pigs and other animals, and assumed human bodies were similar, causing errors later corrected by Vesalius. His experiments showed that arteries carry blood, not air, and that cutting certain nerves in a squealing pig silenced it.

He built on Hippocrates' theory of the four humours, making it a complete system. Translated into Syriac, Arabic and Latin, his works were the basis of medical teaching until early modern times.
