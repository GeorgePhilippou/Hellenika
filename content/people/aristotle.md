<!-- field: summary -->
Plato's student, Alexander's tutor, founder of the Lyceum, and the most systematically encyclopaedic mind of antiquity.

<!-- field: significance -->
Effectively founded logic, biology, literary criticism and political science as distinct disciplines, and dominated European and Islamic thought for nearly two thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Aristotle (384–322 BC) was born at Stagira in Chalcidice, northern Greece. His father Nicomachus was court physician to King Amyntas III of Macedon. At seventeen he went to Athens and studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years, until Plato's death in 347 BC.

He then lived at Assos in Asia Minor and on Lesbos, where he studied animals with remarkable care; some of his observations, such as on the reproduction of the octopus and of a dogfish, were only confirmed in the 19th century. In 343 BC Philip II hired him to tutor the young Alexander at Mieza. In 335 BC he returned to Athens and founded his own school at the Lyceum, where he and his students collected the constitutions of 158 Greek states; only the *Constitution of the Athenians* survives, recovered on papyrus in 1890.

Aristotle rejected Plato's theory of separate Forms: the form of a thing exists in the thing itself. That turned philosophy towards study of the actual world. He wrote on almost everything — logic, physics, biology, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetry — and effectively founded logic, biology and literary criticism as subjects. His surviving works are mostly lecture notes, later edited by Andronicus of Rhodes.

After Alexander's death in 323 BC, anti-Macedonian feeling made Athens dangerous for him; he left, saying he would not let Athens sin twice against philosophy, and died at Chalcis the next year. Through Arabic and Latin translations his work dominated Islamic and European thought for nearly two thousand years.
