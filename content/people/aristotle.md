<!-- field: summary -->
Plato's student, Alexander's tutor, founder of the Lyceum, and the most systematically encyclopaedic mind of antiquity.

<!-- field: significance -->
Effectively founded logic, biology, literary criticism and political science as distinct disciplines, and dominated European and Islamic thought for nearly two thousand years.

<!-- field: body -->
Aristotle's father Nicomachus served as court physician to the Macedonian king Amyntas III, Philip II's father, a connection that likely shaped Aristotle's own later ties to the Macedonian court more than any single later decision did. He studied under Plato at the Academy in Athens for some twenty years, from his late teens until Plato's death, and then broke decisively with his teacher's theory of Forms: for Aristotle, the form of a thing is not located in some separate, perfect realm but exists within the thing itself, inseparable from its matter. That single shift in position turned philosophy firmly toward empirical investigation of the actual world rather than abstract contemplation of ideal originals behind it.

After Plato's death he spent time at Assos and on Lesbos, where he collected and dissected marine specimens with a thoroughness that produced genuinely original science: his observations on the hectocotyl arm of the octopus and on the placental dogfish were dismissed by later naturalists as fanciful and were only confirmed correct in the nineteenth century, roughly two thousand years later. He and his students at the Lyceum also collected written constitutions of 158 Greek city-states as comparative material for his political theory, of which only the *Constitution of the Athenians* survives, recovered on Egyptian papyrus in the nineteenth century after being lost for the rest of antiquity.

He tutored the young Alexander at Mieza in Macedon from 343 BC, teaching him alongside a small group of other young Macedonian nobles. After Alexander's death, a surge of anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens made his position there dangerous, and he left the city for Chalcis, reportedly remarking that he would not let Athens sin twice against philosophy, a pointed reference to the city's earlier execution of Socrates. His surviving works, mostly compressed lecture notes rather than works polished for a reading public, were later edited and organised as a set by Andronicus of Rhodes and, transmitted onward through Islamic scholars such as Averroes before being reabsorbed into medieval Europe, went on to dominate Western and Islamic philosophy for close to two thousand years — earning him, in Dante's later phrase, the title of "the master of those who know."
