<!-- field: summary -->
Tragedian of Oedipus Rex and Antigone, who won more dramatic victories than any of his rivals and served Athens as general and priest.

<!-- field: significance -->
Perfected tragic structure — Aristotle used Oedipus Rex as his model of plot — and shifted focus from cosmic justice to individual character under pressure.

<!-- field: body -->
Sophocles (c. 497–406 BC) was an Athenian tragic playwright, born at Colonus just outside the city. He wrote around 120 plays over more than sixty years and won first prize at the festival of Dionysus about twenty times, reportedly never coming lower than second. Only seven tragedies survive complete, including *Antigone*, *Oedipus Rex*, *Electra* and *Oedipus at Colonus*, with much of a satyr play, *The Trackers*, recovered on papyrus.

He is credited with adding a third actor to the stage and enlarging the chorus, allowing more complex scenes between characters. His plays focus on individuals under extreme pressure — Oedipus hunting the killer who turns out to be himself, Antigone choosing to bury her brother against the king's command. *Oedipus Rex* was Aristotle's model of the perfect tragic plot.

Sophocles was also a public figure. He served as a treasurer of the Delian League, was elected general alongside Pericles during the Samian War of 441–439 BC, and later served on the emergency board set up after the Sicilian disaster. He was a priest of a healing hero and helped bring the cult of Asclepius to Athens, housing the god's image in his own home until a sanctuary was built.

He died in 406 BC, shortly after Euripides, just before Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War.
