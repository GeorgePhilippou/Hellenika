The Greek traveller of the 2nd century AD whose Description of Greece is an invaluable guide to its sanctuaries, monuments and works of art.

<!-- field: body -->
Pausanias (c. AD 110–180) was a Greek writer, probably from Lydia in western Asia Minor, who travelled widely in Greece during the reigns of the Roman emperors Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius. His *Description of Greece*, in ten books, covers Attica, the Peloponnese, Boeotia and Phocis region by region.

Pausanias was most interested in the past — the monuments, temples, statues, tombs and paintings of Archaic and Classical Greece, and the myths, local traditions and religious customs attached to them. He describes, for example, the Acropolis of Athens, the sanctuaries of Olympia and Delphi with their hundreds of statues, the Statue of Zeus at Olympia and the Lion Gate and tombs of Mycenae. He took little interest in the Roman monuments of his own day.

For a long time he was dismissed as a dull compiler. But archaeology has shown him to be an accurate observer. His descriptions guided excavators at Olympia, Delphi and Athens, and helped Schliemann at Mycenae, and they are still used to identify buildings and statues. Many works of art known only through his descriptions would otherwise be entirely lost.

The *Description of Greece* remains the most important guidebook of the ancient world and a key source for Greek art, religion and topography.
