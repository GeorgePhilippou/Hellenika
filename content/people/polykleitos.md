<!-- field: summary -->
Argive bronze sculptor who tried to reduce the perfect human body to a system of proportions, and wrote the book to prove it.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Canon made proportion a subject of theory, not only of craft, and shaped how Greek and Roman sculptors built the human figure for centuries.

<!-- field: body -->
Polykleitos worked in Argos in the second half of the fifth century BC, a generation after Pheidias, and was best known for bronze athletes. Ancient writers ranked him beside Pheidias: Pheidias for gods, Polykleitos for men.

His reputation rested on an idea. He is said to have written a treatise, the Canon, arguing that beauty arises from the exact ratio of each part of the body to every other, and to have made a statue as its demonstration. The book is lost. Galen, writing some six centuries later, reports its claim that beauty lies in the proportion of the parts to one another: finger to finger, the fingers to the palm and wrist, the wrist to the forearm, and so on up the whole body. Scholars have argued for over a century about whether the Doryphoros, the spear-bearer, is the statue in question, and about whether the ratios can be recovered from the copies. The identification is the usual working assumption, not a proven fact.

What the copies do show is a new way of standing. The Doryphoros carries his weight on the right leg and relaxes the left; the shoulders and hips tilt in opposite directions, so the whole body is balanced without being symmetrical. This is the stance that Renaissance artists later revived as contrapposto. His seated Hera at the Argive Heraion, in gold and ivory, was judged by Strabo to be the finest in workmanship of all, though smaller and cheaper than the work of Pheidias.

Because bronze was routinely melted down, nothing Polykleitos himself cast is certainly extant. He is known through Roman marble copies, made mostly for collectors, which preserve the design but not the surface or the metal.
