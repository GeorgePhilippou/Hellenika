<!-- field: summary -->
Long-lived Spartan king who took the war to Persia, defeated Athens' allies, and presided over Sparta's fall from supremacy.

<!-- field: significance -->
His reign spans the peak of Spartan power and its collapse, and much of the blame for the collapse is laid at his door.

<!-- field: body -->
Agesilaus was born lame and was not expected to rule. When his half-brother King Agis died about 400 BC, the succession was contested by Agis's reputed son Leotychidas, whose paternity was rumoured to be Alcibiades. Lysander backed Agesilaus, and an oracle that warned against a "lame kingdom" was read as a warning against a false heir instead. Thus he began his long reign in debt to a man he later resented.

His early years were successful. In Asia Minor in 396–394 he raided the satrapal territory around Sardis and presented himself as a panhellenic champion, a role that recalled the Trojan War, though his campaign ended when Persian money stirred up Athens, Thebes, Corinth and Argos against Sparta. He won at Coronea in 394 and, in the King's Peace of 387, secured a settlement that left Sparta arbiter of Greek affairs.

Then his policy hardened. Sparta seized the Theban acropolis in 382 and tried to break up the Boeotian league; the consequence was the Theban revival that led to defeat at Leuctra in 371 and the loss of Messenia. Agesilaus's personal hostility to Thebes is commonly seen as a cause. He nevertheless helped to defend Sparta itself when Epaminondas invaded Laconia in 370/69, and again in 362, when he was over eighty.

At the age of about eighty he took mercenary service in Egypt to raise money for Sparta. He died on the return voyage, and his body was embalmed in wax and carried home for burial.
