<!-- field: summary -->
Sicilian sophist and teacher of rhetoric who brought ornate prose to Athens and argued that nothing exists or can be known.

<!-- field: significance -->
Gorgias made the power of speech itself a theme of philosophy, and his lost treatise on non-being remains one of the boldest arguments in Greek thought.

<!-- field: body -->
Gorgias came from Leontini in Sicily. In 427 BC, when his city sought Athenian help against Syracuse, he headed the embassy, and Diodorus says his oratory astonished an audience unused to its ornate balance and rhythm. He stayed in Greece for the rest of a very long life, teaching for high fees and speaking at Olympia and Delphi.

Two speeches survive. In the Encomium of Helen he argues that Helen is blameless for going to Troy, whether she was carried off by force, persuaded by words, or overcome by love, and in doing so describes speech as a powerful ruler that can drive out fear and awaken pity. In the Defence of Palamedes he speaks for a mythical man falsely accused of treason. Both are exercises in argument as much as sincere claims.

His treatise On Not-Being is known through summaries by Sextus Empiricus and the author of On Melissus, Xenophanes and Gorgias. It has three theses: nothing exists; if anything exists, it cannot be known; and if it can be known, it cannot be communicated. It reads as a reply to Parmenides that uses Eleatic methods against Eleatic conclusions. Readers are still divided over whether it is a serious metaphysical claim, a spoof, or a showpiece.

Plato gave the name Gorgias to a dialogue in which Socrates questions the value of rhetoric without knowledge of justice, and in which Gorgias is a courteous but outmatched opponent. It is our most influential judgement of him, and a hostile one. Gorgias's own view was that rhetoric offers persuasion and pleasure, and that teachers of it are not responsible for the uses to which their pupils put it.
