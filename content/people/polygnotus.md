<!-- field: summary -->
The great wall painter of the early 5th century BC, whose huge scenes of myth decorated public buildings at Athens and Delphi.

<!-- field: significance -->
The founder of large-scale Greek painting, known to us only through Pausanias' descriptions and the vase painters he influenced.

<!-- field: body -->
Polygnotus came from the island of Thasos in the northern Aegean and worked mainly in Athens around 475–447 BC, under the patronage of the statesman Cimon, who reportedly granted him Athenian citizenship. Ancient writers ranked him as the first great Greek painter.

His works were huge wall paintings on wooden panels or plaster in public buildings. In Athens he painted the sack of Troy in the Stoa Poikile (Painted Stoa) and scenes in the Theseion and elsewhere. At Delphi he painted the Lesche (clubhouse) of the Knidians with two enormous compositions, the fall of Troy and Odysseus' visit to the underworld, which Pausanias describes figure by figure.

Aristotle praised him for showing people better than they are, with nobility of character, and others for the expressive faces of his figures. Instead of placing figures in a single row, he arranged them at different levels across a landscape, suggesting depth. He used only a few colours.

All his works are lost. Their influence can be seen in Athenian vase paintings of the mid-5th century BC, such as the Niobid Krater, which arrange figures across rocky landscapes in the manner described for Polygnotus.
