<!-- field: summary -->
The soldier-poet of Paros in the 7th century BC, the earliest Greek poet to speak in a sharply personal and often mocking voice.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first great personal voice in Greek poetry after Homer — candid, satirical and anti-heroic.

<!-- field: body -->
Archilochus came from the island of Paros in the Cyclades and lived in the mid-7th century BC. He took part in the Parian colonisation of the island of Thasos, fighting the Thracians on the mainland, and seems to have lived as a soldier. A fragment describing the sun blotted out at midday is usually linked to the total eclipse of 6 April 648 BC.

His poems, surviving only in fragments, are in iambic and elegiac metres. They speak of war, wine, love, politics and enmity, in a frank personal voice very unlike epic. In the most famous, he admits leaving his shield behind in a bush to escape the Thracians: "Let it go — I'll get another just as good." For a hoplite, losing one's shield was a disgrace, and the poem cheerfully mocks heroic values.

Ancient writers knew him above all for his invective. They told how, when Lycambes broke off his daughter Neobule's engagement to the poet, Archilochus attacked the family so savagely in verse that they hanged themselves — almost certainly a legend built from his poems. A papyrus published in 1974, the "Cologne Epode", preserves a long, explicit seduction scene.

Ancient critics ranked him with Homer, and he was honoured with a hero shrine on Paros.
