<!-- field: summary -->
The most powerful of Alexander's successors in Asia, first of them to claim the title of king, killed fighting at Ipsus in 301 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
His bid to reunite Alexander's empire under himself was the single greatest threat to the partition into separate kingdoms, and the coalition that finally defeated him at Ipsus set the borders of the Hellenistic world for a generation.

<!-- field: body -->
Antigonus I (c. 382–301 BC), called Monophthalmus, "the One-Eyed", was a Macedonian noble and general who served Philip II and Alexander the Great. He lost an eye in one of Philip's campaigns. Under Alexander he governed Phrygia in central Asia Minor, guarding the empire's lines of communication.

After Alexander's death in 323 BC he became one of the leading Successors. By about 315 BC he controlled most of Asia Minor and Syria, with resources no rival could match, and set out to reunite Alexander's empire under his own rule. Ptolemy, Seleucus, Lysimachus and Cassander repeatedly combined against him.

In 306 BC his son Demetrius destroyed Ptolemy's fleet off Salamis in Cyprus. Antigonus then took the title of king — the first of the Successors to do so, since Alexander's own family had all been killed — and the others soon followed.

In 301 BC the allied kings met him at Ipsus in Phrygia, in one of the largest battles of the age. Seleucus' war elephants, obtained from the Indian king Chandragupta Maurya, cut off Demetrius' cavalry from the battle, and Antigonus, over eighty, was killed fighting. His lands were divided among the victors. His grandson Antigonus II Gonatas later won the kingdom of Macedon, and the Antigonid dynasty ruled it until the Roman conquest in 168 BC.
