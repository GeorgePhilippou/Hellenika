<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian general who persuaded his colleagues to fight at Marathon in 490 BC and devised the tactics that won it.

<!-- field: significance -->
Marathon was the first proof that Persian infantry could be beaten in the open by Greek hoplites, and Miltiades' insistence on fighting, against caution from other generals, made the battle happen at all.

<!-- field: body -->
Before Marathon, Miltiades had spent decades as tyrant of the Thracian Chersonese, ruling as a Persian vassal and fighting alongside Darius' army in the Scythian campaign, before falling out with Persian authority and fleeing to Athens around 493 BC — an unusually compromised background for a man who would soon be Athens' most trusted general against Persia.

At Marathon, the ten Athenian generals were reportedly split on whether to wait for Spartan reinforcement or attack immediately. Miltiades argued for attacking, and as the general holding the tie-breaking vote that day under the rotating command, forced the issue. He thinned the Greek centre to extend the line and prevent encirclement — a risk that worked because the wings broke their opposing Persian flanks and turned inward.

The Athenians honoured him briefly but decisively withdrew that trust: a subsequent expedition he led against Paros, ostensibly to punish it for aiding Persia, failed and left him badly wounded. Prosecuted for deceiving the people, he was fined an enormous sum and died shortly afterward, leaving the debt to his son Cimon, who would later lead the Delian League.
