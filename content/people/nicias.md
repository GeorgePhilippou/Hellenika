<!-- field: summary -->
Wealthy, cautious Athenian commander who made a peace, opposed the Sicilian Expedition, and then led it to disaster.

<!-- field: significance -->
Nicias's career shows how a hesitant, pious leader can be worse for a city than a reckless one when placed in a war he did not want.

<!-- field: body -->
Nicias was one of the richest Athenians of the fifth century, with a fortune from silver mines and a reputation for careful piety, and he became the leading moderate after the deaths of Pericles and Cleon. He pursued a negotiated settlement with Sparta, and the peace that bears his name was concluded in 421 BC. It did not hold, but it gave Athens some years of respite.

He was also the man who tried to stop the expedition against Sicily. In the assembly debate of 415 he argued that the island was too far and too large, and that the city had enemies at home; when the assembly voted to go anyway, he was named one of the commanders, alongside Alcibiades, who had championed the plan, and Lamachus. Alcibiades was recalled to face charges of sacrilege and defected to Sparta. Nicias, now effectively in sole command, moved slowly, let the moment to isolate Syracuse pass, and was outmanoeuvred when Sparta sent the general Gylippus to help the city.

In 413, with the army trapped and losing, he could have withdrawn. When the moon was eclipsed on 27 August, his seers said to wait "thrice nine days", and he obeyed. The Athenians were then defeated in the harbour and ambushed on the road inland; the survivors were held in the stone quarries. Nicias and his fellow general Demosthenes were executed.

Thucydides, who did not spare Nicias's faults, wrote that he least deserved to come to this end, because his whole life had been regulated by virtue. The verdict is one of the most striking in the history.
