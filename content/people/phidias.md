<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest sculptor of classical Athens, who made the Athena Parthenos and the Zeus at Olympia and directed the Parthenon sculptures.

<!-- field: significance -->
Regarded in antiquity as the supreme artist of the gods; his style defines high classical sculpture.

<!-- field: body -->
Phidias (c. 480–430 BC) was an Athenian, the son of Charmides. Little is known of his early career, but by the 450s he had made the colossal bronze Athena that stood on the Acropolis, called the Promachos ("champion") in later times, whose spear tip was said to be visible to sailors rounding Cape Sounion.

Plutarch says that Pericles put Phidias in charge of his entire building programme. Whatever his exact role, the sculptures of the Parthenon — the metopes, the frieze and the pediments, carved between about 447 and 432 BC by many hands — are generally thought to reflect his designs. Inside the temple stood his masterpiece, the Athena Parthenos, about 12 metres high, of gold and ivory over a wooden core, dedicated in 438.

Phidias was accused of stealing gold from the statue, and, according to Plutarch, of impiety for putting portraits of himself and Pericles on her shield. Plutarch says he died in prison; another tradition says he went to Olympia, where he made the seated Zeus of gold and ivory, about 13 metres high, counted among the Seven Wonders of the World. Excavations in the 1950s found his workshop there, with tools, clay moulds for the god's robe, and a black cup inscribed "I belong to Pheidias".
