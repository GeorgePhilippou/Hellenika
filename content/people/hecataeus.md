<!-- field: summary -->
The Milesian writer of the late 6th century BC who pioneered Greek geography and the critical study of legend.

<!-- field: significance -->
A forerunner of Herodotus, whose world map and sceptical approach to myth helped found geography and history.

<!-- field: body -->
Hecataeus (c. 550–476 BC) was a citizen of Miletus in Ionia, the city of the first philosophers. He travelled widely, including to Egypt, and wrote two works now known only from fragments.

His *Periodos Gēs* ("Journey Round the World") described the coasts and peoples of Europe and Asia, including Libya, place by place, and was accompanied by a map improving that of his fellow Milesian Anaximander. His *Genealogies* treated the myths of heroic families critically. It opened: "Hecataeus of Miletus speaks thus: I write what seems to me to be true; for the stories of the Greeks are many and, in my opinion, ridiculous." He tried to rationalise legends — the many-headed dog Cerberus, he suggested, was really a dangerous snake.

Herodotus, who used his work and sometimes mocked it, says Hecataeus advised the Ionians not to revolt against Persia in 499 BC, pointing to the size of the empire, and then suggested they seize the temple treasures to build a fleet. Both pieces of advice were ignored.

His combination of geography, ethnography and criticism of myth laid the ground for Herodotus' *Histories*.
