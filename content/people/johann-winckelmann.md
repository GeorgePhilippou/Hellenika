<!-- field: summary -->
The German scholar whose writings in the 1750s and 1760s made ancient Greek art the ideal of European taste.

<!-- field: significance -->
The founder of classical art history and the prophet of neoclassicism and German Hellenism.

<!-- field: body -->
Winckelmann (1717–1768) was the son of a cobbler in Stendal in Prussia. He taught himself Greek, worked as a schoolteacher and librarian, and in 1755 published *Reflections on the Imitation of Greek Works in Painting and Sculpture*, arguing that modern artists could become great only by imitating the Greeks. In a famous phrase, he found the essence of Greek art in "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur".

The same year he moved to Rome, converted to Catholicism, and became librarian to Cardinal Alessandro Albani, whose collection of antiquities was among the finest in the city. In 1763 he was made superintendent of antiquities in Rome.

In his *History of the Art of Antiquity* (1764) he treated art not as a series of artists' lives but as a development tied to climate, society and political freedom, dividing Greek art into stages of growth, flowering and decline. His descriptions of statues such as the Apollo Belvedere and the Laocoön were rapturous. Yet he never went to Greece, and most of the works he admired were Roman copies.

His ideas shaped neoclassical art and architecture and inspired Goethe, Schiller and German scholarship. In 1768 he was murdered in a hotel in Trieste by a man to whom he had shown some gold coins.
