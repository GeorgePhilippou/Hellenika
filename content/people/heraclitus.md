<!-- field: summary -->
Ephesian philosopher of flux and fire, deliberately obscure, who held that change and the tension of opposites govern the world.

<!-- field: significance -->
His idea that stability is an illusion over underlying change, and that opposed forces hold the world in a kind of dynamic balance, shaped later philosophy from Plato's contrast between being and becoming down to modern readings that find him strikingly systematic beneath the aphoristic surface.

<!-- field: body -->
Heraclitus wrote in short, riddling, deliberately difficult sentences, which earned him the nickname "the Obscure" in antiquity and "the weeping philosopher" for his reputedly dim view of humanity. None of his book survives complete; what remains are roughly 100 fragments quoted by later writers, often stripped of context.

His central claim is usually summarised as panta rhei — everything flows — captured in the image that you cannot step into the same river twice, since new waters are always flowing on. But flux for Heraclitus was not chaos: it was governed by the logos, a rational structure or measure that holds change together, and by the unity of opposites — day and night, war and peace, up and down are, on his account, aspects of a single process rather than separate things. Fire serves as his preferred image for this: a substance that persists only by continually consuming and being renewed.

He was scathing about predecessors and contemporaries alike, criticising Pythagoras, Xenophanes and Hecataeus by name for accumulating information without understanding, and is reported to have held ordinary Ephesians in similar contempt.
