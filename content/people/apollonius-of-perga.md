The Hellenistic mathematician whose Conics, the study of curves cut from a cone, was the summit of Greek geometry.

<!-- field: significance -->
Called "the Great Geometer" in antiquity, his work on conic sections later made possible Kepler's laws of planetary motion.

<!-- field: body -->
Apollonius (c. 262–190 BC) was born at Perga in Pamphylia, in southern Asia Minor, studied at Alexandria with the successors of Euclid, and later worked at Pergamon. Ancient writers called him the "Great Geometer".

His masterpiece, the *Conics*, in eight books, studies the curves produced by cutting a cone with a plane. Apollonius showed that all of them could be produced from a single cone by changing the angle of the cut, and gave them the names still used: the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola. He worked out their properties with great rigour and originality. Books 1–4 survive in Greek and Books 5–7 only in an Arabic translation; Book 8 is lost.

He also wrote on other problems of geometry, such as constructing a circle touching three given circles, and on astronomy, where he developed models using epicycles and eccentric circles to explain the irregular movements of the planets — models later used by Hipparchus and Ptolemy.

His work on conics seemed purely theoretical for nearly two thousand years, until Johannes Kepler discovered in 1609 that the planets move in ellipses, and Newton used conic sections to explain gravity.
