<!-- field: summary -->
The Athenian aristocrat, poet and associate of Socrates who led the brutal regime of the Thirty Tyrants in 404–403 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
His rule, and his link to Socrates, helps explain why Athens put Socrates on trial four years later.

<!-- field: body -->
Critias (c. 460–403 BC) came from one of the oldest families in Athens; he was a cousin of Plato's mother, and appears in Plato's dialogues *Charmides*, *Protagoras*, *Timaeus* and the unfinished *Critias*, where the story of Atlantis is told. He spent time with Socrates, wrote elegies, tragedies and prose works on the constitutions of Sparta and other states, and was associated with the Sophists. A surviving fragment of a satyr play, *Sisyphus*, claims that the gods were invented by a clever man to frighten people into behaving; its author is disputed.

Critias admired Sparta and despised the democracy. Exiled in the last years of the Peloponnesian War, he returned after Athens' defeat in 404 and became the most powerful of the Thirty, the oligarchic board installed with Spartan support. Under the Thirty about 1,500 people were put to death and many more fled. When his colleague Theramenes urged moderation, Critias had him condemned and executed.

In spring 403 the democratic exiles under Thrasybulus defeated the Thirty at Munychia in the Piraeus, and Critias was killed in the fighting. Socrates' association with him and with Alcibiades was remembered at his trial in 399.
