<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest Byzantine religious poet, who in the 6th century wrote kontakia, sung verse sermons retelling the Bible.

<!-- field: significance -->
Often called the Pindar of Christian poetry; his hymns brought dramatic poetry into the worship of the Greek Church.

<!-- field: body -->
Romanos (c. 490–556) was born at Emesa (modern Homs) in Syria, perhaps of Jewish family, and served as a deacon at Berytus (Beirut) before moving to Constantinople in the reign of Anastasius I. He served in the church of the Theotokos in the Kyrou district.

A legend told that he had no gift for music until, on Christmas Eve, the Virgin Mary appeared to him in a dream and gave him a scroll to eat. The next morning he climbed the pulpit and sang his first hymn, "Today the Virgin gives birth to Him who is above all being", still sung at Christmas.

His form was the *kontakion*, a long poem of 18 to 24 stanzas sung by a soloist, with a refrain sung by the choir or congregation. In accentual metres, unlike classical Greek verse, the kontakia retell stories from the Bible — the Nativity, the raising of Lazarus, Judas' betrayal, the Resurrection — with vivid dialogue and drama, influenced by Syriac poetry. About sixty are generally accepted as authentic. Some scholars have attributed to him the famous *Akathist Hymn* to the Virgin, though this is disputed. In later centuries the kontakion was cut down to a few stanzas in the liturgy, but Romanos is honoured as a saint.
