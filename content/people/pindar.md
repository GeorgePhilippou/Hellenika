<!-- field: summary -->
Theban poet whose victory odes for athletes turned a day at the games into myth, praise and moral instruction.

<!-- field: significance -->
Pindar is the great voice of aristocratic values in early Greece, and the main evidence for how athletic victory was understood and paid for.

<!-- field: body -->
Pindar was born near Thebes about 518 BC and composed for over fifty years. He was a professional: families and rulers paid him to celebrate a victory in a chariot race, a wrestling match or a foot race, and the ode was performed by a chorus, often at the victor's home city.

The odes follow a rough pattern. They praise the victor and his family, tell a myth that mirrors or complicates the achievement, and close with a moral: success is a gift from the gods, and mortals should not reach beyond their measure. The Greek is dense and hard, and its abrupt shifts of subject are deliberate. Olympian 1, for Hieron of Syracuse, retells the story of Pelops in a version that pointedly rejects the cannibal feast, saying it is wrong to speak ill of the gods.

His patrons show how far the poetry travelled. He wrote for Aiginetan families, for Thessalian nobles, for Theron of Akragas, and for Hieron in Sicily. Pythian 1 places Hieron's power beside the defeat of Persian and Carthaginian invaders. Because so many odes name their commissioners, they are also a rare source for the social and political world of the Greek aristocracy in the early fifth century.

Thebes had sided with Persia in 480 BC, and Pindar's civic loyalties were awkward. Later anecdote says Alexander spared his house in 335 BC; the story fits the poet's afterlife as the "Theban" poet, more than it proves the fact.
