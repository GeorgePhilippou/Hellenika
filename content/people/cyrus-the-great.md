<!-- field: summary -->
Founder of the Achaemenid Empire, who conquered Media, Lydia and Babylon and established a template of tolerant imperial rule.

<!-- field: significance -->
Created the empire that would dominate Greek foreign policy for two centuries and that Alexander eventually destroyed.

<!-- field: body -->
Cyrus built the largest empire the world had yet seen in the space of roughly twenty years, absorbing Media, then Lydia, then Babylon in rapid succession. His conquest of Lydia in 546 BC, achieved by defeating the fabulously wealthy King Croesus, brought Persian rule directly to the Ionian Greek cities of the Anatolian coast for the first time, making eventual conflict between Persia and the Greek mainland something close to a structural certainty over the following century.

He governed by conciliating rather than erasing existing local structures wherever practical. The Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian-style foundation text written in cuneiform, presents him as restoring neglected temples and returning displaced peoples and their cult statues to their homelands — including, in the biblical account preserved in the Book of Ezra, permitting Jewish exiles held in Babylon to return home and rebuild their Temple in Jerusalem. It is, in form, a royal inscription and therefore inherently self-serving propaganda, but its claims broadly match an evident, consistent practical policy of local religious and administrative accommodation across his conquests rather than blanket destruction.

Cyrus was killed in 530 BC campaigning on the empire's volatile northeastern frontier against a nomadic Central Asian people, though ancient accounts of the campaign, his actual cause of death, and even where exactly he was buried vary considerably and cannot be fully reconciled. Greeks nonetheless regarded him with real and lasting respect even as an adversary's founder: Xenophon wrote an entire idealised fictionalised education of Cyrus, the *Cyropaedia*, using him as a manual of ideal kingship for a Greek audience, and Alexander the Great, generations later, made a deliberate point of restoring Cyrus's tomb at Pasargadae after finding it had been plundered, treating the founder of the empire he had just conquered with conspicuous personal reverence.
