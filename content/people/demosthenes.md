<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest Athenian orator, who spent his career warning that Philip of Macedon meant to end Greek freedom — and was right.

<!-- field: significance -->
His speeches are the finest surviving Greek oratory and the fullest contemporary record of resistance to Macedon.

<!-- field: body -->
Demosthenes was orphaned as a young boy and left a substantial inheritance that his appointed guardians proceeded to mismanage and largely deplete over the following decade; on reaching adulthood he studied rhetoric specifically in order to sue them himself, and won a partial victory that recovered only a fraction of what he was owed but launched his public career as an advocate and speechwriter. Plutarch also reports, more famously, that he overcame a genuine speech impediment and weak voice by declaiming with pebbles in his mouth and practising his delivery over the noise of crashing waves; the story is probably embroidered in its specifics, but the sheer intensity of preparation it describes is consistent with the disciplined, carefully constructed speeches that survive.

The *Philippics* and *Olynthiacs* argue relentlessly, speech after speech, that Philip is not a conventional rival power to be managed through the usual diplomacy but an existential threat to Greek freedom itself, and that Athenians must fund campaigns and serve in them personally rather than relying, as had become common practice, on hired mercenaries with no real stake in the outcome. He personally negotiated the Athenian-Theban alliance that met Philip at Chaeronea in 338 BC and lost. Years later, in 324 BC, his career took a serious blow when he was convicted and briefly exiled over the Harpalus affair, accused of pocketing part of a large sum brought to Athens by a fugitive Macedonian treasurer — a charge whose truth is still debated, but which cost him dearly at the time regardless.

After Alexander's death in 323 BC he returned from exile and supported a final Greek revolt against Macedonian rule, the Lamian War. When it failed, he fled to a temple of Poseidon on the island of Calauria and took poison there rather than be captured and executed by Macedonian agents sent to arrest him. He was, on the one central question that had defined his entire career, proven right by events and yet ultimately unsuccessful in preventing them.
