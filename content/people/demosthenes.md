The greatest Athenian orator, who spent his career warning that Philip of Macedon meant to end Greek freedom — and was right.

<!-- field: significance -->
His speeches are the finest surviving Greek oratory and the fullest contemporary record of resistance to Macedon.

<!-- field: body -->
Demosthenes (384–322 BC) was the greatest of the Athenian orators. Orphaned at seven, he found as a young man that his guardians had squandered his inheritance, and he trained in rhetoric to sue them, winning part of it back. Plutarch says he cured a speech impediment by practising with pebbles in his mouth and speaking over the sound of the waves. He worked as a professional speechwriter before entering politics.

From the 350s BC he saw Philip II of Macedon as a deadly threat to Greek freedom. In the *Olynthiacs* and *Philippics* he urged the Athenians to act: to pay for their wars and fight in them themselves rather than relying on mercenaries. In 339 BC he built an alliance between Athens and Thebes, but Philip crushed it at Chaeronea in 338 BC, where Demosthenes fought. In his speech *On the Crown* (330 BC) he defended his whole career against his rival Aeschines and won.

In 324 BC he was convicted of taking money from Harpalus, Alexander's runaway treasurer, and went into exile; whether he was guilty is uncertain. After Alexander's death in 323 BC he returned to support the Greek revolt known as the Lamian War. When it failed, he fled to the island of Calauria and took poison in the temple of Poseidon rather than be captured by Antipater's men.

His speeches were studied as models of eloquence for two thousand years.
