<!-- field: summary -->
The last Achaemenid king, defeated by Alexander at Issus and Gaugamela and murdered by his own satrap.

<!-- field: significance -->
His defeat ended the two-century Achaemenid Empire and transferred the Near East to Macedonian rule.

<!-- field: body -->
Darius III, born Artashata, came to the throne in 336 BC, the same year as Alexander, placed there by the influential court eunuch Bagoas after a series of court poisonings had cleared away more direct claimants. Bagoas, having made one king, apparently tried to unmake him too: sources report that when Darius later discovered or suspected Bagoas was moving to poison him as well, he compelled the eunuch to drink the prepared poison himself instead, ending Bagoas's remarkable run of kingmaking permanently. Darius faced Alexander's invasion just two years into his reign.

At Issus in 333 BC his considerably larger army was caught fighting on a narrow coastal plain that gave it no room to use its numbers; Darius fled the field early, leaving his mother, wife and children to be captured in the Persian camp, whom Alexander then treated with conspicuous, almost performative respect and courtesy, a gesture calculated to present himself as a legitimate successor to Persian kingship rather than merely a foreign invader. Darius twice offered Alexander enormous territorial and financial concessions to end the war, including, by one account, all Persian territory west of the Euphrates, and was refused both times. At Gaugamela in 331 BC he chose flat, open ground and had it deliberately levelled to give his scythed chariots room to operate, and still lost decisively when Alexander drove the Companion cavalry through a gap that opened in the Persian line as it manoeuvred to counter a Macedonian flanking move.

Darius fled east into the Iranian highlands and was arrested and eventually stabbed by his own kinsman and satrap, Bessus, who hoped to rally continued resistance under his own claim to kingship. Alexander's advance force found Darius dying or already dead in an abandoned wagon, and Alexander had him buried with full royal honours at Persepolis before hunting down and executing Bessus himself — a sequence of actions that let Alexander present his conquest as legitimate succession and vengeance for a murdered king, not simply the destruction of a foreign empire.
