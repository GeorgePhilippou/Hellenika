<!-- field: summary -->
The most inventive Spartan commander of the Peloponnesian War, who broke Athenian control of the north and died winning Amphipolis.

<!-- field: significance -->
Brasidas showed Sparta could fight the war beyond the Peloponnese, and his death at Amphipolis removed the strongest opponent of peace.

<!-- field: body -->
Thucydides called Brasidas an energetic man, unusual for a Spartan in being a capable speaker. His first prominent action was at Methone in 431 BC, where he ran through an Athenian cordon to relieve the town, and was formally praised at Sparta. He later helped to relieve Megara in 424.

The northern campaign was his greatest achievement. He marched through Thessaly and Macedon in 424 with a small force made up in part of helots freed for the purpose, which Thucydides says Sparta was happy to send out of the country. He appealed to cities under Athenian rule by promising liberation and respect for their laws, and many of them, including Amphipolis, changed sides. Thucydides, commanding a fleet at Thasos, arrived a few hours late to save it, and was exiled. This gave him the leisure to write his history.

Brasidas's freedom of action was limited. Sparta's leaders wanted the prisoners taken at Sphacteria back, and once a truce was agreed, Brasidas kept campaigning against its terms. In 422, when Cleon arrived to recover Amphipolis, Brasidas attacked, won, and was fatally wounded. The city buried him within its walls, honoured him as its founder in place of the Athenian Hagnon, and offered yearly games and sacrifices.

Thucydides, generally sparing with praise, treats him as the one Spartan who understood what the war demanded.
