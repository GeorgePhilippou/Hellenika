<!-- field: summary -->
Last king of Lydia, proverbially rich, who tested the oracles and destroyed his own empire by trusting one.

<!-- field: significance -->
His kingdom minted the first true coinage, and his fall to Cyrus brought Persia to the Aegean coast and into contact with the Greeks.

<!-- field: body -->
Croesus (reigned c. 560–546 BC) was the last king of Lydia, the rich kingdom of western Asia Minor with its capital at Sardis. He brought the Greek cities of the Ionian coast under his rule, but was generous to Greek sanctuaries, sending lavish gifts to Delphi and helping rebuild the temple of Artemis at Ephesus. His wealth became proverbial: "rich as Croesus".

Lydia had invented coinage a generation or two earlier, striking electrum, a natural gold-silver alloy. Under Croesus it began issuing separate coins of pure gold and pure silver, the first two-metal currency.

Herodotus makes Croesus the subject of the first great story of his *Histories*. He tests the oracles by asking what he is doing on a certain day; only Delphi answers correctly. He then asks whether to attack Persia and is told that if he does, he will destroy a great empire. He attacks — and destroys his own. In 546 BC Cyrus the Great took Sardis. Herodotus tells how Croesus, placed on a pyre, called out the name of Solon, who had warned him that no one should be called happy until his life was over; Cyrus spared him and kept him as an adviser. The meeting with Solon cannot have happened as told: it is a moral tale about the instability of fortune.

Croesus' fall brought the Persian Empire to the Aegean coast and set the scene for the Persian Wars.
