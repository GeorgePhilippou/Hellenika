<!-- field: summary -->
The Venetian printer who, from 1495, published the first printed editions of most of the great Greek authors.

<!-- field: significance -->
His press turned the manuscripts saved from Byzantium into books that could be read across Europe, securing the survival of Greek literature.

<!-- field: body -->
Aldus Manutius (c. 1450–1515) was a humanist teacher from Bassiano, south of Rome. Around 1494 he set up a printing house in Venice, then the European centre of printing and, thanks to Bessarion's library and a large Greek community, of Greek learning. His aim was to print the Greek classics, which existed only in scattered manuscripts.

His first dated book, in 1495, was a Greek grammar by Constantine Lascaris. There followed the first printed editions of Aristotle in five volumes (1495–98), Aristophanes (1498), Thucydides, Herodotus and Sophocles (1502), Euripides (1503), Demosthenes (1504), and Plato and Pindar (1513). Many were edited by Greek scholars working with him, especially the Cretan Marcus Musurus. He also founded an academy of scholars who spoke Greek at meetings.

Aldus' press was technically innovative. His Greek type was modelled on contemporary Greek handwriting; his punchcutter Francesco Griffo created the first italic typeface; and from 1501 he printed classics in small octavo format that readers could carry, the ancestors of the paperback. His books bore his mark of a dolphin and anchor. By his death in 1515 the main body of Greek literature was in print.
