<!-- field: summary -->
Ionian philosopher, teacher of Pericles, who proposed Mind as the ordering force of the cosmos and was prosecuted at Athens for saying the sun was a stone.

<!-- field: significance -->
He brought Ionian natural philosophy to Athens itself and gave it a direct line into Athenian politics through his association with Pericles, while his trial demonstrated how dangerous purely physical explanations of the heavens could still be treated as impious.

<!-- field: body -->
Anaxagoras came from Clazomenae in Ionia but taught at Athens for roughly three decades, the first philosopher known to have done so, at a time when the city was becoming the artistic and intellectual centre it would remain for centuries. His physics held that everything was originally mixed together in infinitesimal portions of everything else, until Nous — Mind, a substance distinct from ordinary matter and not itself mixed with anything — set the mixture rotating, separating it out into the elements, bodies and world we observe.

He is equally remembered for two specific, strikingly correct astronomical claims: that the moon does not shine by its own light but reflects the sun's, and has mountains and valleys of its own, and — the claim that got him in serious trouble — that the sun itself is not a god but an incandescent mass of stone, larger than the Peloponnese. A meteorite that reportedly fell near Aegospotami around this time was cited as confirming evidence.

His close association with Pericles made him a target once Pericles' political opponents began attacking his circle; charged with impiety, he left Athens for Lampsacus, where the local citizens are said to have honoured him after his death by observing an annual holiday in his memory — a rather better reception than Athens itself had given him.
