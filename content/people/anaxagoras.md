<!-- field: summary -->
Ionian philosopher, teacher of Pericles, who proposed Mind as the ordering force of the cosmos and was prosecuted at Athens for saying the sun was a stone.

<!-- field: significance -->
He brought Ionian natural philosophy to Athens itself and gave it a direct line into Athenian politics through his association with Pericles, while his trial demonstrated how dangerous purely physical explanations of the heavens could still be treated as impious.

<!-- field: body -->
Anaxagoras (c. 500–428 BC) was a philosopher from Clazomenae in Ionia who moved to Athens around 460 BC and taught there for about thirty years, the first philosopher known to have done so. He was a close associate of Pericles, and Euripides may have heard him.

He taught that originally everything was mixed together, with a portion of everything in everything. Then *Nous* — Mind, a separate, unmixed substance — set the mixture spinning, and the rotation separated out the world as we know it. Mind as the ordering force of the cosmos influenced later philosophers, though Socrates complained in Plato's *Phaedo* that Anaxagoras then explained everything by mechanical causes instead.

He gave physical explanations of the heavens. He said, correctly, that the Moon shines by light reflected from the Sun and that eclipses are caused by shadows, and claimed that the Sun is a red-hot stone larger than the Peloponnese. A meteorite that fell at Aegospotami around 467 BC was linked in ancient sources to his ideas.

To say the Sun was a stone rather than a god could be treated as impiety. Probably as part of political attacks on Pericles, Anaxagoras was prosecuted and left Athens for Lampsacus on the Hellespont, where he died; the people of Lampsacus honoured him with a yearly holiday.
