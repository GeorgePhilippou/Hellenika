<!-- field: summary -->
Bronze sculptor from Sikyon who made the portraits of Alexander the Great and gave Greek statues their taller, lighter Hellenistic proportions.

<!-- field: significance -->
Lysippos moved sculpture from the compact ideal of the fifth century towards the open, spatial figures of the Hellenistic age, and gave Alexander his enduring face.

<!-- field: body -->
Lysippos came from Sikyon, near Corinth, and worked in the middle and late fourth century BC. Anecdote made him a self-taught craftsman who began as a bronze-worker, and claimed that his only teacher was nature. That is a tidy story, and it should be read as one.

Pliny preserves the clearest statement of his style. Earlier sculptors, he says, had made men as they were; Lysippos made them as they appear. His figures have smaller heads and slimmer bodies than Polykleitos's, and they seem to reach into the space around them instead of standing inside a block. In the Apoxyomenos, an athlete scrapes oil from an outstretched arm, so that the viewer must move round the figure to read it. The Vatican marble is a Roman copy, and its Lysippan origin is likely but not proven.

He is best known as Alexander's sculptor. Ancient writers report that Alexander would be portrayed in bronze by no one else, and Plutarch and Arrian say that Lysippos made the bronze portrait group of the horsemen who died at the Granicus. The turned head, upward gaze and lion-like hair of Alexander's later images probably come from his lost portraits, though Andrew Stewart cautions that most surviving heads are Hellenistic reworkings, not close copies.

Nothing certainly by his hand survives. His influence is visible instead in the Hellenistic portrait, the athlete statue and the ruler cult image.
