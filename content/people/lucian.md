<!-- field: summary -->
The Syrian-born Greek satirist of the 2nd century AD who mocked gods, philosophers and charlatans and imagined a voyage to the Moon.

<!-- field: significance -->
The great comic writer of the Roman empire, whose wit inspired Erasmus, Thomas More and Jonathan Swift.

<!-- field: body -->
Lucian (c. 125–after 180) was born at Samosata on the Euphrates, in the Roman province of Syria; his first language may have been Aramaic. In his own telling he was apprenticed to a sculptor uncle, ran away, and learned Greek rhetoric instead. He made a career as a travelling lecturer in Asia Minor, Greece, Italy and Gaul, settled for a time in Athens, and late in life held an official post in Egypt.

About eighty works survive under his name, in polished Attic Greek. Many are comic dialogues: the *Dialogues of the Gods* and *Dialogues of the Dead* make fun of mythology and of human vanity, with the Cynic philosopher Menippus mocking the dead rich. Others attack philosophers who do not live up to their teaching, and religious frauds — *Alexander the False Prophet* exposes the founder of a snake oracle, and *The Passing of Peregrinus* describes a Cynic who burned himself alive at Olympia, with an early pagan view of the Christians. *How to Write History* is a sharp guide to the historian's craft.

His *True History* parodies fantastic travel writing: the narrator sails beyond the Pillars of Heracles, is carried to the Moon by a whirlwind, and witnesses a war between the kings of the Moon and the Sun.
