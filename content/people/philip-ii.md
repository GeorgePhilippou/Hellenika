<!-- field: summary -->
The king who reforged Macedon's army, subdued Greece at Chaeronea, and planned the invasion of Persia his son carried out.

<!-- field: significance -->
Built the instrument of conquest. Alexander's campaigns are inconceivable without Philip's army, treasury and diplomatic groundwork.

<!-- field: body -->
Philip's education for kingship began, unusually, as a hostage. Sent to Thebes for roughly three years in his mid-teens as a guarantee of Macedonian good behaviour toward the dominant power in Greece at the time, he was held in the household of a leading Theban citizen and had the chance to observe at close range the tactics of Epaminondas, the general whose reformed phalanx formation had just broken Sparta's supposedly unbeatable army at Leuctra. Later historians have generally treated those years as the making of Philip's military education: whatever he absorbed from watching Thebes' innovations firsthand, he returned home in his early twenties with a clear sense of how a Greek army could be restructured to hit harder than anyone expected.

He took the throne in 359 BC amid a genuine crisis — his brother the previous king had just died in battle against invading Illyrians, leaving the kingdom militarily humiliated and its succession contested — and within two decades had turned Macedon into the strongest single power in mainland Europe. The military reforms underpinning that transformation were structural rather than incremental: infantry armed with the sarissa, a pike several times longer than a standard Greek spear, formed into a dense phalanx that could keep enemies at a distance no ordinary hoplite line could match; the Companion Cavalry, recruited from the Macedonian nobility, used as a decisive shock arm rather than the supporting role cavalry typically played in Greek warfare; a standing, professionally paid army that trained and campaigned year-round instead of citizen militias mobilised only for a campaigning season; and a siege train built around torsion catapults capable of reducing fortified cities that had previously been effectively unassailable.

Philip fought his battles with money and marriage as often as with the sarissa. Demosthenes, the Athenian orator who spent much of his career warning Greece about Macedonian ambition, complained bitterly that no fortress in Greece was truly impregnable so long as a mule loaded with enough silver could reach its gate — a fair description of how much Macedonian bribery, rather than Macedonian spears, secured for Philip. His marriages served the same strategic purpose as his bribes: he married at least seven times over his reign, to Illyrian, Thessalian, Epirote and other Macedonian noblewomen among others, with Olympias of Epirus, Alexander's mother, only one among several wives each of whom sealed a specific alliance rather than a personal romantic choice.

The wars themselves cost him physically in ways few kings of the period survived to tell. He lost an eye to an arrow at the siege of Methone, and ancient sources describe a severe leg wound from another campaign serious enough that it should show clearly on his skeleton, along with damage consistent with a shattered collarbone from yet another engagement — a body that by his mid-forties had absorbed more direct battlefield injury than most professional soldiers of the era. His decisive political and military victory came at Chaeronea in 338 BC, where a combined Macedonian force under Philip and the eighteen-year-old Alexander broke an alliance of Greek city-states including Athens and Thebes, ending mainland Greece's practical independence and allowing Philip to organise the defeated states into the League of Corinth under his own presidency, with the invasion of the Persian Empire already being planned as the League's shared project.

He never led that invasion himself. In 336 BC, at the wedding of his daughter at the old capital of Aegae, Philip was stabbed to death by one of his own bodyguards, a man named Pausanias, in circumstances that have never been fully explained: whether Pausanias acted from a purely personal grievance, as the version favourable to Alexander's court insisted, or was recruited by Olympias, by Alexander himself, or by agents of the Persian king with every reason to want Philip dead before his army crossed into Asia, remains genuinely disputed more than two thousand years later. In November 1977 the Greek archaeologist Manolis Andronikos opened a cluster of unlooted royal tombs at Vergina, ancient Aigai, and identified one of them, Tomb II, as Philip's own burial on the strength of its rich, unplundered grave goods and skeletal evidence including damage to the skull consistent with the eye wound at Methone — an identification most specialists still accept, though a vocal minority continues to argue the tomb instead belongs to Philip's son and short-lived successor Philip III Arrhidaeus.
