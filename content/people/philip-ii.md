<!-- field: summary -->
The king who reforged Macedon's army, subdued Greece at Chaeronea, and planned the invasion of Persia his son carried out.

<!-- field: significance -->
Built the instrument of conquest. Alexander's campaigns are inconceivable without Philip's army, treasury and diplomatic groundwork.

<!-- field: body -->
Philip's education for kingship began, unusually, as a hostage. In his mid-teens he was sent to Thebes for roughly three years as a guarantee of Macedonian good behaviour towards the dominant power in Greece. He lived in the household of a leading Theban and could observe at close range the tactics of Epaminondas, whose reformed phalanx had just broken Sparta at Leuctra. Later historians generally treat those years as the making of his military education. He returned home in his early twenties with a clear sense of how a Greek army could be restructured to hit harder than anyone expected.

He took the throne in 359 BC in a genuine crisis. His brother, the previous king, had just died in battle against invading Illyrians, leaving the kingdom militarily humiliated and its succession contested. Within two decades Philip had made Macedon the strongest power in mainland Europe.

The reforms behind that rise were structural, not incremental. Infantry carried the *sarissa*, a pike several times longer than a standard Greek spear, and formed a dense phalanx that could hold enemies at a distance no ordinary hoplite line could match. The Companion Cavalry, recruited from the Macedonian nobility, served as a decisive shock arm, not in the supporting role cavalry usually played in Greek warfare. A standing, professionally paid army trained and campaigned year-round, instead of citizen militias called up for a season. And a siege train built around torsion catapults could reduce fortified cities previously thought unassailable.

Philip fought as often with money and marriage as with the sarissa. Demosthenes, who spent much of his career warning Greece of Macedonian ambition, complained that no fortress was impregnable so long as a mule loaded with silver could reach its gate. He married at least seven times, to Illyrian, Thessalian, Epirote and Macedonian noblewomen. Olympias of Epirus, Alexander's mother, was one wife among several, each of whom sealed a particular alliance.

The wars cost him physically. He lost an eye to an arrow at the siege of Methone. Ancient sources describe a severe leg wound from another campaign, and a shattered collarbone from yet another. By his mid-forties his body had absorbed more direct injury than most professional soldiers of the era.

His decisive victory came at Chaeronea in 338 BC. A Macedonian force under Philip and the eighteen-year-old Alexander broke an alliance of Greek states that included Athens and Thebes. Mainland Greece lost its practical independence, and Philip organised the defeated states into the League of Corinth under his presidency, with the invasion of the Persian Empire already planned as its shared project.

He never led that invasion. In 336 BC, at the wedding of his daughter at the old capital of Aegae, he was stabbed to death by one of his own bodyguards, a man named Pausanias. The circumstances have never been fully explained. Pausanias may have acted from a personal grievance, as the version favourable to Alexander's court insisted. He may equally have been recruited by Olympias, by Alexander, or by agents of the Persian king, who had every reason to want Philip dead before his army crossed into Asia. The question remains open.

In November 1977 the Greek archaeologist Manolis Andronikos opened a cluster of unlooted royal tombs at Vergina, ancient Aigai. He identified Tomb II as Philip's, on the strength of its rich, unplundered grave goods and of skeletal evidence including skull damage consistent with the eye wound at Methone. Most specialists still accept the identification. A vocal minority argues that the tomb belongs instead to Philip's son Philip III Arrhidaeus, who reigned briefly after Alexander's death.
