<!-- field: summary -->
Roman consul who destroyed Corinth in 146 BC after crushing the Achaean League, earning the title Achaicus.

<!-- field: significance -->
The destruction of Corinth, in the same year Rome also destroyed Carthage, announced a harder Roman policy toward the Greek world than Flamininus's "freedom" a half-century earlier — resistance would now be punished with annihilation, not negotiation.

<!-- field: body -->
The Achaean League, Greece's last significant independent military power, provoked war with Rome by refusing to dissolve itself as Rome demanded. Mummius defeated it quickly and took Corinth, then one of the wealthiest cities in Greece. On explicit orders from the Senate, intended as an example to the rest of the Greek world, he had the male population killed, the women and children sold into slavery, the city's famous art collections looted, and the buildings burned.

Ancient writers, including Polybius, who witnessed the aftermath and helped organise the settlement of Greece afterward, portray Mummius as a competent soldier but a cultural blank slate — he is said to have warned the contractors shipping the looted artwork to Rome that if any piece were lost or damaged, they would have to provide an exact replacement, apparently unaware that the originals were irreplaceable. Whether the anecdote is strictly true or an exaggeration aimed at Roman philistinism generally, it stuck to his name. Corinth lay largely abandoned for a century until Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman colony in 44 BC.
