<!-- field: summary -->
Roman consul who destroyed Corinth in 146 BC after crushing the Achaean League, earning the title Achaicus.

<!-- field: significance -->
The destruction of Corinth, in the same year Rome also destroyed Carthage, announced a harder Roman policy toward the Greek world than Flamininus's "freedom" a half-century earlier — resistance would now be punished with annihilation, not negotiation.

<!-- field: body -->
Lucius Mummius Achaicus (2nd century BC) was a Roman politician and general, consul in 146 BC. That year he defeated the Achaean League, the last significant independent power in Greece, which had gone to war with Rome rather than accept Roman demands to break it up.

After defeating the League near the Isthmus, Mummius captured Corinth, one of the richest cities in Greece. On the orders of the Senate, as a warning to the Greeks, the city was destroyed: its men were killed, its women and children sold into slavery, its art treasures shipped to Rome and its buildings burned. Carthage was destroyed by Rome in the same year. Greece came under the authority of the Roman governor of Macedonia, ending Greek independence. Mummius took the surname Achaicus, "conqueror of Achaea", and celebrated a triumph in Rome.

Ancient writers portray him as an honest but uncultured soldier. The historian Velleius Paterculus tells how he warned the men shipping Corinth's masterpieces to Rome that if they lost any, they would have to replace them with new ones. True or not, the story became a byword for Roman philistinism. The historian Polybius, who saw the aftermath, helped organise the settlement of Greece that followed.

Corinth lay largely empty for a century, until Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman colony in 44 BC.
