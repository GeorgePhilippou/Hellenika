<!-- field: summary -->
Tyrant who made Syracuse the largest Greek city of the west and fought Carthage for control of Sicily.

<!-- field: significance -->
Dionysius created a Greek military monarchy on a new scale, and his career shaped later thinking about tyranny, including Plato's.

<!-- field: body -->
Dionysius came to power at Syracuse in 405 BC, during a crisis. Carthage was besieging Greek cities in the south of Sicily and the Syracusan generals had failed. He took the general's office, quickly won unlimited authority, and made peace, leaving Carthage in control of much of the island.

The peace did not last. He spent years preparing for war: building a strong fleet, recruiting mercenaries, and fortifying the city. Ortygia, the island citadel, became his palace; walls across the Epipolae, with the great fortress at Euryalus, made Syracuse difficult to besiege. In 397 he attacked the Carthaginian city of Motya, using new kinds of artillery, including an early catapult, which Diodorus says was invented in his workshops. His campaigns were up and down: Syracuse itself was besieged in 396 and rescued by an outbreak of plague in the Carthaginian camp. Later he extended his power to the toe of Italy, capturing Rhegion in 387.

Tyranny made his name a synonym for suspicion. Anecdotes describe a ruler who slept behind a moat and never let barbers near him; in one of them, a courtier is given a seat under a sword hung by a single hair. Plato visited about 388 and later tried to educate Dionysius's son; the resulting quarrel is one of the sources of Plato's political thought.

Dionysius also wrote poetry, and his tragedy won at Athens in the year he died. His son inherited a state too big for him to control.
