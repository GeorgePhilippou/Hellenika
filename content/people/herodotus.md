<!-- field: summary -->
Author of the Histories, the first sustained prose enquiry into the human past — an account of the Persian Wars that ranges across the known world.

<!-- field: significance -->
Invented history as a genre: a named investigator, weighing evidence and reporting conflicting versions rather than reciting tradition.

<!-- field: body -->
Herodotus (c. 484–425 BC) was born at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum), a Greek city on the coast of Caria in south-western Asia Minor, then under Persian rule. After involvement in local politics he left his home city, travelled widely, and spent time in Athens and at Thurii, a new Athenian-led colony in southern Italy, where he may have died.

His *Histories*, written around 440–425 BC, are the first great work of history in the Western tradition. His opening sentence sets out his aim: to record his *historie* — "enquiry" — so that the great deeds of Greeks and non-Greeks would not be forgotten, and to explain why they fought each other. The book follows the rise of the Persian Empire and its wars with the Greeks, ending with the defeat of Xerxes in 480–479 BC.

Herodotus travelled to Egypt, the Black Sea, Babylon and Libya, and his descriptions of other peoples have no earlier parallel in Greek writing. He gave public readings of parts of his work. His method is to report what his informants told him, giving rival versions and marking his own doubts. He was called "the father of history" by Cicero, and "the father of lies" by critics such as Plutarch.

Archaeology has since confirmed many of his details, such as Scythian burial customs, though his numbers, especially for the Persian armies, are greatly exaggerated.
