<!-- field: summary -->
Author of the Histories, the first sustained prose enquiry into the human past — an account of the Persian Wars that ranges across the known world.

<!-- field: significance -->
Invented history as a genre: a named investigator, weighing evidence and reporting conflicting versions rather than reciting tradition.

<!-- field: body -->
Herodotus was born in Halicarnassus, a Greek city on the Anatolian coast that spent much of his lifetime under Persian rule, a background that likely shaped his unusually even-handed, curious attitude toward the very empire his book's central narrative describes as the enemy. His opening sentence sets out the whole programme in a single breath: this is the *historie* — enquiry, or investigation — of Herodotus, set down so that human achievements are not forgotten with time and so that the reasons Greeks and non-Greeks went to war with one another are preserved for later generations. The Greek word *historia* originally just meant investigation in this general sense; it acquires something much closer to its modern meaning specifically through the influence of this one book.

He travelled widely for a man of his era, describing Egypt, Scythia, Babylon and Libya with an ethnographic curiosity that has essentially no earlier precedent in surviving Greek writing, and he is thought to have given public readings of sections of his still-developing work to audiences, including reportedly at Athens and even at the Olympic Games, well before it existed as a single finished text. His basic method throughout is to report what he was told by local informants while carefully marking his own personal doubt about it: he repeatedly insists that he is obliged to record what people say happened, without being obliged to believe all of it himself. Ancient critics, Plutarch prominent among them, accused him outright of being a liar and unfairly biased against various peoples he described; modern archaeology has since vindicated him on numerous specific details that once struck sceptical readers as pure fancy, including aspects of Egyptian religious and burial practice and, notably, Scythian nomadic burial customs later confirmed almost exactly by excavated grave sites on the Eurasian steppe.
