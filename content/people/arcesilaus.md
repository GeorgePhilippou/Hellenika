<!-- field: summary -->
Head of the Academy who turned Plato's school towards scepticism, arguing against the Stoics that certainty is unattainable.

<!-- field: significance -->
Arcesilaus made doubt a method of teaching, and forced the Stoics to sharpen their theory of knowledge in reply.

<!-- field: body -->
Arcesilaus was born at Pitane in Aeolis, studied in Athens and became head of the Academy about 268 BC. He reinterpreted Plato's Socrates as a questioner who claimed to know nothing, and taught in the same way, inviting a pupil to propose a thesis and then arguing against it, so that the student could see that reasons could be found on both sides.

His chief opponent was Zeno of Citium. Zeno claimed that the wise person could recognise certain impressions, which he called cognitive, as so clear and distinct that they must be true. Arcesilaus argued that for any such impression a false one indistinguishable from it could be found, so nothing is certain, and the wise person should suspend judgement on everything. The issue of how to act without certain knowledge led to the later Academic theory of the probable or reasonable.

He wrote nothing, so his positions are known through hostile or second-hand reports from Cicero, Plutarch and Sextus Empiricus. Scholars disagree about whether he was sincerely sceptical or simply a dialectician, using Stoic premises to embarrass them. Among those who heard him, according to Diogenes Laertius, was the young Chrysippus, who learned from him the skill of arguing both sides.

Ancient biographers describe a generous, witty, wealthy man with a love of Homer, who died after drinking too much undiluted wine.
