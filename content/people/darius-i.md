<!-- field: summary -->
Achaemenid king who organised the empire into satrapies, built Persepolis and the Royal Road, and lost at Marathon.

<!-- field: significance -->
His administrative system held an empire of perhaps 25 million people together, and his invasion of Greece began the Persian Wars.

<!-- field: body -->
Darius began his career as Cambyses II's spear-bearer, a position of personal trust rather than high rank, and rose to the throne in 522 BC only after joining six other Persian noblemen in killing a man who claimed to be Smerdis, Cyrus the Great's younger son, but whom Darius's own later inscriptions insist was an impostor — a magus named Gaumata who had impersonated the genuine Smerdis after his secret earlier murder. Whatever the truth of this politically convenient story, Darius justified his own disputed and rather thin claim to legitimacy at enormous length in the trilingual Behistun inscription, carved into a cliff face roughly 100 metres above a well-travelled road — a text whose parallel Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian versions gave nineteenth-century scholars, chiefly Henry Rawlinson, the key that finally unlocked cuneiform script.

He reorganised the empire into about twenty satrapies with fixed annual tribute, standardised coinage around the gold daric, and built the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, a route of some 2,700 km along which relay couriers are said by Herodotus to have covered the distance in about a week — a description of the system's speed and reliability, regardless of weather, that centuries later supplied the unofficial creed inscribed on the New York City Post Office building. His reach had real limits, though: a campaign north of the Danube against the nomadic Scythians ended in a costly, humiliating withdrawal, having failed to bring an elusive enemy to open battle at all.

The Ionian Revolt of 499 BC, in which Athens and Eretria briefly supported the rebel Greek cities of Anatolia, gave Darius his pretext for intervention in mainland Greece, reportedly instructing a servant to remind him three times at every meal, "Master, remember the Athenians." His punitive expedition was defeated at Marathon in 490 BC, and he was still assembling a much larger invasion force to avenge it when he died in 486 BC, leaving the project to his son Xerxes.
