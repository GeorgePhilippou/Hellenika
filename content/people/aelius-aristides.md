<!-- field: summary -->
The great orator of the 2nd century AD, whose Sacred Tales record his lifelong illness and his healing dreams sent by Asclepius.

<!-- field: significance -->
His speeches praise Rome and Athens; his Sacred Tales are the most personal record of religious experience to survive from paganism.

<!-- field: body -->
Aelius Aristides (117–c. 181) came from a rich family in Mysia in north-western Asia Minor and studied rhetoric in Athens, including with Herodes Atticus. In 143, on his way to make his name in Rome, he fell seriously ill, and he suffered for the rest of his life from a series of illnesses.

He turned to Asclepius, the god of healing, and spent long periods at his sanctuary at Pergamon, where patients slept in the temple to receive dreams. In his six *Sacred Tales* he recorded the god's instructions — cold baths in winter, fasts, vomiting, long runs — and his own devotion. The work is a unique window into ancient religious experience and, some think, anxiety and hypochondria.

He became one of the most admired orators of his time, though he refused to improvise like other sophists. His *Panathenaic Oration* celebrates Athens and its history; his speech *To Rome* praises the empire as a world of peace in which the whole earth is one city. When an earthquake devastated Smyrna in 177, his letter to Marcus Aurelius moved the emperor to tears and won funds to rebuild it. Byzantine teachers used his speeches as models of style.
