<!-- field: body -->
Themistocles (c. 524–459 BC) was the Athenian statesman who made Athens a naval power and led the Greek victory at Salamis. He came from an established but not aristocratic family, and was archon in 493 BC, when he began fortifying the harbour of Piraeus.

In 483 BC a rich new vein of silver was found at the state mines of Laurion. Themistocles persuaded the Assembly not to share it out among citizens but to build some 200 triremes, officially for a war with the island of Aegina. Three years later that fleet was Athens' defence against Xerxes. When the Persians came in 480 BC, he interpreted the Delphic oracle's "wooden wall" as the ships, had Athens evacuated, and held the Greek fleet together at Salamis. Before the battle he sent a servant to Xerxes with a false message that the Greeks were about to flee, luring the Persian fleet into the narrow straits, where its numbers counted against it.

After the war he had Athens' walls rebuilt over Spartan objections, delaying Spartan envoys while the work was rushed through. His power made enemies, and around 471 BC he was ostracised; a cache of 190 ostraka with his name, written by only a few hands, survives from an earlier vote. Condemned for treason, he fled and ended his life in the service of the Persian king Artaxerxes I, as governor of Magnesia in Asia Minor.

His navy, rowed by the poorest citizens, became the basis of Athens' empire and of its democracy.
