<!-- field: summary -->
The Seleucid king (175–164 BC) who invaded Egypt, was turned back by Rome, and provoked the Maccabean revolt in Judaea.

<!-- field: significance -->
Remembered in Jewish tradition as the archetypal persecutor; the revolt he provoked is commemorated in the festival of Hanukkah.

<!-- field: body -->
Antiochus IV (c. 215–164 BC) was a son of Antiochus III. After his father's defeat by Rome he spent about fourteen years as a hostage in Rome, then lived in Athens, and in 175 BC seized the Seleucid throne. He took the title *Epiphanes*, "god made manifest"; critics punned it into *Epimanes*, "madman".

In 170–168 BC he invaded Ptolemaic Egypt and was about to take Alexandria when a Roman envoy, Gaius Popillius Laenas, met him outside the city and handed him a decree of the Senate ordering him to leave. When Antiochus asked for time to consider, Popillius drew a circle around him in the sand and told him to answer before stepping out of it. Antiochus withdrew.

In Judaea, a conflict between rival high priests and between Hellenising and traditional Jews led him to intervene. In 167 BC he banned Jewish religious practices and rededicated the Temple in Jerusalem to Zeus Olympios. This provoked the revolt of the Maccabees, who recaptured and purified the Temple in 164.

Antiochus was a lavish patron of Greek culture. He staged a great festival at Daphne near Antioch and paid for the resumption of work on the temple of Olympian Zeus at Athens. He died on campaign in Iran in 164 BC.
