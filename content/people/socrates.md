<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian philosopher who wrote nothing, questioned everyone, and was executed by the democracy in 399 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
Turned philosophy from cosmology to ethics — how one ought to live — and made cross-examination the method of philosophical enquiry.

<!-- field: body -->
Socrates was the son of a stonemason, Sophroniscus, and a midwife, Phaenarete, and Plato has him compare his own method to hers: he delivered ideas, not children, drawing out beliefs a person already held and testing them rather than implanting knowledge from outside. He was famously ugly by Athenian standards — snub-nosed, bulging-eyed, often compared in the sources to a satyr — a contrast contemporaries clearly relished between his appearance and what Alcibiades, in Plato's *Symposium*, calls the extraordinary things that came out of him once he started talking.

He was no armchair figure. He served as a hoplite in three campaigns, at Potidaea, Delium and Amphipolis, and Alcibiades credited him with saving his life at Potidaea and standing his ground calmly during the panicked Athenian retreat from Delium. He also intervened, at real personal risk, in Athenian politics exactly twice: once in 406 BC as a member of the presiding council, when he alone refused to let the Assembly hold an illegal single trial of the generals who had failed to recover bodies after Arginusae, and again under the Thirty Tyrants, when he simply ignored an order to help arrest an innocent man for execution.

Socrates claimed to be guided by a *daimonion*, an inner divine voice that warned him away from actions rather than telling him what to do, and reported that the Delphic oracle had told his friend Chaerephon that no one was wiser than he was — a claim he says he spent the rest of his life testing by questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen, concluding each time that his only real advantage was knowing that he did not know. Unlike the sophists, he refused payment for his conversations, professed no doctrine of his own, and left behind a wife, Xanthippe, later caricatured as a shrew, and three sons.

He was tried in 399 BC for impiety and corrupting the young. The charge is inseparable from politics: Athens had just lost the war and endured the murderous rule of the Thirty Tyrants, several of whom, including Critias, had been in his circle, and under the general amnesty they could not be prosecuted directly for it. He was convicted by a narrow margin, deliberately proposed free meals for life at the Prytaneum as his counter-penalty rather than a plausible fine, and drank hemlock in prison surrounded by friends, a death scene Plato's *Phaedo* renders as calm philosophical instruction to the end.

Everything known about him comes through others — Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes — who disagree substantially, from Aristophanes' comic portrait of him in *Clouds* as a cloud-headed natural philosopher running a "Thinkery" to Plato's austere moral teacher and Xenophon's practical, rather conventional gentleman. This gap between sources is the "Socratic problem," and it means the historical Socrates is, to a real degree, permanently obscured behind the very different Socrates each of his students needed him to be.
