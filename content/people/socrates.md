<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian philosopher who wrote nothing, questioned everyone, and was executed by the democracy in 399 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
Turned philosophy from cosmology to ethics — how one ought to live — and made cross-examination the method of philosophical enquiry.

<!-- field: body -->
Socrates was born in Athens around 470 BC, the son of a stonemason, Sophroniscus, and a midwife, Phaenarete. He lived through Athens' greatest years and its defeat in the Peloponnesian War, and spent his life in the city's streets and gymnasia, questioning anyone who would talk to him. He wrote nothing.

His method was cross-examination. He asked people who claimed to know what courage, justice or piety were to define them, then showed that their answers contradicted each other. He said that when the oracle at Delphi declared no one wiser than him, he set out to test it, and concluded that his only advantage was knowing that he did not know. Unlike the paid teachers called sophists, he took no fees and offered no doctrine. He turned philosophy away from questions about the physical world towards ethics: how one ought to live, and whether virtue can be known and taught.

He was also a citizen-soldier who served as a hoplite at Potidaea, Delium and Amphipolis. He twice defied authority at personal risk: in 406 BC he alone opposed the illegal mass trial of the generals after Arginusae, and under the Thirty he refused an order to arrest an innocent man.

In 399 BC he was tried for impiety and corrupting the young. The trial had a political background: several of his followers, including Alcibiades and Critias, leader of the Thirty, had harmed the city. He was convicted by a narrow margin. Asked to propose a penalty, he first suggested free meals at public expense, then a fine of thirty minas guaranteed by his friends (Plato, *Apology* 36d–38b); the jury chose death, and he drank hemlock in prison.

He is known only through others — above all Plato and Xenophon, and Aristophanes' mocking portrait in *Clouds* — whose accounts differ. Separating the real Socrates from their versions is the "Socratic problem".
