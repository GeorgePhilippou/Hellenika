<!-- field: summary -->
Macedonian general who governed Macedon and Greece as regent throughout Alexander's entire Asian campaign, and again after his death.

<!-- field: significance -->
His decade holding Macedon together while Alexander campaigned thousands of miles away made the entire expedition possible; his final choice of successor, passing over his own son, set off the war that followed his death.

<!-- field: body -->
A trusted lieutenant of Philip II before he ever served Alexander, Antipater was left in charge of Macedon and the League of Corinth when the invasion of Asia began in 334 BC — a decade-long assignment that required suppressing unrest, managing Greek cities that resented Macedonian control, and sending reinforcements east. His most serious test came in 331 BC, when the Spartan king Agis III raised a revolt; Antipater defeated and killed him at Megalopolis while Alexander was deep in Persia.

Alexander's death in 323 BC handed him a harder problem: Athens and other cities launched the Lamian War, betting that Macedonian power would collapse without its king. Antipater was besieged in Lamia before reinforcements from Asia relieved him and the revolt was broken; Athens was forced into an oligarchy under Macedonian garrison, and Demosthenes, its leading anti-Macedonian voice, died soon after rather than be taken.

His death of old age in 319 BC should have passed power smoothly to his son Cassander. Instead he named the general Polyperchon regent, judging him fitter to hold the empire together — a decision Cassander refused to accept, and which reignited the wars among Alexander's successors that Antipater's own decades in Macedon had, until then, kept largely out of Europe.
