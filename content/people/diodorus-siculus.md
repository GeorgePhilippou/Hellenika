<!-- field: summary -->
The Sicilian Greek historian of the 1st century BC whose universal history preserves material from many lost sources.

<!-- field: significance -->
For long stretches of Greek history, including the wars after Alexander, his compilation is the only continuous narrative that survives.

<!-- field: body -->
Diodorus came from Agyrium in Sicily and lived in the 1st century BC, in the last decades of the Roman Republic. He visited Egypt around 60–56 BC and later lived in Rome. Over some thirty years he compiled the *Bibliotheca historica* ("Library of History"), a universal history in 40 books from the mythical past to Julius Caesar's conquests in Gaul. Books 1–5 and 11–20 survive complete.

Diodorus was a compiler rather than an original historian. He worked through earlier writers one at a time — Ephorus and Theopompus for the 4th century BC, Hieronymus of Cardia for the wars of Alexander's successors, Timaeus for Sicily — arranging events year by year. His chronology is often confused and he contradicts himself, but because almost all his sources are lost, his work is invaluable. For the Successors after 323 BC, it is the main narrative we have.

He believed history should teach moral lessons and saw the past of all peoples — Egyptians, Mesopotamians, Greeks and Romans — as one connected story.
