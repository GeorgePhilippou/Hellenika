<!-- field: summary -->
The boy-king of Egypt (204–180 BC) whose coronation decree is inscribed on the Rosetta Stone.

<!-- field: significance -->
His reign marks the turning point when Ptolemaic power declined; it is remembered today through the Rosetta Stone.

<!-- field: body -->
Ptolemy V (210–180 BC) was about six when his father Ptolemy IV died in 204 BC. The courtiers who had concealed the death murdered his mother and ruled in his name, until a riot in Alexandria lynched them. The weakness of the regency invited attack: Antiochus III of the Seleucid empire and Philip V of Macedon agreed to divide Ptolemaic possessions, and in 200 BC Antiochus defeated the Egyptian army at Panium and took southern Syria and Palestine.

At the same time, much of Upper Egypt was in revolt under native rulers who claimed to be pharaohs, based at Thebes, from 206 to 186 BC. The government needed the support of the Egyptian priests. In 196 BC Ptolemy was crowned at Memphis in the traditional Egyptian rite, and the priests, assembled there, passed a decree praising his gifts to the temples and his victories over rebels. It was inscribed on stone in hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek; one copy is the Rosetta Stone.

In 193 BC he married Cleopatra I, daughter of Antiochus III. He died in 180 BC, perhaps poisoned, and Cleopatra ruled as regent for their young son.
