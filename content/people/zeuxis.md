<!-- field: summary -->
The painter of the late 5th century BC whose illusionistic realism was so famous that birds were said to peck at his painted grapes.

<!-- field: significance -->
A pioneer of shading and illusion in painting, who made realism the goal of Greek painters.

<!-- field: body -->
Zeuxis came from Heraclea, probably the Greek city in southern Italy, and worked from about 435 to 390 BC in Athens, Macedon and elsewhere. None of his paintings survives; we know them from writers such as Pliny the Elder and Lucian.

He was celebrated for developing light and shade (*skiagraphia*) to make figures look solid and real. Pliny tells a famous story of a contest between Zeuxis and his rival Parrhasius. Zeuxis painted grapes so lifelike that birds flew down to peck at them. Then he asked Parrhasius to draw back the curtain over his own painting — only to find that the curtain was the painting. Zeuxis conceded: he had deceived birds, but Parrhasius had deceived a painter.

Another story says that to paint Helen of Troy for the city of Croton, he chose the five most beautiful girls of the city as models and combined their best features — an idea of ideal beauty much quoted by Renaissance artists. He painted unusual subjects, such as a family of centaurs, and decorated the palace of King Archelaus at Pella.

He became rich and famous, and was said to have died laughing at a painting he had made of an old woman.
