<!-- field: summary -->
The Alexandrian physician of the 3rd century BC who studied the heart, the brain and the nerves through dissection.

<!-- field: significance -->
With Herophilus, the founder of anatomy and physiology, who came close to understanding the circulation of the blood.

<!-- field: body -->
Erasistratus (c. 304–250 BC) came from the island of Ceos and worked at the Seleucid court and then at Alexandria, a younger contemporary of Herophilus. His writings are lost, and his ideas are known mainly from Galen, who argued with them at length.

Through dissection he made important discoveries. He described the valves of the heart and understood that the heart works as a pump, and traced the veins and arteries, though he believed that arteries carried air (*pneuma*) rather than blood. He distinguished the brain from the cerebellum, and sensory nerves from motor nerves, and noted that the folds of the human brain are more complex than those of animals, linking this to intelligence.

He rejected the Hippocratic theory of the four humours, explaining disease instead by blockages and excess of blood, and preferred diet and exercise to the bleeding favoured by others.

A famous story told how, as a court physician, he discovered that the young Seleucid prince Antiochus was wasting away for love of his stepmother Stratonice, by noticing that his pulse quickened when she came near. King Seleucus I gave her to his son in marriage.
