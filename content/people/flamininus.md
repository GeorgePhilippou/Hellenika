<!-- field: summary -->
Roman general who defeated Philip V at Cynoscephalae in 197 BC and then declared the Greek cities free at the Isthmian Games.

<!-- field: significance -->
His declaration of Greek freedom, however qualified by Roman oversight in practice, marked Rome's first major intervention in the Greek world as arbiter rather than conqueror — a role it would abandon within a generation.

<!-- field: body -->
Flamininus was unusually young for a Roman consul and unusually philhellene for a Roman general, reportedly fluent in Greek and genuinely engaged with Greek culture rather than merely conquering it. Sent to fight Philip V of Macedon, he won the decisive battle at Cynoscephalae in 197 BC, breaking Macedonian power in Greece for a generation.

The following year, at the Isthmian Games — one of the great pan-Hellenic festivals — a herald announced that Rome restored freedom, autonomy and immunity from tribute to the Greek cities that had been under Macedonian control. Ancient accounts describe an eruption of gratitude so loud it was said to have startled birds out of the sky. Rome's freedom proved to mean freedom to align with Roman interests rather than full independence, but the gesture, and Flamininus personally, were honoured across Greece for years afterward, including cults in his name.

He remained active in Greek affairs, helping curb Antiochus III's ambitions there, and was later sent to press the Bithynian king harboring the exiled Carthaginian general Hannibal to give him up; Hannibal took poison rather than be captured, an end later writers laid at Flamininus's door.
