<!-- field: summary -->
The immensely rich Athenian orator and Roman consul of the 2nd century AD who filled Greece with public buildings.

<!-- field: significance -->
The greatest private benefactor of Roman Greece and a leading figure of the Second Sophistic.

<!-- field: body -->
Herodes Atticus (c. 101–177) was born at Marathon into an old Athenian family that had become enormously rich; his father was said to have found a hoard of treasure on his estate. He studied rhetoric with the leading sophists of the day and became the most famous orator of his generation. He held office in Asia Minor, was tutor to the future emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and in 143 became consul at Rome — one of the first Greeks to hold the office.

He spent his fortune on public buildings. In Athens he rebuilt the Panathenaic Stadium in white marble and built the Odeon at the foot of the Acropolis, a roofed theatre for music, in memory of his Roman wife Regilla; it is still used for performances today. At Olympia he and Regilla built a monumental fountain (nymphaeum) that brought fresh water to the sanctuary, and he gave buildings to Delphi, Corinth and other cities.

He was quarrelsome and resented by many Athenians, who accused him before the emperor of tyranny. When Regilla died in 160, pregnant, after being struck by one of his freedmen, her brother charged Herodes with murder; he was acquitted. Philostratus' *Lives of the Sophists* gives a vivid portrait of him.
