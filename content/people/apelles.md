<!-- field: summary -->
The court painter of Alexander the Great, regarded in antiquity as the greatest of all painters — none of whose works survives.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous painter of the ancient world, whose lost works set the standard by which painting was judged for centuries.

<!-- field: body -->
Apelles, probably from Colophon in Ionia, trained at Ephesus and Sicyon and worked in the second half of the 4th century BC. He became court painter to Philip II and then Alexander the Great, who, according to Pliny, would allow no one else to paint his portrait, just as only Lysippos might sculpt him and only Pyrgoteles engrave his image on gems.

Ancient writers praised his grace and his skill at lifelike images, made with a palette of only four colours. His most famous works included a portrait of Alexander holding a thunderbolt, painted for the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, whose hand seemed to stand out from the picture; and an *Aphrodite Anadyomene*, "rising from the sea", later taken to Rome by Augustus. A famous anecdote tells how he and the painter Protogenes competed by drawing ever finer lines on a panel.

He is credited with the saying "no day without a line" and with telling a shoemaker who criticised a painted sandal and then the leg: "Let the cobbler stick to his last."

None of his paintings survives. His reputation, preserved by Pliny and others, inspired Renaissance painters such as Botticelli, whose *Calumny of Apelles* recreates one of his described works.
