<!-- field: summary -->
The poet of Ceos who wrote the memorials of the Persian Wars, and was said to have been the first to compose for pay.

<!-- field: significance -->
The voice of Greek remembrance for the dead of Marathon, Thermopylae and Plataea, and the legendary inventor of the art of memory.

<!-- field: body -->
Simonides (c. 556–468 BC) came from the island of Ceos in the Cyclades and worked across the Greek world, for the tyrant Hipparchus in Athens, for noble families in Thessaly, and finally for Hieron of Syracuse, where he died. He wrote choral songs, victory odes, laments, elegies and epigrams, and was said to be the first poet to write for a fee.

He is above all the poet of the Persian Wars. Epigrams for the dead of Marathon and Thermopylae were attributed to him, including the famous epitaph for the Spartans: "Stranger, tell the Spartans that here we lie, obedient to their words." Whether he wrote all of these is uncertain. In 1992 papyri from Oxyrhynchus revealed parts of a long elegy he wrote on the battle of Plataea, comparing the Greek dead to the heroes of Troy.

Ancient anecdotes made him clever and greedy. Cicero tells that when a banqueting hall collapsed, killing everyone inside, Simonides, called outside moments before, could identify the crushed bodies by remembering where each guest had sat — and so invented the "art of memory", the technique of placing things to be remembered in imagined locations.

His nephew Bacchylides was also a famous poet, and a rival of Pindar.
