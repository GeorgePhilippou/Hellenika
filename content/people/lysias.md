<!-- field: summary -->
The Athenian speechwriter, a resident foreigner whose family was ruined by the Thirty Tyrants, famous for his clear and natural style.

<!-- field: significance -->
The master of plain Attic prose, whose courtroom speeches give vivid glimpses of everyday life and law in Athens.

<!-- field: body -->
Lysias (c. 445–380 BC) was the son of Cephalus, a wealthy shield-maker from Syracuse who settled in Athens as a metic, or resident foreigner, at the invitation of Pericles. Plato's *Republic* is set in the family's house in Piraeus. As a young man Lysias went to Thurii in southern Italy, returning to Athens after the Sicilian disaster.

In 404 BC the Thirty Tyrants, seeking money, arrested the rich metics. Lysias' brother Polemarchus was executed and the family's property seized; Lysias escaped and helped fund the democratic exiles. When democracy returned he was briefly granted citizenship, but it was annulled. In his speech *Against Eratosthenes* he prosecuted one of the Thirty for his brother's death.

As a metic Lysias could not speak in the Assembly, so he made his living as a *logographos*, writing speeches for citizens to deliver themselves in court. About 35 survive. Famous for their simple, clear language and their skill in suiting each speech to its speaker, they cover murder, wounding, inheritance and the scrutiny of officials. *On the Murder of Eratosthenes* tells of a husband who killed his wife's lover, and gives a vivid picture of an Athenian household.
