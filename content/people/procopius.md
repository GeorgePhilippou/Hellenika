<!-- field: summary -->
The historian of Justinian's wars, whose Secret History reveals what he really thought of the emperor and empress.

<!-- field: significance -->
The last great historian of antiquity in the classical tradition, and our chief witness to the age of Justinian.

<!-- field: body -->
Procopius (c. 500–after 565) came from Caesarea in Palestine and trained in law. In 527 he became legal adviser and secretary to Belisarius, Justinian's leading general, and went with him on campaign against the Persians, the Vandals in North Africa, and the Ostrogoths in Italy, until about 540. He was in Constantinople during the great plague of 542, which he described in detail.

His *Wars*, in eight books, follow the model of Herodotus and Thucydides, written in classicising Greek and full of speeches, battles and descriptions of peoples. His *Buildings* is a work of flattery, praising Justinian's construction projects, including a famous description of Hagia Sophia.

His third work, the *Anekdota* ("unpublished things"), known as the *Secret History*, is utterly different. Written in secret around 550, it portrays Justinian as a demon in human form who ruined the empire, Theodora as a shameless prostitute, and Belisarius as a weakling ruled by his wife. It circulated privately and was printed only in 1623 from a manuscript in the Vatican Library. Historians still debate how to reconcile the three faces of Procopius.
