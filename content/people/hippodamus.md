<!-- field: summary -->
A fifth-century BC town planner from Miletus, credited with laying out the Piraeus on a regular grid and with an early theory of the ideal state.

<!-- field: significance -->
Traditionally the father of town planning, though the grid plan was older than he was.

<!-- field: body -->
Hippodamus came from Miletus, a city rebuilt on a regular plan after its destruction by the Persians in 494 BC. He was active in the middle of the fifth century BC and, according to Aristotle, took an interest in politics as well as design.

Ancient writers attribute to him the layout of the Piraeus, the port of Athens, with straight streets and separate zones, and later the new colony of Thurii in Italy and the city of Rhodes. The excavated remains of these places are consistent with careful planning, though the evidence for his own role is thin. Cities planned on a grid existed in the west before him, for example at Megara Hyblaea, and he is best seen as someone who developed and popularised these ideas.

Aristotle's *Politics* describes his ideal state, in which 10,000 citizens would be divided into artisans, farmers and soldiers, and the land into sacred, public and private parts. Aristotle criticised it as impractical and open to abuse. Grid-planned cities are still called Hippodamian.
