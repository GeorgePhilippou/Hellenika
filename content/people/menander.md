<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian comic dramatist whose plays of mistaken identity, family quarrels and reconciliation set the pattern for European comedy.

<!-- field: significance -->
Menander moved comedy from political satire to private life, and his plots reached Rome and then the modern stage.

<!-- field: body -->
Menander was born at Athens in 342 or 341 BC and wrote for the theatre from his early twenties. By then the city had lost real independence, and his plays reflect a world in which the household, not the assembly, is the stage of drama. The old comedy of Aristophanes had attacked politicians by name; New Comedy stays inside the family, with fathers and sons, young lovers, slaves, soldiers and courtesans, and a story that turns on misunderstanding and ends in recognition or marriage.

For most of history his work was known only from quotations and from Roman versions by Plautus and Terence. Since 1905 papyri from Egypt have changed that. The Cairo codex added long stretches of Samia, Epitrepontes and Perikeiromene; in 1958 the Bodmer papyrus published Dyskolos in nearly complete form, along with the record that it took first prize in 316 BC. It is a light story of a misanthropic old farmer, and it is the only Menander play we can read from start to finish.

The plays are quiet and observational. Speech is natural, characters are seldom plain villains, and ethical questions, such as whether a man should acknowledge a child, are decided through feeling as much as rule. The ancients admired this: Aristophanes of Byzantium reportedly asked who imitated whom, life or Menander.

He died young, in his early fifties, about 290 BC. Tradition says he drowned while swimming at Piraeus.
