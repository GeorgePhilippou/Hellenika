<!-- field: summary -->
The physician of Kos whose name attaches to a body of medical writing that separated disease from divine punishment.

<!-- field: significance -->
Established medicine as a discipline based on observation, prognosis and ethical obligation rather than religious healing.

<!-- field: body -->
The Hippocratic Corpus is around sixty works by many hands over more than a century; how much Hippocrates himself wrote is unknown. What unifies it is a naturalistic stance. *On the Sacred Disease* attacks the idea that epilepsy is divine: it has a natural cause like every other illness, and those who call it sacred are concealing their ignorance.

The clinical case histories in the *Epidemics* are careful daily observations that often end with the patient dying — recorded anyway, because the point was to learn the course of disease. The Oath, with its commitments to confidentiality and to doing no harm, remains the ancestor of medical ethics, though it is unlikely to be by Hippocrates.

The underlying humoral theory was wrong, and it dominated European medicine for two thousand years.
