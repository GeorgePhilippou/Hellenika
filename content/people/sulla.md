<!-- field: summary -->
Roman general who stormed Athens in 86 BC during the war against Mithridates of Pontus, and later became dictator of Rome.

<!-- field: significance -->
His siege and sack of Athens caused physical and institutional damage the city never fully recovered from, ending its centuries as a genuine political power even as it remained a revered centre of philosophy.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens allied with Mithridates VI of Pontus against Rome, calculating that a rising eastern power offered a better chance of freedom than Roman rule had. Sulla besieged the city through a brutal winter — sources describe the population reduced to boiling shoe leather for food — before storming it on 1 March 86 BC. He is said to have stopped his own troops from continuing a massacre in the Kerameikos, remarking that he spared the many for the sake of the few, a reference to the city's past rather than any present merit.

He needed timber for siege works and took it from the sacred groves of the Academy and the Lyceum, the philosophical schools founded by Plato and Aristotle, and shipped Aristotle's own library back to Rome among the plunder — a loss later scholars have mourned as much as the human cost. In the same year he defeated Mithridates' field armies in Greece at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, securing the province.

Sulla's later career took him back to Rome, where he marched on the city itself — the first Roman general to do so — and ruled as dictator from 82 to 79 BC, using the office to purge his political enemies before retiring and dying in 78 BC. His Greek campaign remains the episode most directly relevant to the Greek world he had, by his own account, saved from Mithridates while wrecking Athens to do it.
