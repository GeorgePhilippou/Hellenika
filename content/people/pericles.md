<!-- field: summary -->
The dominant Athenian politician for three decades, who rebuilt the Acropolis, extended democratic participation and led Athens into the Peloponnesian War.

<!-- field: significance -->
His name marks the height of Athenian power, and the buildings he commissioned remain the visual signature of Greek civilisation.

<!-- field: body -->
Pericles was born around 495 BC into the Athenian aristocracy. His father Xanthippus commanded the Greek fleet at Mycale in 479 BC, and his mother belonged to the Alcmaeonids, the family of Cleisthenes, founder of the democracy. He was taught by the philosopher Anaxagoras.

From the early 450s BC until his death he was the leading figure in Athens. His power rested on being elected *strategos* (general) almost every year and on persuading the Assembly; he held no permanent office above any other citizen. Thucydides judged that Athens was "in name a democracy, but in fact rule by the first man".

He deepened the democracy, introducing pay for jurors so that poor citizens could serve. In 451 BC he carried a law limiting citizenship to children of two Athenian parents — a law that later caught his own son by Aspasia of Miletus, his partner, until the Assembly made an exception. He moved the Delian League's treasury to Athens and used allied tribute to fund the rebuilding of the Acropolis, with the Parthenon, the Propylaea and Pheidias' statue of Athena, despite critics who said Athens was dressing itself up with its allies' money.

When war with Sparta broke out in 431 BC, Pericles' strategy was to bring the people of Attica inside the walls, avoid battle on land and rely on the navy. Thucydides gives him the Funeral Oration for the first year's dead, the most famous statement of Athenian democratic ideals. But the crowded city was struck by plague in 430 BC; Pericles lost his two elder sons and died himself in 429 BC.

The great buildings of his era made his name a byword for Athens' golden age, the "Age of Pericles".
