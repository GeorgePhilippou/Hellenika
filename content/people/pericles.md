<!-- field: summary -->
The dominant Athenian politician for three decades, who rebuilt the Acropolis, extended democratic participation and led Athens into the Peloponnesian War.

<!-- field: significance -->
His name marks the height of Athenian power, and the buildings he commissioned remain the visual signature of Greek civilisation.

<!-- field: body -->
Pericles came from the Athenian political aristocracy on both sides. His father Xanthippus had commanded the fleet at Mycale in 479 BC, and his mother belonged to the Alcmaeonids, a family closely tied to the overthrow of the tyranny and the origins of Athenian democracy.

From 461 BC until shortly before his death, he was repeatedly elected *strategos*, or general. The office was elective and could be renewed indefinitely, and it was the whole basis of his authority. He held no permanent power greater than any other citizen's, and had to win the assembly's vote again and again for essentially his entire career.

He used that mandate to reshape public life. He introduced pay for jury service, so that citizens too poor to lose a day's wages could take part in the courts. He moved the Delian League's treasury from Delos to Athens and spent a substantial share of allied tribute on the Parthenon, the Propylaea and the Odeon. Critics, according to Plutarch, said this dressed Athens up "like a harlot" with money paid by the allies for their common defence. Thucydides gives him the Funeral Oration for the war dead of 431 BC, the most quoted statement of Athenian democratic self-understanding to survive. Yet the same historian judged his government "in name a democracy, but in fact rule by the first man". He saw no contradiction in holding both views.

In 451 BC Pericles carried a law limiting citizenship to children of two Athenian parents. Before this, descent through the father alone had been enough. The measure is usually read as narrowing the aristocratic marriage networks between Athens and other cities, the kind his own family had once belonged to.

The law caught up with him. From the 440s his companion was Aspasia, an immigrant from Miletus whom he never married, but treated, by all report, as something close to an intellectual equal. Comic playwrights made her a running target of sexual insult and accused her of corrupting his judgement. Under his own law, their son could not be an Athenian citizen. When Pericles' two sons by his Athenian marriage died in the same epidemic that killed him, the assembly voted a special exception to legitimise the son of Aspasia, so that the family would not die out. The law he had persuaded the assembly to pass was undone in his own household, at the assembly's request.

At the outbreak of war with Sparta in 431 BC his strategy was to abandon the Attic countryside, bring the rural population inside the walls, rely on the fleet and the Long Walls to Piraeus to keep the city supplied, and avoid open battle against Sparta's stronger land army. It was militarily sound, and a factor no general could have planned for undid it. Crowding all of Attica into the city created ideal conditions for an epidemic. A devastating plague broke out in 430 and killed a substantial fraction of the population, Pericles himself among them in 429. It removed, at the worst moment, the one leader with enough authority to keep his strategy from being abandoned by less patient successors.
