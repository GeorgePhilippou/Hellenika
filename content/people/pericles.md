<!-- field: summary -->
The dominant Athenian politician for three decades, who rebuilt the Acropolis, extended democratic participation and led Athens into the Peloponnesian War.

<!-- field: significance -->
His name marks the height of Athenian power, and the buildings he commissioned remain the visual signature of Greek civilisation.

<!-- field: body -->
Pericles came from Athens' political aristocracy on both sides: his father Xanthippus had commanded the Athenian fleet at the victory of Mycale in 479 BC, and his mother belonged to the Alcmaeonids, one of the families most closely associated with the earlier overthrow of tyranny and the origins of Athenian democracy. He was elected *strategos* — general — repeatedly from 461 BC until shortly before his death three decades later. That office, unlike almost every other position in the Athenian constitution, was both elective and indefinitely re-electable, and it was the entire base of his authority; he held no permanent constitutional power greater than any other citizen and had to win the assembly's vote for the generalship again and again, year after year, for essentially the whole of his political career.

He used that repeatedly renewed mandate to reshape Athenian public life. He introduced pay for jury service, which made participation in the courts financially possible for citizens too poor to give up a day's wages otherwise, and he moved the Delian League's treasury from the island of Delos to Athens itself, then spent a substantial share of allied tribute on the Parthenon, the Propylaea gateway and the Odeon concert hall — a building programme critics at the time, according to Plutarch, denounced as dressing Athens up "like a harlot" with money contributed by allied cities for their own common defence rather than for Athenian monuments. Thucydides gives him the Funeral Oration for the war dead of the Peloponnesian War's first year, the single most quoted statement of Athenian democratic self-understanding to survive from antiquity, while in the same history judging Pericles' actual government to have been "in name a democracy, but in fact rule by the first man" — two verdicts on the same man that Thucydides apparently saw no contradiction in holding at once.

In 451 BC Pericles carried a law restricting Athenian citizenship to children born of two Athenian parents, tightening a status that had previously been available more loosely through the father's line alone — a measure generally read as narrowing the pool of families who could forge marriage alliances between Athens' aristocracy and that of other cities, and so curbing exactly the kind of inter-city aristocratic network Pericles' own family had once belonged to. The law caught up with him personally in the most direct way possible. His long-term companion from around the 440s BC was Aspasia, an immigrant from Miletus whom he never married but treated, by all report, as something close to an intellectual equal — an arrangement scandalous enough on its own terms that comic playwrights made her a running target of sexual insult and even accused her of corrupting Pericles' judgement, but which also meant that under Pericles' own citizenship law, their son could not legally be an Athenian citizen at all, since his mother was Milesian rather than Athenian. When Pericles' two sons by his legitimate Athenian marriage both died in the same epidemic that would shortly kill Pericles himself, the Athenian assembly took the unusual step of voting a special exception to legitimise his son by Aspasia, so that the family name would not simply die out — the same citizenship law Pericles had pushed through the assembly a generation earlier undone, in his own household, at the request of the assembly he had spent his career persuading.

His strategy at the outbreak of war with Sparta in 431 BC — abandon the indefensible Attic countryside, crowd the rural population inside Athens' walls, rely on the fleet and the Long Walls to Piraeus to keep the city supplied by sea, and simply wait Sparta's invading armies out rather than risk open battle against Sparta's superior land forces — was militarily sound and was undone by a variable no general could have planned for. Overcrowding the entire population of Attica inside the city walls created ideal conditions for an epidemic, and a devastating plague broke out in 430 BC, killing a substantial fraction of the population over the following years, Pericles himself among them in 429 BC — removing, at the worst possible moment, the one Athenian leader with enough sustained personal authority to keep the wartime strategy he had designed from being abandoned by his less patient successors.
