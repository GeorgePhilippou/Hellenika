<!-- field: summary -->
The Titan who overthrew his father Uranus, swallowed his own children and was in turn defeated by Zeus.

<!-- field: myth -->
Kronos is the youngest Titan child of Gaia and Uranus. Armed with Gaia's sickle, he castrates Uranus and takes power. Warned that one of his own children will overthrow him, he swallows Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon as they are born. Rhea saves the youngest, Zeus, by giving Kronos a wrapped stone. Zeus later forces him to release his siblings and defeats the Titans in the Titanomachy.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* supplies the principal early narrative. The *Works and Days* associates Kronos' reign with the first, Golden race of mortals.

<!-- field: religious -->
Kronos was not only a defeated literary villain. The Kronia, an agricultural festival remembered at Athens and elsewhere, temporarily relaxed normal social hierarchy and was linked with abundance and an earlier age without labour.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The succession pattern has strong parallels in Hurrian and Hittite myth, where divine rulers are displaced through comparable acts of violence. Parallel structure supports cultural contact or shared narrative inheritance; it does not make the Greek poem a direct translation.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Cult places and festival testimony establish worship, but the Titanomachy is not a recoverable historical event. Kronos appears less frequently in early Greek visual art than the Olympian gods.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Romans identified Kronos with Saturn and transformed his Golden Age into a major theme of Latin poetry. Confusion between *Kronos* and *Chronos*, time, encouraged the familiar later image of Father Time devouring his children.
