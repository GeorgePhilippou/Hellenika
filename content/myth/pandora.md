<!-- field: summary -->
The first woman in Hesiod's account, fashioned by the gods as Zeus' dangerous answer to Prometheus and human fire.

<!-- field: myth -->
After Prometheus steals fire, Zeus orders Hephaestus to form a woman from earth and water. Athena teaches her weaving, Aphrodite gives desire, Hermes supplies a deceptive mind and the gods adorn her irresistibly. She is sent to Prometheus' brother Epimetheus, who accepts her despite a warning never to receive Zeus' gifts. Pandora opens a great storage jar and releases suffering, disease and toil into human life; only Hope remains within.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod tells related but not identical versions in the *Theogony* and *Works and Days*. The latter names her Pandora and narrates the jar.

<!-- field: religious -->
Pandora is principally a literary and explanatory figure, not the centre of a major cult. Her name, “all-gifted” or “all-giving,” also intersects with titles for earth divinities, complicating any simple origin story.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The myth encodes an aggressively male account of marriage, labour and sexual reproduction: women are presented as both necessary and costly. Reading that ideology critically is essential; repeating it as a neutral Greek belief is not.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Classical sculpture and vase painting sometimes show Pandora's creation. The evidence illustrates reception of Hesiodic themes rather than an archaeological event.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The “box” results from a Renaissance mistranslation or substitution for Hesiod's large *pithos* jar. Debate continues over Hope: is it retained for human use, or withheld as one more harm?
