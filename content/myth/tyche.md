<!-- field: summary -->
The goddess of chance and fortune, who became the protector of Hellenistic cities and whose statue for Antioch set the type for all later city-goddesses.

<!-- field: significance -->
The divinity of the Hellenistic age, which saw events as driven more by chance than by the old gods.

<!-- field: myth -->
Tyche has few myths. In Hesiod she is an Oceanid; Pindar calls her a daughter of Zeus the Liberator. As a personification she was shown with a rudder, to steer fortunes, and a cornucopia, to give plenty. After Alexander, cities adopted their own Tyche and depicted her as a woman with a crown of walls. Polybius, Demetrius of Phalerum and others wrote about the role of fortune in history.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* (line 360) and the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* mention a Tyche among the Oceanids. Pindar's poems give her a larger role.

<!-- field: religious -->
Temples to Tyche were built in many cities, such as Alexandria and Antioch. The Tyche of Antioch by Eutychides, whose Roman copies survive in the Vatican and elsewhere, showed her seated on a rock with a swimmer at her feet representing the Orontes.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Romans took over the idea as Fortuna, and the figure of Lady Fortune with her wheel is a theme of medieval art and thought.
