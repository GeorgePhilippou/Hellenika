<!-- field: summary -->
Daughter of Demeter, taken by Hades to be queen of the underworld, and returned to the world for part of each year.

<!-- field: myth -->
Persephone was gathering flowers when the earth opened and Hades carried her away. Demeter searched the world with torches, refusing to let anything grow; famine threatened to destroy humanity. Zeus ordered her return, but Persephone had eaten pomegranate seeds in the underworld, which bound her to it. The compromise was division: she spends part of each year below and part above, and the earth is barren while she is gone.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Homeric Hymn to Demeter, c. 650–550 BC, which is the fullest early narrative and is closely connected to the Eleusinian Mysteries.

<!-- field: religious -->
Central. The Eleusinian Mysteries, celebrated for close to two thousand years, enacted this story, and initiates were promised a better condition after death. Persephone is also the underworld queen invoked in curse tablets, and appears in Orphic gold leaves buried with the dead as instructions for what to say when they arrive.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The myth encodes the agricultural year, and specifically the Mediterranean one: the barren period is the hot dry summer, when seed grain is stored underground, not the northern winter. Read that way it is precisely accurate to Greek farming.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Telesterion at Eleusis is a real building designed for mass initiation, in use for centuries and rebuilt repeatedly. Inscribed Orphic gold leaves from graves in Italy, Crete and Thessaly give the dead instructions involving Persephone by name. The mystery's content remains unknown.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The story has become one of the most reworked myths in modern literature and psychology, read variously as a narrative of seasonal renewal, of abduction and trauma, and of the transition from daughter to wife.
