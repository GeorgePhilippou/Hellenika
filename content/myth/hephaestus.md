<!-- field: summary -->
The disabled master craftsman of Olympus, whose forge produces divine weapons, animated servants and works of impossible skill.

<!-- field: myth -->
Greek traditions give Hephaestus different parents and two falls from Olympus. In one, Hera rejects her disabled child and throws him down; in another, Zeus casts him out for defending Hera. He lands on Lemnos and becomes the gods' smith. He makes palaces, thrones, armour and marvellous automata. In the *Iliad*, Thetis asks him to replace Achilles' lost armour, and his description of the new shield becomes an image of an entire human world.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer presents Hephaestus as both comic and formidable: his impaired gait draws divine laughter, while his technical power has no equal. Hesiod supplies alternative genealogical details.

<!-- field: religious -->
At Athens, Hephaestus was worshipped alongside Athena, linking skilled production with civic intelligence. The well-preserved temple commonly called the Hephaisteion overlooked an area associated with potters and metalworkers. Lemnos claimed a particularly close connection with the god.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The god embodies the social ambiguity of craft: physically marginalised yet indispensable, working with dangerous fire and secret knowledge to create objects rulers and warriors cannot make themselves. Modern efforts to diagnose a medical condition from myth remain speculative.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Athenian Hephaisteion, inscriptions and vase paintings attest cult and iconography. Excavated workshops illuminate real Greek metallurgy but cannot identify a historical smith behind the deity.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Romans identified him with Vulcan. His self-moving tripods and golden attendants are now frequently discussed as ancient imaginings of robots, though they belong to poetic wonder rather than evidence for historical automation.
