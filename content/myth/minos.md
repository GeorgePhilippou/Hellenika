<!-- field: summary -->
The legendary king of Crete who kept the Minotaur in the labyrinth, exacted tribute from Athens, and became a judge of the dead.

<!-- field: myth -->
Minos, son of Zeus and Europa, ruled Crete from Knossos. He asked Poseidon for a bull to sacrifice; the god sent a magnificent white one, and Minos kept it. Poseidon's punishment was to make Minos' wife Pasiphaë desire the bull, and their child was the Minotaur. Minos had Daedalus build the labyrinth to contain it, and demanded seven youths and seven maidens from Athens every nine years as tribute, until Theseus killed the creature. He later imprisoned Daedalus, pursued him to Sicily, and was killed there in a bath by the daughters of King Cocalus. After death he became one of the three judges of the underworld.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer mentions Minos as a king of Knossos who "conversed with Zeus" and as a judge of the dead (Odyssey 11, 19). The Minotaur and the labyrinth are elaborated by later authors; the fullest surviving narrative is in Apollodorus, over a thousand years later.

<!-- field: religious -->
Minos anchored Cretan claims to divine descent and to the antiquity of Cretan law — Greeks credited Crete with the oldest law code, and the Spartans claimed to have derived their own institutions from it.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Some scholars have suggested that "Minos" was a Cretan royal title rather than a personal name, comparable to "pharaoh" or "caesar". This is an inference from the pattern of the tradition, not from any Bronze Age text: no Minoan or Mycenaean document mentions Minos, and Linear A remains unread.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Bronze Age Knossos was a real, enormously complex palatial centre with strong bull iconography, double-axe symbols and Athenian-facing trade. None of this evidences a king named Minos. Arthur Evans named the civilisation after him as a convenience, and that naming has retroactively lent the myth a spurious archaeological solidity.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Dante places Minos at the entrance to the second circle of Hell, assigning sinners by coiling his tail. The euhemerist reading — a real thalassocratic king remembered as myth — was standard from Thucydides onward and remains popular, but rests on no independent evidence.
