<!-- field: summary -->
The winged lion-woman of Thebes who asked a deadly riddle until Oedipus gave the answer, and whose image came to Greece from Egypt and the Near East.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-known riddle in Greek myth, and the beginning of Oedipus' rise to kingship of Thebes.

<!-- field: myth -->
Hera, or in other tales Ares or Dionysus, sent the Sphinx to Thebes to punish the city for a crime. She sat on a rock outside the walls and asked every traveller a riddle: 'What walks on four legs in the morning, two at midday and three in the evening?' Those who failed were devoured. Oedipus answered 'man', who crawls as a baby, walks upright, and uses a stick in old age. Defeated, the Sphinx killed herself. Thebes gave him the throne and the hand of the widowed queen.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod mentions the Sphinx in the *Theogony*, and she appears in the epic cycle (the lost *Oedipodea*). The riddle is preserved in later accounts such as Apollodorus and Sophocles' play refers to it.

<!-- field: religious -->
The Sphinx was not worshipped, but is frequent on tombs and sanctuaries, such as the Naxian sphinx at Delphi, as a guardian figure. The Great Sphinx of Giza was an Egyptian royal image and not the source of the Greek story.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Freud and others discussed the riddle, and Ingres' *Oedipus and the Sphinx* (1808) is a classic image. The word is used for any enigmatic person.
