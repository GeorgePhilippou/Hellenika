<!-- field: summary -->
God of healing, whose sanctuaries offered cure by dream — operating alongside, not against, Hippocratic medicine.

<!-- field: myth -->
Son of Apollo and the mortal Coronis, cut from his mother's body on the funeral pyre and raised by the centaur Cheiron, who taught him medicine. He became so skilled that he raised the dead, and Zeus killed him with a thunderbolt to preserve the boundary between mortal and immortal. He was afterwards made a god.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Mentioned in the Iliad as a mortal physician whose sons are Greek doctors at Troy. His promotion to god comes later, and his cult expands rapidly from the fifth century BC.

<!-- field: religious -->
Patients slept in the *abaton* at his sanctuaries and hoped for a healing dream. Epidaurus was the greatest of these, with over 200 sanctuaries eventually across the Greek world. His cult was formally brought to Athens in 420 BC, during the plague years — Sophocles reportedly housed the god's sacred snake in his own home while a temple was built.

Non-venomous snakes moved freely in the sanctuaries and were part of the cure. His staff with a single entwined snake remains the symbol of medicine, frequently confused with the caduceus of Hermes, which has two snakes and wings and is a herald's staff.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Temple medicine and rational medicine coexisted without evident conflict; Hippocratic physicians did not attack Asclepius, and some inscriptions describe treatments that sound surgical, credited to the god. The sanctuaries offered rest, diet, bathing, exercise and hope, which plausibly helped with a range of complaints.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Epidaurus sanctuary with its abaton, theatre and *tholos*; inscribed *iamata* tablets recording hundreds of individual cures in the patients' framing; and large numbers of anatomical votive offerings — terracotta eyes, ears, limbs, breasts and genitals dedicated in thanks, which effectively map what ancient people most needed cured.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Rod of Asclepius remains the international symbol of medicine. His sanctuaries have been described as the ancestors of the hospital, though the resemblance is loose.
