<!-- field: summary -->
Colchian princess, healer and sorceress who secures Jason's quest, abandons her home and is later betrayed at Corinth.

<!-- field: myth -->
Medea is daughter of Aeetes, king of Colchis, and descendant of the Sun. She helps Jason yoke fire-breathing bulls, defeat earthborn warriors and overcome the serpent guarding the Golden Fleece. She flees with him and uses dangerous knowledge to protect their escape. At Corinth Jason leaves her to marry a princess. In Euripides' tragedy, Medea kills the bride and king with poisoned gifts, then kills her own children and escapes in a chariot of the Sun.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Early epic fragments and lyric knew Medea, but surviving versions are uneven. Euripides' *Medea* of 431 BC and Apollonius' Hellenistic *Argonautica* are decisive, very different portraits: an abandoned adult exile and a young woman experiencing first love.

<!-- field: religious -->
Corinthian traditions connected Medea and her children with local rites. The evidence is complicated by later attempts to explain ritual through narrative and narrative through ritual.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Medea is marked as foreign, female and intellectually powerful. Greek texts use her to test boundaries between insider and outsider, civilisation and barbarism, healing and poison, justified anger and intolerable violence.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Vase paintings document changing scenes and emphases, including Medea rejuvenating a ram and escaping after the murders. Images show the tradition's development but cannot establish a historical woman.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Modern theatre repeatedly returns to Medea through questions of migration, race, motherhood, abuse and revenge. The child-killing version is dominant because of Euripides, not because Greek tradition was ever uniform.
