<!-- field: summary -->
King of the gods, wielder of the thunderbolt, and guarantor of oaths, hospitality and the political order.

<!-- field: myth -->
Youngest son of Kronos, who swallowed his children to prevent a prophesied overthrow. Rhea hid Zeus in a Cretan cave and gave Kronos a stone instead. Grown, Zeus freed his siblings, defeated the Titans in a ten-year war, and divided the cosmos with his brothers: sky to Zeus, sea to Poseidon, underworld to Hades. His serial affairs with mortals and goddesses, and Hera's reprisals, generate a large part of Greek myth.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Attested in Linear B as *di-we* on tablets from Knossos and Pylos, receiving offerings — so he was worshipped under this name at least 500 years before Homer. The narrative theogony is Hesiod's.

<!-- field: religious -->
The most widely worshipped Greek god, with major sanctuaries at Olympia, Dodona and Nemea. His key civic functions were guaranteeing oaths (*Zeus Horkios*) and protecting strangers and suppliants (*Zeus Xenios*) — mistreating a guest was an offence against Zeus himself, which made hospitality enforceable in a world without international law.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The name is securely Indo-European: *Dyēus phtēr*, "sky father", cognate with Sanskrit *Dyaus Pitar*, Latin *Iuppiter* and Germanic *Tiw*. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that Greek religion inherited a common Indo-European sky-god, later merged with local Aegean traditions.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Linear B tablets record offerings to *di-we*. The Temple of Zeus at Olympia held Pheidias' colossal statue, one of the Seven Wonders. Dedications at Dodona include thousands of lead tablets with questions addressed to him.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Identified with Roman Jupiter, and by Hellenistic syncretism with Ammon in Egypt and Baal in the Levant. Alexander's visit to Zeus-Ammon at Siwa is a key episode in his self-presentation.
