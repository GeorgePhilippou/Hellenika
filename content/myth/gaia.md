<!-- field: summary -->
The living Earth: a primordial being, mother of Uranus and ancestor of Titans, gods, monsters and humans.

<!-- field: myth -->
In Hesiod's *Theogony*, Gaia emerges after Chaos and produces Uranus, the starry sky, to cover her. With Uranus she bears the Titans, Cyclopes and Hundred-Handers. When Uranus suppresses their children, Gaia fashions the sickle with which Kronos attacks him. She later helps Zeus overthrow Kronos, yet also produces or supports challengers to Zeus, including the giant Typhon in Hesiod's account.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod gives the earliest surviving systematic genealogy. Homer invokes Earth in solemn oath formulas but does not make her central to an extended narrative.

<!-- field: religious -->
Gaia could be addressed as Earth itself, especially in oaths, prayers concerning fertility and traditions about oracles. Later authors remembered an early oracle of Earth at Delphi, although the sanctuary's documented historical cult was dominated by Apollo.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The overlap between landscape and personality is fundamental: Gaia is simultaneously ground, ancestor and speaking agent. Greek sources never reduce her to a merely metaphorical personification, but neither do they separate her cleanly from the physical earth.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Dedications and sanctuaries attest cults of Ge or Gaia in several Greek communities, though these were usually smaller than the great civic cults of Olympian gods. Archaeology cannot confirm the divine genealogy told by Hesiod.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The name became a scientific and ecological shorthand for Earth, most famously in the modern Gaia hypothesis. That modern model should not be projected back as an ancient Greek ecological doctrine.
