<!-- field: summary -->
The older generation of gods, children of Earth and Sky, overthrown by Zeus and the Olympians.

<!-- field: significance -->
Central to the Greek story of how the present order of the gods came to be — and the origin of the word "titanic".

<!-- field: myth -->
In Hesiod's *Theogony*, the Titans were the twelve children of Gaia (Earth) and Uranus (Sky): six sons, including Oceanus, Hyperion, Iapetus and Kronos, and six daughters, including Rhea, Themis and Mnemosyne. Urged by Gaia, Kronos castrated Uranus and took power. Kronos swallowed his own children to stop them overthrowing him, but Rhea saved the youngest, Zeus. When Zeus grew up he freed his siblings and fought the Titans for ten years in the Titanomachy, with the help of the hundred-handed giants and the Cyclopes, who made his thunderbolt. The defeated Titans were imprisoned in Tartarus. Some, such as Themis and Mnemosyne, joined the new order; Atlas, son of Iapetus, was condemned to hold up the sky.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* (c. 700 BC).

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The story of generations of gods overthrown by their sons closely resembles the Hittite *Song of Kumarbi* and the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*, evidence that Greek myth drew on Near Eastern traditions.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
"Titanic" means enormous; the largest moon of Saturn is Titan, and the giant liner was named *Titanic*.
