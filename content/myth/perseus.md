<!-- field: summary -->
The Argive hero who kills Medusa, rescues Andromeda and becomes the legendary founder of Mycenae.

<!-- field: myth -->
An oracle warns Acrisius that his daughter Danae's son will kill him. He confines her, but Zeus reaches her as a shower of gold and Perseus is born. Mother and child are cast into the sea and survive. Later Perseus is sent to bring back Medusa's head. Athena and Hermes equip him; he approaches the Gorgon by reflection, decapitates her and escapes. On his return he rescues Andromeda from a sea monster and eventually fulfils the oracle accidentally by killing Acrisius with a discus.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiodic poetry and early lyric preserve fragments of the story, while vase painting shows Perseus and the Gorgons from the seventh century BC. The continuous narrative survives most fully in later mythographers.

<!-- field: religious -->
Perseus received heroic honours and belonged especially to Argive identity. Genealogies made him ancestor of Heracles and founder of Mycenae, connecting communities and ruling houses to the age of heroes.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The myth combines folktale patterns—an exposed child, a fatal prophecy, magical helpers and an impossible quest—with local claims about real places. Those structures explain transmission better than attempts to recover a Bronze Age biography.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Mycenae is a real Bronze Age centre, but no inscription or object connects its foundation to Perseus. Archaic images demonstrate that his story was established early; they do not demonstrate the events depicted.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The rescue of Andromeda became a major subject in Roman and European art. Modern retellings often make Medusa the centre of the story, examining violence and power from the monster's perspective.
