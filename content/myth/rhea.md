<!-- field: summary -->
Titan mother of the central Olympian siblings, who saves the infant Zeus from being swallowed by Kronos.

<!-- field: myth -->
Rhea bears six children to Kronos, who swallows each because Uranus and Gaia have warned that his own child will displace him. When Zeus is born, Rhea hides him and presents Kronos with a stone wrapped like a baby. Cretan versions place Zeus in a cave, guarded by armed youths whose clashing weapons conceal his cries. Once grown, Zeus frees his swallowed siblings and begins the war against the Titans.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod preserves the earliest continuous version of the divine succession. Homer knows Rhea as a mother of gods but gives her a much smaller narrative role.

<!-- field: religious -->
Rhea's cult overlaps with that of the Mother of the Gods, often called Cybele in later Greek and Roman contexts. The association connected divine motherhood, mountains, ecstatic music and armed attendants, but local identities and histories should not be flattened into one timeless mother goddess.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Cretan claims about Zeus' birthplace helped bind an island landscape into the pan-Greek genealogy. Several caves competed for the association. Their ritual use is archaeological fact; identifying any deposit with the literal childhood of Zeus is not.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Idaean and Diktean caves contain long-lived ritual deposits. Bronze shields and other dedications fit the Cretan tradition of noisy armed protectors, while still leaving the myth's development and direction of influence open to interpretation.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Rhea was identified increasingly with the Phrygian and Roman Magna Mater. Modern retellings often call her simply an earth goddess, but Greek genealogy distinguishes her from Gaia, Earth herself.
