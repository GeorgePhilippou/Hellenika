<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest Greek hero, who performed twelve labours in penance for killing his own family, and became a god after death.

<!-- field: myth -->
Son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmene, Heracles was persecuted from birth by Hera. Driven mad by her, he killed his wife and children, and was set twelve labours in penance: the Nemean lion, the Hydra of Lerna, the Ceryneian hind, the Erymanthian boar, the Augean stables, the Stymphalian birds, the Cretan bull, the mares of Diomedes, Hippolyta's belt, the cattle of Geryon, the apples of the Hesperides, and Cerberus. He died from a poisoned robe and was taken up to Olympus as a god.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer and Hesiod both know him. The canonical set of twelve labours is later, standardised perhaps by the metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia (c. 460 BC), which show exactly twelve.

<!-- field: religious -->
Uniquely, Heracles received both hero cult (sacrifices to the dead) and divine cult (sacrifices to a god) — Herodotus notes that some cities did both, at separate altars. The Dorians claimed descent from him, and the "Return of the Heraclidae" was the traditional Greek explanation for the Dorian settlement of the Peloponnese. Macedonian kings, including Alexander, claimed him as ancestor.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The figure is composite, absorbing local strongman legends from across Greece and clear Near Eastern elements — the lion-fighting hero is a Mesopotamian motif going back to Gilgamesh, and the club-and-lion-skin iconography has Levantine parallels. The Dorian descent claim served a political function: it made conquest into repossession.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Lerna, where he killed the Hydra, is a real Early Bronze Age site — the myth may attach to visible ancient ruins, as often happens. The Olympia metopes survive and fixed the labours in art. Nothing evidences a person.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Choice of Heracles — between Virtue and Vice personified as two women — became a standard moral allegory in European art. As Hercules he was adopted by Roman emperors and by the Renaissance as an emblem of virtuous strength, and later by everything from Disney to bodybuilding.
