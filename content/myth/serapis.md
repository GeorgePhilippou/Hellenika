<!-- field: summary -->
The god created under the early Ptolemies by blending the Egyptian Osiris-Apis with Greek gods, and worshipped across the Roman world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The clearest example of the fusion of Greek and Egyptian religion, and the patron god of Alexandria.

<!-- field: myth -->
Serapis had no myths of his own in the Greek sense; his stories were those of the gods he absorbed. As Osiris, he was the murdered and revived king of the dead, husband of Isis and father of Horus; as Zeus and Hades he was lord of the sky and the underworld; as Asclepius he healed the sick who slept in his temples. His cult statue showed him seated, bearded and mature, wearing a corn-measure (*kalathos*) on his head as a sign of fertility, with Cerberus at his feet.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The name comes from the Egyptian *Osor-Hapi*, Osiris-Apis, the dead Apis bull worshipped at Memphis, attested long before the Greeks. Serapis as a Greek-looking god appears in the reign of Ptolemy I. Plutarch and Tacitus tell how Ptolemy dreamed of a colossal statue and brought it from Sinope on the Black Sea, advised by the Egyptian priest Manetho and the Athenian Timotheus; the story is probably a later legend.

<!-- field: religious -->
His main temple was the Serapeum of Alexandria, with others at Memphis and Canopus, where the sick slept in the temple to receive healing dreams. He was the protector of the Ptolemaic dynasty and of sailors. Together with Isis, Harpocrates and Anubis, he was worshipped by Greeks and later Romans from Delos to Britain; the emperors Vespasian and Septimius Severus favoured him.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Older scholars saw Serapis as a political invention to unite Greeks and Egyptians; most now think the cult grew from the existing Memphite worship, adapted for Greeks rather than imposed. In practice Egyptians continued to worship Osiris and Apis, while Serapis became mainly a god of Greeks and Romans. His cult ended with the destruction of the Serapeum in AD 391.
