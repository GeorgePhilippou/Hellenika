<!-- field: summary -->
The bird-women of the sea whose song lured sailors to destruction, and whom Odysseus heard by being tied to his mast.

<!-- field: significance -->
An image of fatal allure, and of knowledge that is deadly to possess.

<!-- field: myth -->
Circe warned Odysseus about the Sirens, who sat in a meadow beside a heap of bones and sang of the past. To pass them safely, he blocked his crew's ears with wax and had himself bound so that he could not steer towards the island however much he begged. The Sirens promised that he would know everything that happens on earth. According to later tradition, they were fated to die if a ship got past them, and they threw themselves into the sea.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Odyssey, Book 12, in which the number of Sirens is two. Later sources vary, and give their parents as the river Achelous and a Muse.

<!-- field: religious -->
The Sirens appear as grave figures and mourners on funerary monuments from the sixth century BC, which suggests they were connected with lament and the afterlife.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Early depictions are of birds. Later artists made them part fish, which is the origin of the mermaid-like image. 'Siren' now also refers to an alarm sound.
