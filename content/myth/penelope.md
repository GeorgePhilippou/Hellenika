<!-- field: summary -->
The wife of Odysseus, who kept faith and outwitted her suitors on Ithaca for twenty years until her husband returned.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Odyssey's match for Odysseus in cunning, and for later ages the model of the faithful and clever wife.

<!-- field: myth -->
Penelope was the wife of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, and mother of their son Telemachus. While Odysseus was away at Troy and on his wanderings, more than a hundred suitors gathered at their palace, feasting on its wealth and demanding that she choose one of them to marry. She said she would choose once she had woven a shroud for Odysseus' father, Laertes — and for three years she wove it by day and unpicked it by night, until a maid betrayed her. When Odysseus returned disguised as a beggar, she set the suitors the test of stringing his great bow, which only he could do. Even after the suitors were killed, she tested him herself, ordering their bed to be moved; only Odysseus knew it could not be, because he had built it around the trunk of a living olive tree.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer's *Odyssey*, where she is a central figure.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Penelope belongs to legend; no historical figure lies behind her.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Penelope became the model of the faithful wife from antiquity onward. Modern writers, such as Margaret Atwood in *The Penelopiad*, have retold the story from her point of view.
