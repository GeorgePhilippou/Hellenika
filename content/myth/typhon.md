<!-- field: summary -->
The hundred-headed monster born to challenge Zeus, whom the king of the gods buried under Mount Etna.

<!-- field: significance -->
The last great threat to Zeus's rule, whose defeat completed the Olympian order.

<!-- field: myth -->
After the Olympians beat the Titans, Gaia bore her last child, Typhon, huge enough to touch the stars, with a hundred snake heads that spat fire and made the sounds of all animals. The gods fled in fear to Egypt, disguising themselves as animals. Zeus stood his ground and fought, and after a terrible battle struck him down with thunderbolts and threw him into Tartarus or beneath Etna. Hesiod makes the stormy winds that harm sailors his offspring. His mate Echidna bore many of Greece's most famous monsters.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony*, lines 820–880, is the main account, and the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* mentions a snake-like Typhaon. Pindar and Aeschylus place him under Etna.

<!-- field: religious -->
There was no cult. Volcanic activity at Etna and at the Phlegraean Fields was explained by his struggles beneath the earth.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The English word 'typhoon' is probably of mixed Chinese, Arabic and Greek origin, and may have been influenced by the name Typhon. Typhon's snaky form has parallels in later dragon stories.
