<!-- field: summary -->
The legendary singer of Thrace, whose music charmed all nature and who went down to the underworld to bring back his wife Eurydice.

<!-- field: significance -->
The ideal of the poet-musician, and the legendary founder of Orphism, a religious movement concerned with the soul and the afterlife.

<!-- field: myth -->
Orpheus was a singer from Thrace, son of the Muse Calliope and, in some accounts, of Apollo, who gave him the lyre. His music was so beautiful that wild beasts followed him, trees moved and rocks were softened. He sailed with Jason's Argonauts and drowned out the Sirens' song with his own. When his wife Eurydice died of a snake bite, he went down to the underworld and sang so movingly that Hades and Persephone let her return — on condition that he did not look back at her until they reached daylight. At the last moment he turned, and she vanished. Orpheus later died at the hands of Thracian women, the Maenads, who tore him apart; his head floated, still singing, to Lesbos.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
He is named by the poet Ibycus in the 6th century BC; the story of Eurydice is told fully by Virgil and Ovid.

<!-- field: religious -->
From the 6th century BC, poems attributed to Orpheus taught that the soul was immortal and could be purified. Gold leaves buried with the dead and the Derveni Papyrus preserve Orphic texts.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Orpheus has inspired operas from Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* (1607) and Gluck to Offenbach, and countless poems and paintings.
