<!-- field: summary -->
Goddess of sexual desire, born from sea foam, whose power in Greek thought is compulsion rather than romance.

<!-- field: myth -->
Hesiod has her born from the foam where Ouranos' severed genitals fell into the sea near Cyprus; Homer makes her a daughter of Zeus and Dione. Married to Hephaestus and caught in a net with Ares by her husband. She won the golden apple by promising Paris the most beautiful woman in the world, which set the Trojan War in motion, and protected Aeneas through it.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer and Hesiod, who give incompatible birth stories. She is not clearly attested in Linear B, which distinguishes her from Zeus, Poseidon, Hera, Athena, Artemis and Dionysus.

<!-- field: religious -->
Major cult centres at Paphos on Cyprus, Corinth and Kythera. Her power was understood as dangerous and involuntary — Sappho's Fragment 1 asks the goddess for help against a desire the speaker cannot control, and Euripides' *Hippolytus* has her destroy a man for refusing her. At Sparta she was worshipped armed.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Her Near Eastern derivation is well supported. The Cypriot and Levantine goddess Astarte, and behind her Mesopotamian Ishtar/Inanna, share her attributes: love and war combined, dove and morning-star associations, and a dying-consort myth paralleled by Adonis, whose name derives from the Semitic *adon*, "lord". Her cult reached Greece through Cyprus, which is why Homer calls her "the Cyprian".

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The sanctuary at Palaipaphos on Cyprus has Bronze Age origins and continuity into the Roman period, with a conical stone as the cult object rather than an anthropomorphic image — a distinctly Near Eastern form. Phoenician and Cypriot votive material connects the traditions.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Identified with Roman Venus. Botticelli's *Birth of Venus* fixed the foam-born image in European art. The Venus de Milo made her body the standard reference for classical beauty in the modern imagination.
