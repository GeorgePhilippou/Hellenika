<!-- field: summary -->
The king of Lydia who offended the gods and was punished in the underworld with eternal hunger and thirst within sight of food and water.

<!-- field: significance -->
The origin of the English word 'tantalise'.

<!-- field: myth -->
Tantalus was a friend of the gods who dined at their table. He abused their trust by stealing nectar and ambrosia, or by testing their omniscience, in the most notorious version by serving them the flesh of his son Pelops. The gods realised and Demeter alone ate a shoulder, which was replaced with ivory when Pelops was restored. Tantalus was punished in the underworld, where he stood chin-deep in water that drained away when he stooped, beneath branches that rose out of reach. A rock hung above his head.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The *Odyssey* (Book 11) describes the punishment. Pindar's *Olympian 1*, from 476 BC, is the earliest surviving account of the banquet, which he rejects.

<!-- field: religious -->
His tomb and a ruined city were shown on Mount Sipylus in Lydia. The line of Pelops, from whom the Peloponnese took its name, was thought to be accursed through him.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The curse on the house of Tantalus is the background of the Oresteia and of other Greek tragedies. The English verb 'tantalise' derives from the myth.
