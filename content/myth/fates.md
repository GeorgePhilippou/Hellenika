<!-- field: summary -->
The three goddesses who spin, measure and cut the thread of each human life.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Greek expression of fixed destiny, which constrains mortals and, to varying degrees, gods.

<!-- field: myth -->
The Moirai assign each person his portion of life. Clotho spins the thread, Lachesis allots its length, and Atropos, the inflexible, cuts it. In the *Iliad*, Zeus holds up golden scales to weigh the fates of Achilles and Hector, and considers saving Sarpedon, but Hera tells him other gods will not approve. The Fates attended weddings and births, and at the birth of Meleager they told his mother of the burning log that determined his life.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
In Homer, Moira is a single force. Hesiod's *Theogony* presents the three Moirai as daughters of Night, or of Zeus and Themis. Plato's *Republic* closes with the Myth of Er, in which they spin the lots of rebirth.

<!-- field: religious -->
The Moirai had shrines at Delphi, Sparta and Corinth, and were invoked at weddings and at birth. Brides at Delphi offered locks of hair to them, according to Pausanias, and Apollo was called Moiragetes.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Roman Parcae and Norse Norns are comparable. They appear in Shakespeare's witches and in Goya's *Atropos*, and the term 'fatal' derives from fate.
