<!-- field: summary -->
The goat-legged god of shepherds, flocks and wild places from Arcadia, whose sudden terrors gave us the word "panic".

<!-- field: significance -->
The god of the untamed countryside and its music, whose cult Athens adopted after Marathon and who became a lasting symbol of nature.

<!-- field: myth -->
Pan was usually said to be the son of Hermes. He had the horns, legs and beard of a goat, and roamed the mountains of Arcadia with the nymphs, playing the pipes. In one story he pursued the nymph Syrinx, who was turned into reeds to escape him; from the reeds he made the shepherd's pipe, the *syrinx* or panpipes. He was said to fill herds and armies with sudden, irrational fear — panic.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Homeric *Hymn to Pan* tells of his birth and his delight in the woods. Herodotus records his appearance to the runner Pheidippides before Marathon.

<!-- field: religious -->
Pan was worshipped mainly in Arcadia, often in caves. After Marathon in 490 BC, Herodotus says, the Athenians gave him a cave shrine on the slope of the Acropolis, believing he had promised to help them; caves sacred to him and the nymphs were found across Attica.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Plutarch tells a famous story of a voice announcing "Great Pan is dead", later read by Christians as the end of paganism. Pan became a symbol of nature in Romantic poetry and art, and the Romans identified him with Faunus.
