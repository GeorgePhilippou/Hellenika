<!-- field: summary -->
The national hero of Athens, who killed the Minotaur and was credited with uniting Attica into a single state.

<!-- field: myth -->
Raised in Troezen, Theseus lifted a rock to recover his father's sword and sandals, then travelled to Athens overland killing bandits on the way. He volunteered for the Cretan tribute, killed the Minotaur with Ariadne's help, abandoned her on Naxos, and forgot to change his black sail for a white one — so his father Aegeus, watching, threw himself into the sea that bears his name. As king he unified the villages of Attica into one polis (*synoikismos*), fought the Amazons, joined the Calydonian boar hunt, and helped his friend Pirithous attempt to abduct Persephone from the underworld, where he was trapped in a chair until Heracles freed him.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer mentions him only in passing. His prominence grows sharply in the sixth century BC under the Peisistratids and again after the Persian Wars; Plutarch's Life, written around AD 100, is the fullest surviving account and openly warns that it strays into the realm of fable.

<!-- field: religious -->
Theseus was Athens' answer to Dorian Heracles — a hero embodying civic rather than brute virtue. In 476/5 BC Cimon brought bones from Skyros identified as his and installed them in a shrine at Athens, a deliberate act of state myth-making at the height of Athenian confidence.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The *synoikismos* of Attica — the political unification of its villages under Athens — did happen, though gradually, and Theseus is the figure to whom Athenians attributed it. This is the pattern seen with Lycurgus at Sparta: a real institutional process credited to a founding individual.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Attica shows real archaeological unification over the Bronze Age and early Iron Age. Nothing connects it to any individual. The bones Cimon recovered were from a large Bronze Age burial, which proves only that Bronze Age Greeks were sometimes tall.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The "ship of Theseus", preserved by Athenians who replaced each plank as it rotted, became the standard philosophical puzzle about identity over time, first recorded in Plutarch and still in use.
