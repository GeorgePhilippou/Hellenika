<!-- field: summary -->
The legendary Athenian craftsman who built the labyrinth of Crete and escaped on wings with his son Icarus, who flew too close to the sun.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Greek archetype of the inventor and artist, and in Icarus a lasting image of ambition that overreaches.

<!-- field: myth -->
Daedalus was an Athenian craftsman of unmatched skill, who fled to Crete after killing his talented nephew out of jealousy. There he worked for King Minos. He built the hollow wooden cow in which Queen Pasiphae mated with a bull, producing the Minotaur, and then the labyrinth to imprison it. When he helped Theseus escape — giving Ariadne the idea of the thread — Minos shut him and his son Icarus in the labyrinth. Daedalus made wings of feathers and wax for them both. He warned Icarus not to fly too low, near the sea, or too high, near the sun. But Icarus soared upward, the wax melted, and he fell into the sea that bears his name, the Icarian Sea. Daedalus reached Sicily, where King Cocalus protected him from the pursuing Minos.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The *Iliad* mentions a dancing floor he made for Ariadne. The full story is told by later writers, above all Ovid's *Metamorphoses*.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Greeks credited Daedalus with the first lifelike statues and inventions such as the saw and glue. His name, meaning "skilfully made", suggests a personification of craft.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Icarus' fall, painted in the scene attributed to Bruegel, became a symbol of overreaching ambition; Daedalus of the inventor and artist.
