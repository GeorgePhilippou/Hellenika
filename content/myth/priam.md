<!-- field: summary -->
The aged king of Troy during the Trojan War, father of Hector and Paris, killed when the city fell.

<!-- field: significance -->
The image of a father and king in defeat, whose meeting with Achilles over Hector's body is the emotional climax of the Iliad.

<!-- field: myth -->
Priam was the king of Troy, set on the throne as a boy after Heracles sacked the city and killed his father Laomedon. He had fifty sons, among them Hector and Paris, and many daughters, including Cassandra. When Paris carried off Helen, Priam took her into his family and so brought the Greek army to Troy. In *Iliad* Book 24, after Achilles has killed Hector, Priam goes alone by night to the Greek camp, guided by Hermes, kisses the hands of the man who killed his son, and ransoms the body. Achilles, thinking of his own father, weeps with him. When Troy fell, Achilles' son Neoptolemus killed Priam at the altar of Zeus in his own palace.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer's *Iliad*; his death is told in the lost epics of the Trojan cycle and in Virgil's *Aeneid*, Book 2.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Heinrich Schliemann named a gold hoard found at Troy in 1873 "Priam's Treasure", but it dates to about 2400 BC, a thousand years before any plausible Trojan War.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
His death at the altar became a symbol of the fall of a city and the cruelty of war, retold by Virgil and painted many times.
