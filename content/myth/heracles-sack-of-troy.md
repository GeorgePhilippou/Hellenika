<!-- field: summary -->
A generation before the Trojan War, Heracles sacked Troy himself over an unpaid debt, killing King Laomedon and putting the young Priam on the throne.

<!-- field: myth -->
Troy's walls were said to have been built by Poseidon and Apollo, sent to serve King Laomedon for a year as punishment by Zeus. When the work was finished, Laomedon refused to pay them. Poseidon answered by sending a sea monster to ravage the coast, which oracles said could only be stopped by sacrificing Laomedon's own daughter, Hesione. Heracles arrived as she was being offered up, killed the monster, and saved her -- but Laomedon reneged again, refusing the promised reward of his immortal horses, a gift Zeus had once given his ancestor Tros. Heracles left, gathered an army, and returned to storm the city, killing Laomedon and all his sons but one. The sole survivor, the youngest son Podarces, was spared and given the throne under a new name: Priam. It was this Priam, a generation later, who was ruling Troy when the Greeks came again.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Iliad 5.638–642, where Tlepolemus recalls his father Heracles taking Troy "for the sake of the horses of Laomedon" with only six ships and few men -- told as a story the audience is assumed to already know, not one the poem needs to explain. The fuller narrative, with Hesione's rescue and Priam's accession, survives only in much later sources: Pseudo-Apollodorus's Bibliotheca and Diodorus Siculus's Bibliotheca historica, both centuries after Homer.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Ancient chronographers placed this sack a generation before Agamemnon's coalition returned, which is precisely why the war of the Iliad finds a different king on the throne. The doubled destruction may preserve a genuine memory that Troy, occupied for millennia, was sacked more than once -- reworked by later tradition into two separate stories a generation apart, tied together by a shared cast of heroic families, rather than left as one blurred event.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Nothing in the excavated sequence at Hisarlik distinguishes this sack from Troy's many others. The site was destroyed and rebuilt repeatedly across some three thousand years, and no single layer can be confidently tied to Heracles rather than to any of the site's numerous other violent ends, including the one usually associated with the Iliad's own war a generation later.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The episode functions in the Iliad as backstory rather than narrative -- cited in passing by a minor character rather than told by the poet, which is itself evidence it was already a familiar story by the time the Iliad took shape. Later mythographers used it to bind Heracles's generation of heroes to the Trojan War generation: Telamon, who fought beside Heracles at Troy and was rewarded with Hesione, was the father of Ajax, one of the Greek champions at the second, more famous siege.
