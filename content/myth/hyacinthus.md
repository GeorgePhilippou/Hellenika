<!-- field: summary -->
The Spartan youth loved by Apollo and killed by a discus, whose death was mourned each year at Amyclae.

<!-- field: significance -->
A Laconian hero-cult merged with the cult of Apollo, and the source of the flower named hyacinth.

<!-- field: myth -->
Hyacinthus, a beautiful young Spartan, was the lover of Apollo. While the two were throwing the discus, a throw by Apollo struck him in the head and killed him. In some versions the west wind Zephyrus, jealous, blew the discus off course. Apollo, grieving, made a flower from his blood, with the markings 'AI' (alas) on its petals.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The myth is not found in Homer. Euripides' *Helen* and Nicander give the story, and Ovid tells it in *Metamorphoses* 10. Pausanias describes the monuments at Amyclae.

<!-- field: religious -->
The Hyacinthia was celebrated at Amyclae, south of Sparta, in the summer. It began with offerings at the tomb of Hyacinthus, with no garlands or songs of triumph, and ended in a joyful feast for Apollo. Spartan soldiers were said to have returned home to attend it, even during war.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Ovid's version influenced Renaissance poets, and Mozart wrote a youthful opera, *Apollo et Hyacinthus* (1767). The flower in the myth was probably a lily or iris rather than the modern hyacinth.
