<!-- field: summary -->
Messenger, trickster and boundary-crosser: protector of travellers and trade, inventor, thief and guide of the dead.

<!-- field: myth -->
Born to Zeus and Maia in an Arcadian cave, Hermes escapes his cradle on his first day, invents the lyre from a tortoise shell and steals Apollo's cattle by making them walk backwards. When confronted, he charms Apollo with the new instrument and exchanges it for authority over cattle and divination. Elsewhere he carries divine messages, gives Odysseus protection against Circe and escorts the souls of the dead.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Linear B tablets record a divine name read as Hermes centuries before Homer. The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* gives the fullest early account of his extraordinary infancy and reconciliation with Apollo.

<!-- field: religious -->
Hermes protected movement across boundaries: roads, merchants, heralds, shepherds, athletes and thieves all belonged within his unusually broad domain. Squared stone *herms*, usually with a head and erect phallus, stood at doors, crossroads, gymnasia and territorial limits.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
His apparently contradictory roles share a logic. Messenger, trader, thief and soul-guide all move things across borders. His intelligence is practical and opportunistic rather than the ordered civic strategy associated with Athena.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Numerous herms, dedications and images establish widespread cult. The mutilation of the Athenian herms in 415 BC caused a major political panic because these markers represented protection, boundaries and the city's collective security.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Romans identified Hermes with Mercury. The winged helmet and sandals now treated as his standard costume became especially dominant in later art; early Greek images more often show a mature bearded herald carrying a staff.
