<!-- field: summary -->
Goddess of practical intelligence, weaving, and war conducted with strategy — patron of Athens and of Odysseus.

<!-- field: myth -->
Zeus swallowed the pregnant Metis, and Athena was later born fully armed from his head. She competed with Poseidon for Athens: he struck the rock and produced a salt spring, she produced the olive tree, and the citizens chose hers. She turned Arachne into a spider for weaving better than her, guided Odysseus and Perseus, and remained a virgin goddess.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
A Linear B tablet from Knossos names *a-ta-na-po-ti-ni-ja*, plausibly "Mistress of Athana", so a goddess associated with the place-name existed in the Bronze Age. Homer and Hesiod supply the narrative.

<!-- field: religious -->
Athens' patron, honoured at the Panathenaia, at which a new *peplos* was woven and presented to her ancient olive-wood image. She was Athena Polias (of the city), Athena Parthenos (the maiden, in the Parthenon), Athena Nike (victory) and Athena Ergane (of craftwork) — one goddess covering government, war, and skilled labour.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The name appears to be pre-Greek, and the relationship between goddess and city is contested: it is not clear whether the city is named after the goddess or the reverse. Her association with the household, crafts and a protective palace role has been argued to descend from a Minoan-Mycenaean palace goddess.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Linear B "Mistress of Athana" tablet from Knossos; the Parthenon and its frieze; the Erechtheion, built around the spot where the olive and the salt spring were shown to visitors; thousands of dedications on the Acropolis; and her owl on Athenian coinage circulating across the Mediterranean.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Identified with Roman Minerva. Her image became the emblem of universities, learned societies and libraries across Europe, and the owl remains a general symbol of wisdom.
