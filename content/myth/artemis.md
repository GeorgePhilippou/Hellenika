<!-- field: summary -->
Virgin goddess of the hunt and of wild places, who protects young animals and young women — and kills without hesitation.

<!-- field: myth -->
Twin of Apollo, born first and helping deliver her brother, which made her a goddess of childbirth. She asked Zeus for eternal virginity and a bow. She turned Actaeon into a stag for seeing her bathe, and his own dogs killed him. She demanded the sacrifice of Iphigenia to release the Greek fleet, and in some versions substituted a deer at the last moment.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Named in Linear B at Pylos as *a-te-mi-to*, so worshipped in the Bronze Age. Homer calls her "mistress of animals", a title with clear Near Eastern and Minoan parallels.

<!-- field: religious -->
Widely worshipped in transitional roles: girls served as "little bears" at Brauron in Attica before marriage, and women dedicated clothing to her after surviving childbirth, or had it dedicated for them if they did not. Her temple at Ephesus was one of the Seven Wonders.

The Ephesian Artemis is a distinct figure — a many-breasted image, probably an Anatolian mother goddess assimilated to the Greek name, and not the huntress at all.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The "Mistress of Animals" type — a female figure flanked by wild beasts — appears in Minoan, Mycenaean and Near Eastern art long before Greek literature, and Artemis is the clearest Olympian continuation of it.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Linear B tablets from Pylos; the Artemision at Ephesus with Croesus' inscribed column drums; the sanctuary at Brauron with its dedications of children's clothing and toys; and abundant Mistress-of-Animals imagery from the Bronze Age onwards.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Identified with Roman Diana. The Ephesian image was reinterpreted by early Christians in Ephesus as a rival to the Virgin Mary, and the city later hosted the council that declared Mary *Theotokos*.
