<!-- field: summary -->
The many-headed serpent of the Lernaean marsh, whose heads grew back when cut off until Heracles seared the stumps.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the most famous labours of Heracles, and the origin of the use of 'hydra' for a problem that multiplies when attacked.

<!-- field: myth -->
The Hydra lived in the swamp of Lerna and ravaged the surrounding country. Its breath and blood were deadly. Heracles went to Lerna in his chariot with Iolaus, flushed the monster out with fire arrows and grappled it. As he struck off each head, two more grew in its place. Iolaus burned each stump with a torch to prevent new growth. The central head was immortal, and Heracles buried it under a rock. He then dipped his arrows in the gall. Hera sent a giant crab to bite his foot, which he crushed; both Hydra and crab became constellations.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* (lines 313–318) gives the Hydra's parentage and its killing by Heracles and Iolaus. The number of heads varies among sources from five to a hundred.

<!-- field: religious -->
Lerna was a sacred site, and Pausanias speaks of a spring whose depth was unfathomable, a rite of Demeter and Dionysus, and a plane tree under which the Hydra was supposed to have lived.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Hydra gives its name to the small freshwater polyp, and to the Hydra constellation. It is a standard image for a problem that multiplies.
