<!-- field: summary -->
The hollow wooden horse in which Greek warriors entered Troy — the most famous stratagem in Western literature.

<!-- field: myth -->
Unable to take Troy by siege, the Greeks built a great wooden horse, hid warriors inside, and sailed away as if defeated, leaving it as an offering. The priest Laocoön warned against it and was killed with his sons by serpents from the sea. The Trojans took the horse inside; the Greeks emerged at night and opened the gates.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Not in the Iliad, which ends before the fall. The Odyssey refers to it several times as a known story. The full narrative was in the lost epic *Iliupersis*; the surviving detailed account is Virgil's Aeneid Book 2, over a thousand years after the supposed events.

<!-- field: religious -->
Laocoön's death establishes the theme of the true prophet disbelieved, alongside Cassandra, whose curse was to foresee accurately and never be believed.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Several rationalisations have been offered: that the horse was a siege engine — Assyrian siege towers were covered in damp horse hides and are depicted in reliefs — or that it stands for an earthquake, since Poseidon is god of both horses and earthquakes, and Troy VI does show earthquake damage. Both are ingenious and neither is evidenced.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Nothing. Wooden objects do not survive, and no ancient depiction predates the story's literary circulation. The earliest known image is on a seventh-century BC fibula and a relief pithos from Mykonos of c. 670 BC — both centuries after the traditional date, and both illustrating the tale rather than recording an event.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
"Trojan horse" is now standard vocabulary in computer security for malicious code disguised as something benign — a myth that has become a technical term. The Laocoön group of sculpture, unearthed in Rome in 1506, made the episode central to European art.
