<!-- field: summary -->
Ruler of the underworld and the dead, husband of Persephone and a god approached through cautious euphemism.

<!-- field: myth -->
After the defeat of the Titans, Zeus, Poseidon and Hades cast lots for the sky, sea and underworld. Hades receives the realm of the dead. With Zeus' permission he abducts Persephone, daughter of Demeter, and makes her his queen. Demeter's grief stops the crops until a settlement allows Persephone to return, but pomegranate seeds bind her to spend part of the cycle below.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer describes the gloomy house of Hades and the shades who inhabit it. The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* gives the earliest surviving extended account of Persephone's abduction and negotiated return.

<!-- field: religious -->
Because naming the lord of the dead could be ominous, worshippers often used titles such as Plouton, the Wealthy One. The title linked him to mineral and agricultural wealth rising from below ground. Sacrifice to underworld powers differed in time, posture and treatment from ordinary Olympian ritual.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Hades is not an embodiment of evil. Greek sources present him as implacable and feared, but lawful within the cosmic division. Later Christian imagery profoundly changed how modern audiences imagine an underworld ruler.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Votive reliefs and sanctuaries attest joint worship of Plouton and Persephone, especially in contexts connected with fertility and mystery cult. They evidence religious practice, not the physical geography of a subterranean kingdom.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Roman Pluto and later European images of hell merged with Hades in literature and art. Modern retellings often recast the Persephone story as romance, while the early hymn foregrounds abduction, maternal grief and divine negotiation.
