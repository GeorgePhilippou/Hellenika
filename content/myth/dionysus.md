<!-- field: summary -->
God of wine and ecstatic release, whose festivals produced Greek drama — and who dissolves the boundaries of the self.

<!-- field: myth -->
Son of Zeus and the mortal Semele, who was destroyed by seeing Zeus in his true form; Zeus sewed the unborn child into his thigh. Dionysus travelled the world with a following of maenads and satyrs, driving those who denied him mad. In Euripides' *Bacchae* he returns to Thebes and destroys King Pentheus, who is torn apart by his own mother in a state of ecstatic delusion.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Linear B tablets from Pylos and Khania name *di-wo-nu-so*, which settled a long argument — he was once thought a late foreign import to Greece, but he was present in the Bronze Age.

<!-- field: religious -->
The City Dionysia at Athens is where tragedy and comedy were performed: Greek theatre is a religious festival of this god. His worship involved wine, masks, processions with phalloi, and — in some places, and largely as reported by outsiders — ecstatic mountain revels by women.

He is the god of dissolved boundaries: between human and animal, male and female, individual and group. That made him useful and dangerous, and Greek cities regulated his cult carefully.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The tradition that he arrived from Thrace or the East is contradicted by the Linear B evidence. The "arriving god" is a feature of his myths rather than his history — he is repeatedly the outsider who must be admitted, which may be a structural feature rather than a memory of introduction.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Linear B tablets naming him; the Theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Athenian Acropolis; enormous quantities of drinking vessels showing his imagery; and the Derveni papyrus, a carbonised Orphic commentary of the fourth century BC and the oldest surviving European manuscript.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Identified with Roman Bacchus. Nietzsche made the Dionysian the counterpart of the Apollonian, and much twentieth-century criticism of tragedy runs through that opposition.
