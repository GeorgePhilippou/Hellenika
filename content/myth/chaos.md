<!-- field: summary -->
The first yawning gap in Hesiod's cosmogony—not yet the modern idea of confusion, randomness or disorder.

<!-- field: myth -->
Hesiod opens the *Theogony* with Chaos. After it come Gaia, broad Tartarus and Eros. From Chaos emerge Erebus and Night, who in turn produce Aether and Day. Chaos does not manufacture the cosmos through a deliberate act and is not a creator god ruling from outside it. The poem instead begins with a minimal opening or separation from which a populated, genealogically ordered universe can develop.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The earliest surviving systematic account is Hesiod's *Theogony*. Other Greek cosmogonies, including Orphic traditions and later philosophical models, begin differently and should not be treated as footnotes to one canonical version.

<!-- field: religious -->
Chaos was not the centre of a major documented Greek cult. Its importance is cosmogonic and poetic, illustrating again that a figure can be fundamental to a genealogy without receiving widespread ritual worship.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The Greek word suggests a gap, chasm or yawning opening. The now-familiar meaning of total disorder developed through later philosophical, literary and scientific usage. Translating Hesiod's Chaos as “confusion” therefore imports ideas not clearly present in the poem.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Cosmogony is preserved through texts and reception rather than a material event recoverable by excavation. Archaeology can study the contexts in which poems and rituals circulated, not verify a first state of the universe.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Chaos became a term in theology, political language, mathematics and physics. Those later concepts have their own histories and should be distinguished from Hesiod's primordial gap.
