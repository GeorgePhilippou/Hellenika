<!-- field: summary -->
The god of love and desire — in early myth a primal force of creation, later the winged son of Aphrodite with his arrows.

<!-- field: significance -->
Desire personified, central to Greek poetry and philosophy, and the origin of the image of Cupid.

<!-- field: myth -->
In Hesiod's *Theogony*, Eros is one of the first beings to appear, after Chaos and Gaia: "fairest of the immortal gods", who loosens the limbs and overpowers the mind of gods and men. He is a cosmic force that brings things together and makes generation possible. In later stories he becomes the son of Aphrodite, usually by Ares, a beautiful winged youth or mischievous child whose arrows make gods and mortals fall in love. Apuleius' Roman tale tells of his love for the mortal Psyche.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* (c. 700 BC). Lyric poets such as Sappho, who called him "bittersweet", made Eros a central figure of love poetry.

<!-- field: religious -->
Eros had a cult at Thespiae in Boeotia, where a Praxiteles statue of him stood, and was honoured with Aphrodite at many sanctuaries and in gymnasia.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Plato's *Symposium* develops Eros into a philosophical force leading the soul towards beauty and truth. The Romans called him Cupid or Amor, and his image as a winged child with a bow passed into Renaissance and modern art.
