<!-- field: summary -->
The snake-haired goddesses of vengeance who hunt down those who shed kindred blood, and whom Athena turned into the Eumenides.

<!-- field: significance -->
The embodiment of the moral claim of blood and oath, and in Aeschylus the passage from private revenge to law.

<!-- field: myth -->
The Furies lived beneath the earth and rose to punish those who killed a parent or sibling. In the *Oresteia*, they hound Orestes after he kills his mother Clytemnestra. He reaches Athens, where Athena sets up a court on the Areopagus to try him. The votes are tied, Athena votes for acquittal, and the angered Furies threaten the land. Athena offers them a cult and a home in Athens, and they become the Eumenides, 'kindly ones', who guarantee the fertility of the land.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The *Iliad* has Erinyes who punish perjury and injury to parents. Hesiod's *Theogony* gives their birth. The *Eumenides*, the third play of Aeschylus' *Oresteia* (458 BC), is the classic account.

<!-- field: religious -->
They were worshipped as the Semnai Theai in a cave below the Areopagus and at Colonus, as Eumenides, and offerings were made to them without wine. Those who killed suppliants at their altar, such as in Cylon's coup, were thought to have offended them.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Sartre's *The Flies* and Eugene O'Neill's *Mourning Becomes Electra* adapt the story. Paintings by Bouguereau and others show them chasing Orestes.
