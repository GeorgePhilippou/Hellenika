<!-- field: summary -->
The most beautiful woman in the world, whose departure with Paris was the stated cause of the Trojan War.

<!-- field: myth -->
Daughter of Zeus and Leda, Helen was courted by every prince in Greece; her suitors swore an oath to defend whoever won her, which is how the coalition was assembled. She married Menelaus of Sparta. Paris, awarding the golden apple to Aphrodite, was promised the most beautiful woman in the world and took Helen to Troy. After the city fell Menelaus intended to kill her, and did not.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Iliad, where she is already at Troy and full of self-reproach. Notably, Homer never describes her appearance — her beauty is conveyed entirely through the old men of Troy agreeing that she is worth fighting for, and then saying she should go home.

<!-- field: religious -->
Helen received cult at Sparta as a goddess associated with young women and marriage, at the Menelaion and at Therapne. This is significant: a figure worshipped as divine at Sparta appears in epic as a mortal woman, which suggests the epic character may be a demoted deity rather than the reverse.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
None. Wars are not fought over abductions, and the Iliad itself knows this — the Greek leaders discuss plunder and honour far more than Helen. The abduction is best read as narrative cause rather than historical one.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Menelaion at Sparta is a real Bronze Age and later cult site with dedications to Helen and Menelaus. It attests to the cult, not the person.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Stesichorus wrote a poem attacking Helen, went blind, and recanted with a *Palinode* claiming she never went to Troy at all — only a phantom did, while the real Helen waited in Egypt. Euripides built a whole play on that version. Marlowe gave her the face that launched a thousand ships. Modern retellings by Atwood, Barker and others have restored her a point of view.
