<!-- field: summary -->
A bull-headed man confined in the labyrinth beneath Knossos, fed on Athenian youths and killed by Theseus.

<!-- field: myth -->
Born to Pasiphaë after Poseidon caused her to desire a white bull, the Minotaur — properly named Asterion — was a creature with a bull's head and a man's body. Daedalus built the labyrinth to contain it. Athens sent seven youths and seven maidens as periodic tribute to be devoured. Theseus volunteered, and Minos' daughter Ariadne gave him a thread to retrace his path. He killed the Minotaur and escaped, abandoning Ariadne on Naxos on the way home.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The story is alluded to in the seventh century BC and depicted on Greek vases from the same period; the François Vase of c. 570 BC shows Theseus and the Athenian youths. There is no Bronze Age source of any kind.

<!-- field: religious -->
The tale served Athenian civic identity above all: it explains the end of subjection to Crete and establishes Theseus as the founder-hero of Athens. Its retelling was tied to the annual sacred ship sent to Delos, which delayed the execution of Socrates.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
A frequently proposed reading is that the myth encodes a memory of Athenian subordination to Bronze Age Crete, with the bull representing Minoan power. Alternatively it may derive from bull-leaping ritual observed or reported by mainlanders. Both are plausible reconstructions and neither is testable.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Knossos genuinely is a complex of interconnecting rooms, and the double axe (*labrys*) is carved throughout it, which may lie behind the word *labyrinthos*. Minoan art is saturated with bulls: leaping frescoes, rhyta shaped as bull heads, horns of consecration. But no maze exists at Knossos, no chamber suggests confinement of anything, and no depiction of a bull-headed man has ever been found in Minoan art. The monster is Greek, not Minoan.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The labyrinth becomes a Christian symbol of the path to salvation and is laid into cathedral floors, notably at Chartres. Borges retells the story from the Minotaur's side in "The House of Asterion", where the creature is lonely and waits gratefully for its killer. Picasso used the Minotaur repeatedly as a self-figure.
