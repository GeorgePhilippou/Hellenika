<!-- field: summary -->
God of prophecy and music — and of the plague arrows that open the Iliad. Order and destruction in the same figure.

<!-- field: myth -->
Born on Delos with his twin Artemis after Leto, pursued by Hera, found refuge on a floating island. He killed the serpent Python at Delphi and took the oracle. He flayed the satyr Marsyas alive for challenging him to a music contest. His loves generally end badly: Daphne became a laurel tree escaping him, Cassandra received prophecy and the curse of never being believed, Hyacinthus was killed by a discus.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Iliad opens with Apollo sending plague on the Greek camp — the first divine action in European literature. His name may appear in Linear B, but the reading is uncertain.

<!-- field: religious -->
Delphi made him the most consulted god in the Greek world, effectively arbitrating colonial foundations, constitutional changes and wars. He was also Apollo Paean the healer and, paradoxically, the sender of plague — the two functions belong together, since a god who sends disease can withhold it. The Delphic maxims "know thyself" and "nothing in excess" were carved at his temple.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Unusually, his origin is disputed between Anatolian and northern Greek sources. Hittite and Luwian parallels have been proposed, and Homer notably places him on the Trojan side. If Anatolian, he is a major foreign import into the Olympian system.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Delphi with its temple, treasuries and thousands of dedications; the sanctuary on Delos; the temple at Bassae; and the Charioteer of Delphi, dedicated to him. The geology beneath the Delphic temple, with intersecting faults and hydrocarbon gases, is real.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Nietzsche's *Birth of Tragedy* opposed the Apollonian — form, clarity, individuation — to the Dionysian, and the pair has been standard critical vocabulary ever since. Apollo is one of very few Greek gods whose name Romans adopted unchanged.
