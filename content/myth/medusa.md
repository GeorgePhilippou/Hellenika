<!-- field: summary -->
The mortal Gorgon whose face turns viewers to stone and whose severed head becomes a powerful protective emblem.

<!-- field: myth -->
Medusa is one of three Gorgon sisters and the only one who can die. Perseus is ordered to obtain her head. Guided by Athena and Hermes, he uses a reflective surface to avoid looking directly at her and cuts off her head while she sleeps. Pegasus and Chrysaor spring from her severed neck, children conceived with Poseidon. Perseus uses the head as a weapon before giving it to Athena, who places it on her aegis.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Homer knows the terrifying Gorgon head, while Hesiod supplies Medusa's genealogy and death. Early Greek art shows a monstrous, frontal face with staring eyes, tusks and protruding tongue.

<!-- field: religious -->
The *gorgoneion* was not simply an illustration of a story. Its confrontational face appeared on shields, temples, vessels and clothing as an apotropaic sign intended to repel danger. Athena's use of the head made terror protective.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Versions changed substantially. Early Greek Medusa is born a Gorgon; the influential transformation narrative—in which she is assaulted by Poseidon in Athena's temple and punished—comes from Ovid's Latin *Metamorphoses* many centuries later.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Gorgon images are abundant from the Archaic period onward and track changes from grotesque monster to beautiful woman with snakes. They establish the emblem's importance, not a zoological creature or historical encounter.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Medusa has become a symbol of female rage, trauma, protection and resistance. These readings are culturally powerful but should be identified as receptions of an ancient figure rather than presented as one original meaning.
