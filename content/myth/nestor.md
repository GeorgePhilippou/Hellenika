<!-- field: summary -->
The aged king of Pylos in the Iliad, valued for counsel and long-winded reminiscence rather than fighting.

<!-- field: myth -->
Nestor had already outlived two generations when the war began. He advises, mediates the quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon, and repeatedly tells stories about how much better things were done in his youth. Homer gives him a famously enormous drinking cup that only he can lift. He is one of the few Greek leaders to reach home safely.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Iliad and Odyssey, where Telemachus visits him at Pylos in search of news of his father.

<!-- field: religious -->
No significant independent cult. His importance is literary — he embodies the authority of age and memory in a heroic world that mostly values youth and force.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Pylos was a genuine Mycenaean palace centre. When Carl Blegen excavated the site whose location matched Homer's Pylos, he found a major palace with the largest Linear B archive on the mainland. Homer preserved a correct geographical memory of an important Bronze Age site that had been in ruins for four centuries.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The Palace of Nestor is real, and named for the myth. Its tablets name many individuals; none is Nestor. A gold cup found at Mycenae was once linked to Nestor's cup, and the inscribed clay cup from Pithekoussai jokes about it around 730 BC — which proves the story was in circulation, not that the man existed.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
"A Nestor" came to mean a wise elder statesman in English and other European languages. The Pithekoussai cup shows the joke was already available to Greek colonists in Italy within a generation of the alphabet's arrival.
