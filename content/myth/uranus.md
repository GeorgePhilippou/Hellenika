<!-- field: summary -->
The primordial sky, whose violent overthrow by Kronos begins the succession struggle at the centre of Hesiod's cosmos.

<!-- field: myth -->
Gaia produces Uranus as an equal partner able to cover her completely. Their children include the twelve Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handers. Uranus forces some of these children back inside Gaia, causing her pain. She gives Kronos an adamantine sickle, and he ambushes and castrates his father. Blood falling on Gaia produces the Erinyes, Giants and Melian nymphs; in Hesiod's version, Aphrodite forms from the severed genitals cast into the sea.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The fullest early account is Hesiod's *Theogony*. The sky is naturally present in Homeric divine language, but Uranus is not an active Homeric character on the scale of Zeus or Poseidon.

<!-- field: religious -->
Uranus belongs principally to cosmogony and genealogy. Evidence for sustained independent worship is slight, and it would be misleading to treat every named divine being in poetry as the centre of a major cult.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The story establishes a repeated pattern: an older ruler suppresses his children, a younger generation overthrows him, and sovereignty passes through violence. Comparable divine-succession narratives occur in ancient Near Eastern traditions, although Greek poets reshaped them in their own genealogical system.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
No archaeological evidence can demonstrate the succession myth. Images confidently identified as Uranus are uncommon and usually later than the poems that preserve his genealogy.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The Roman equivalent Caelus appears in later art and genealogy. The planet Uranus received its modern name in the eighteenth century, extending the Greco-Roman sequence used for other planets.
