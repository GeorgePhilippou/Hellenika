<!-- field: summary -->
Alexander's horse for twenty years, tamed by him as a boy and commemorated with a city on the Hydaspes.

<!-- field: myth -->
A Thessalian dealer offered Philip II a magnificent but unmanageable horse. The twelve-year-old Alexander noticed it was frightened of its own shadow, turned it to face the sun, and mounted it. Philip is said to have wept and told him to find a kingdom big enough for himself, since Macedon was too small. Bucephalus carried Alexander across Asia and died after the Hydaspes; Alexander founded Bucephala in his honour.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Arrian and Plutarch, both writing four centuries later, though drawing on earlier campaign accounts. Arrian treats the horse's death and the city foundation as straightforward fact.

<!-- field: religious -->
None, though the founding of a city for a horse is an unusual honour with a whiff of hero cult about it.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The city foundation is reported by multiple independent sources and is likely genuine — Alexander founded another nearby city for his dog Peritas. The taming story has the shape of a legend attaching to a great man's childhood, and Plutarch, who tells it best, is explicitly interested in character-revealing anecdote rather than verification.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
The site of Bucephala has not been securely located; it is placed somewhere on the Jhelum in modern Pakistan, and the river has changed course substantially. No physical trace has been identified.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
The taming became one of the most retold stories of Alexander's youth, standard in medieval Alexander romances across Europe, Persia and India, and a staple of the "great man revealed in childhood" genre.
