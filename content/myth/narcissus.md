<!-- field: summary -->
The beautiful youth who fell in love with his own reflection and died of longing, from whom a flower and the term 'narcissism' take their names.

<!-- field: significance -->
A myth about self-love and vain beauty, particularly important in later European culture.

<!-- field: myth -->
At Thebes the seer Tiresias told Narcissus' mother that he would live long 'if he never knew himself'. The boy grew up beautiful and spurned all lovers, among them the nymph Echo. A rejected suitor prayed that he would feel the same unanswered love, and the goddess Nemesis granted it. Narcissus bent over a pool, fell in love with the image he saw there, and could not leave it. He pined away and was changed into a flower with white petals around a yellow centre.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The best-known version is Ovid's, *Metamorphoses* 3, from the early first century AD. Pausanias reports a variant in which he loves his dead sister's face in the water. Conon, a mythographer of the first century BC, tells another variant in which a rejected suitor kills himself.

<!-- field: religious -->
The narcissus flower was linked with the underworld, and appears in the myth of Persephone's abduction. At Thespiae in Boeotia a local tradition honoured Narcissus' death, and the Thespians also kept a cult of Eros.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Freud made 'narcissism' a central concept in psychoanalysis, and the myth has inspired painters such as Caravaggio and Dalí.
