<!-- field: summary -->
The Titan trickster who negotiates sacrifice, steals fire for mortals and endures Zeus' punishment for crossing divine boundaries.

<!-- field: myth -->
At Mecone, Prometheus divides an ox into an attractive pile of bones concealed in fat and an unappealing pile of edible meat. Zeus chooses the deceptive portion, establishing why humans eat sacrificial meat while smoke and bones go to the gods. Zeus hides fire; Prometheus steals it in a fennel stalk. Zeus answers with Pandora for humanity and chains Prometheus while an eagle consumes his liver each day. Heracles eventually kills the eagle and releases him.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod's *Theogony* and *Works and Days* give the earliest surviving connected accounts. The later tragedy *Prometheus Bound* radically intensifies his role as a suffering benefactor who teaches humanity the arts of civilisation.

<!-- field: religious -->
Prometheus had cult associations especially at Athens, where a torch race connected him with fire and craft. His religious profile was narrower than his enormous literary afterlife.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The story explains permanent conditions: sacrificial practice, the separation of humans and gods, labour, fire and technical knowledge. It is not a memory of one inventor discovering combustion.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Attic art shows his punishment and release. Evidence for ritual confirms worship but cannot turn the Titan into a historical culture-bringer.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Prometheus became an emblem of intellectual rebellion, scientific ambition and resistance to tyranny. Mary Shelley's subtitle for *Frankenstein*, “The Modern Prometheus,” captures both creative power and catastrophic responsibility.
