<!-- field: summary -->
The Trojan princess whose true prophecies were never believed, and who was taken captive by Agamemnon.

<!-- field: significance -->
The type of the truth-teller who is disregarded.

<!-- field: myth -->
Apollo, taken with Cassandra, gave her the gift of prophecy, but when she rejected him, he ensured that her prophecies would never be believed. She warned that Paris' voyage would end in disaster, and that the wooden horse hid enemy soldiers, but she was ignored. When Troy fell, the Locrian Ajax tore her from the statue of Athena, an act of impiety that angered the goddess. Agamemnon took her home as a captive, and Clytemnestra killed both of them at Mycenae.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Iliad names Cassandra only briefly, as the first to see Priam bring Hector's body back to Troy. Her gift of prophecy and the curse are told in later sources such as Aeschylus and Apollodorus.

<!-- field: religious -->
A cult of Cassandra, under the name Alexandra, was observed in Laconia, and in southern Italy she was linked with the Locrian girls, sent annually to serve at Athena's temple in Ilion to atone for Ajax's crime.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* offers the best-known scene, in which she cries out visions at the palace door. 'Cassandra' now names a person whose warnings are ignored, and a 'Cassandra complex' in psychology.
