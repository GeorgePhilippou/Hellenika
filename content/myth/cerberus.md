<!-- field: summary -->
The many-headed hound guarding the underworld, subdued and brought to daylight in Heracles' final labour.

<!-- field: myth -->
Cerberus stands at the threshold of Hades, admitting the dead but preventing their escape. For his final labour Heracles must bring the hound to the upper world without weapons. With divine permission, he wrestles Cerberus into submission, displays him to Eurystheus and returns him below. Other visitors use different means: Orpheus charms the guardian with music, while Psyche later distracts him with drugged cakes in Roman narrative.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod calls Cerberus a fifty-headed, bronze-voiced dog born to Echidna and Typhon. Visual art and later literature increasingly give him three heads, a serpent tail and snakes rising from his body.

<!-- field: religious -->
Cerberus belongs mainly to poetic and visual representations of the underworld. Funerary beliefs did include guardians, thresholds and provisions for the dead, but there was no uniform Greek dogma about his exact appearance.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
The monster makes death's one-way boundary visible. Heroes who pass him demonstrate exceptional temporary access to a realm ordinary mortals enter only once.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Vase paintings and sculptures show Heracles leading or wrestling Cerberus and document the development of the three-headed form. They are evidence for myth and iconography, not an underworld animal.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Cerberus became the standard name for an intimidating guard. Dante relocates him within Christian hell, while modern fiction and games retain the three-headed design more consistently than ancient sources did.
