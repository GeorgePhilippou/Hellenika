<!-- field: summary -->
Goddess of the hearth whose quiet ritual centrality held together household, sacrifice and the political community.

<!-- field: myth -->
Hestia is the first child swallowed by Kronos and the last released, making her both eldest and youngest among the central divine siblings. In the Homeric Hymns she refuses marriage to Poseidon or Apollo and swears perpetual virginity. Zeus grants her honour in every house and a share in sacrificial offerings. Unlike Athena or Artemis, her virginity expresses fixedness at the centre rather than movement beyond the household.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
Hesiod places her in the genealogy of Kronos and Rhea. Short Homeric Hymns preserve the clearest statements of her ritual privileges and her place in household worship.

<!-- field: religious -->
The hearth was both practical fire and sacred centre. Domestic rites addressed Hestia, while a polis maintained a public hearth, often in the prytaneion. Colonists could carry fire from the mother city's hearth to establish continuity in a new settlement.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Hestia demonstrates why mythology alone cannot measure a deity's importance. She has few adventures and little visual iconography, yet her role in sacrifice and community was fundamental. Narrative prominence and religious prominence are different things.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Hearths are common archaeological features, but an excavated fire installation is not automatically a sanctuary of Hestia. Literary and epigraphic context is needed to identify cult rather than ordinary domestic activity.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Romans identified Hestia with Vesta, whose public fire was guarded by the Vestal Virgins. The Roman institution was far more centralised than anything shared uniformly across the Greek cities.
