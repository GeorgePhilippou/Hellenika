<!-- field: summary -->
King of Mycenae and commander of the Greek coalition at Troy, murdered by his wife on his return.

<!-- field: myth -->
Agamemnon led the expedition against Troy to recover Helen for his brother Menelaus. Becalmed at Aulis, he sacrificed his daughter Iphigenia to obtain a favourable wind. At Troy he quarrelled with Achilles over a captive woman, nearly losing the war. He returned home with the prophetess Cassandra and was killed in his bath by his wife Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus — she had waited ten years, and Iphigenia was her reason. Their son Orestes avenged him by killing her, and was pursued by the Furies until acquitted at Athens.

<!-- field: earliestSource -->
The Iliad and Odyssey. Aeschylus' Oresteia (458 BC) gives the murder and its aftermath their canonical form; Homer's version differs in detail, with Aegisthus more prominent.

<!-- field: religious -->
The house of Atreus functioned as the standard Greek example of inherited guilt — a curse working itself out across generations until it is broken by law rather than blood. That structure is the argument of the Oresteia.

<!-- field: historicalBackground -->
Hittite records name a great king of Ahhiyawa, generally identified with a Mycenaean power, who dealt with the Hittite king as an equal. Some have proposed this reflects an overlord of the kind Agamemnon represents. The identification of Ahhiyawa with Mycenaean Greeks is well supported; the leap from that to Agamemnon personally is not.

<!-- field: archaeology -->
Mycenae was genuinely a rich, fortified Bronze Age centre. Schliemann's gold mask, called the Mask of Agamemnon, dates to around 1550–1500 BC — roughly three centuries before any traditional date for the Trojan War. The name is a nineteenth-century flourish that has proved impossible to dislodge.

<!-- field: laterInterpretation -->
Aeschylus' Clytemnestra is one of the great roles in world drama, and modern readings increasingly present her as a woman with an unanswerable grievance rather than a monster. The mask remains the most reproduced image of Bronze Age Greece.
