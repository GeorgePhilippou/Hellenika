<!-- field: summary -->
The three and a half centuries from Actium to the founding of Constantinople, when Greece was a province of the Roman empire and Greek culture became the culture of the empire's elite.

<!-- field: significance -->
Rome conquered Greece, but Greek became the language of the eastern empire, of philosophy, science and medicine, and of the New Testament — the bridge by which Greek civilisation passed into Byzantium, Christianity and Europe.

<!-- field: overview -->
After Octavian's victory at Actium in 31 BC and the annexation of Egypt in 30 BC, the whole Greek world lay inside the Roman empire. Mainland Greece became the province of Achaea in 27 BC, governed from Corinth, refounded as a Roman colony; Augustus founded Nicopolis to celebrate Actium. The *pax Romana* ended the wars between cities that had marked the Hellenistic age, though the old cities of the mainland shrank and many sanctuaries declined.

Rome's rulers admired Greek culture. Educated Romans learned Greek, studied in Athens and toured its monuments; the poet Horace wrote that captured Greece had taken its fierce conqueror captive. Nero competed at the Olympic Games, and Hadrian, the most Greek of emperors, finished the temple of Olympian Zeus at Athens, built a library and founded the Panhellenion. Wealthy Greeks like Herodes Atticus gave cities theatres and fountains.

Greek writers flourished in a movement later called the Second Sophistic: Plutarch, Lucian, Pausanias, Arrian, the physician Galen and the astronomer Ptolemy. Greek was also the language of the first Christians; Paul preached at Athens and Corinth, and the New Testament was written in koine Greek. In the 3rd century AD Gothic and Herulian raids sacked Athens in 267, and in 330 Constantine dedicated a new capital at Byzantium — Constantinople.

<!-- field: politics -->
Greek cities kept their councils and magistrates under Roman governors, competing for imperial favour and titles. Many leading Greeks became Roman citizens and senators; the emperor was the ultimate patron.

<!-- field: warfare -->
Little fighting in Greece itself, apart from the civil wars of the late Republic and the barbarian raids of the 3rd century AD; Greeks served in the Roman legions and auxiliaries.

<!-- field: culture -->
The Second Sophistic, Neoplatonism, Greek medicine and astronomy; Roman baths, aqueducts and theatres alongside restored Greek sanctuaries; mummy portraits and papyri in Roman Egypt.

<!-- field: boundaryNote -->
Starts with the end of the last Hellenistic kingdom in 30 BC; some historians start Roman Greece with the sack of Corinth in 146 BC. Ends with the dedication of Constantinople in AD 330.
