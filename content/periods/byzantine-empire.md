<!-- field: summary -->
The Greek-speaking, Christian Roman empire of the east, ruled from Constantinople from its dedication in AD 330 to its fall to the Ottomans in 1453.

<!-- field: significance -->
For over a thousand years it preserved Roman law, Greek learning and Orthodox Christianity, shielded Europe from successive invasions, and — by copying and finally exporting its manuscripts — handed ancient Greek literature to the Renaissance.

<!-- field: overview -->
In AD 330 the emperor Constantine, the first Christian emperor, dedicated a new capital on the site of the old Greek city of Byzantion, on the Bosporus. After the western half of the empire fell to Germanic kingdoms in the 5th century, the eastern half carried on. Its people called themselves Romans; modern historians call it Byzantine, after Byzantion. Under Justinian (527–565) it reconquered Italy and North Africa, codified Roman law and built Hagia Sophia.

In the 7th century Arab armies took Syria, Egypt and North Africa, and the empire shrank to Asia Minor, the Balkans and southern Italy. It became more Greek: Greek replaced Latin in government, and the emperor took the Greek title *basileus*. It survived sieges of Constantinople, the long struggle over icons (iconoclasm), and under the Macedonian dynasty in the 9th–11th centuries recovered its power and converted the Slavs and the Rus' to Orthodox Christianity. Byzantine scholars copied and annotated the classics.

From 1071 the Seljuk Turks took most of Asia Minor, and in 1204 the knights of the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople. The Byzantines recovered their capital in 1261, but the restored empire was small and poor, while the Ottoman Turks advanced. On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II took Constantinople; Mystras fell in 1460 and Trebizond in 1461. Greek scholars fleeing west carried manuscripts and learning to Italy.

<!-- field: politics -->
An autocracy ruled by an emperor chosen by God, crowned by the patriarch, but often made and unmade by the army, the court and the people of Constantinople. Government was run by a large salaried bureaucracy and a sophisticated tax system.

<!-- field: warfare -->
Defensive warfare against Persians, Arabs, Bulgars, Turks and Crusaders, relying on fortifications such as the Theodosian Walls, diplomacy, gold, and secret weapons like Greek fire.

<!-- field: culture -->
Orthodox Christianity, icons, mosaics and domed churches; Greek scholarship and manuscript copying; Roman law; monasticism on Mount Athos; a revival of Plato at Mystras.

<!-- field: boundaryNote -->
The start is conventional: some historians begin with Constantine (330), others with the fall of the west (476), Justinian (527) or Heraclius (610). The end, 29 May 1453, is precise, though Byzantine states survived at Mystras and Trebizond until 1460–61.
