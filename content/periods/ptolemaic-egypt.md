<!-- field: summary -->
The three centuries from Alexander's conquest of Egypt in 332 BC to the death of Cleopatra in 30 BC, when a Macedonian dynasty ruled the land of the pharaohs from Greek Alexandria.

<!-- field: significance -->
Made Alexandria the intellectual capital of the Greek world, fused Greek and Egyptian religion and kingship, and — through the dry sands of Egypt — preserved much of what survives of Greek writing on papyrus.

<!-- field: overview -->
Greeks had lived in Egypt since the 7th century BC, as traders at Naukratis and as mercenaries of the pharaohs. In 332 BC Alexander took Egypt from Persia without a fight and founded Alexandria on the coast. After his death his general Ptolemy took Egypt as his province, hijacked Alexander's body for burial in Alexandria, and in 305 BC made himself king. His descendants, all called Ptolemy, ruled for nearly three hundred years.

The early Ptolemies were the richest kings of their age. They ran Egypt through a Greek-speaking administration that taxed the Nile harvest closely, and spent the profits on a fleet, on wars with the Seleucids over Syria, and on Alexandria — its lighthouse, its Museum and Library, and the scholars, poets and scientists who worked there. To Egyptians they were pharaohs: they built temples in the traditional style at Edfu, Dendera and Philae, and the priests passed decrees in their honour in hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek, like the Rosetta Stone.

From the late 3rd century the dynasty weakened. Native revolts broke out, royal family quarrels turned into civil wars, and Rome increasingly decided who ruled. The last Ptolemaic ruler, Cleopatra VII, allied herself with Caesar and Antony; after Actium she killed herself, and in 30 BC Egypt became a Roman province.

<!-- field: politics -->
An absolute monarchy, Macedonian at court and pharaonic in Egyptian eyes, often shared between brother-sister couples. Greek officials governed the provinces (nomes) alongside a native priesthood whose support the kings bought with temple building and tax privileges.

<!-- field: warfare -->
Six Syrian Wars against the Seleucids over Palestine and southern Syria. The army was built on Greek and Macedonian settlers given land in return for service; at Raphia in 217 BC Egyptians fought in the phalanx for the first time.

<!-- field: culture -->
Alexandrian scholarship and poetry — Callimachus, Theocritus, Apollonius — alongside Egyptian temples, demotic literature and the new god Serapis. Greek and Egyptian lived side by side, mixing slowly through intermarriage and shared cults.

<!-- field: boundaryNote -->
Starts with Alexander's conquest in 332 BC rather than Ptolemy's kingship in 305 BC, so that the founding of Alexandria falls inside it. Greek Egypt continued under Rome after 30 BC.
