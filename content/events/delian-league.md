<!-- field: summary -->
An alliance against Persia founded in 478 BC that Athens converted within a generation into an empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Members swore oaths at the League's founding to have the same friends and enemies as Athens, and contributed either ships or cash to a common treasury on the sacred island of Delos, with Athens providing overall leadership and the general Cimon its most effective early commander, winning a major combined land-and-sea victory over Persia at the Eurymedon river around 466 BC. The alliance succeeded at its stated task: Persian naval power was pushed decisively out of the Aegean within a generation.

It then became something else. States that tried to leave once the Persian threat had receded were besieged and forced back into line — Naxos around 470 BC, then Thasos in 465 BC after a dispute over mainland mining rights. Most members, especially smaller ones, found it far easier to pay cash tribute than to build and crew their own warships, which meant in practice that Athens alone kept building ships and so alone retained the fleet, steadily converting a voluntary alliance of nominal equals into unmistakable Athenian domination. In 454 BC the treasury was moved from Delos to Athens, officially on the grounds that it needed better protection after a disastrous Athenian-backed expedition to Egypt had exposed League forces to Persian counter-attack, though many members clearly experienced the move as confirmation that they were no longer treated as genuine allies. Tribute revenue was soon funding the Parthenon and the rest of Pericles's building programme on the Acropolis; Athenian coinage, weights and measures were imposed across the alliance, and major legal cases involving member states were increasingly transferred to Athenian courts.

The Athenian Tribute Lists — inscribed public records of the one-sixtieth of each member's payment dedicated to Athena — survive carved on stone and give modern historians an unusually precise year-by-year account of who paid what, and how those payments shifted as cities left, rejoined, or were reassessed. The League did not long outlast the empire it had become: it was formally dissolved after Athens's total defeat in the Peloponnesian War in 404 BC, when Sparta imposed its own settlement on the cities Athens had ruled.
