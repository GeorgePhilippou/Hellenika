<!-- field: summary -->
Thucydides' dramatised negotiation of 416 BC in which Athens told the island of Melos to submit or be destroyed.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous passage in Greek historiography on power politics, quoted in discussions of international relations to this day.

<!-- field: body -->
In the sixth year after the Peace of Nicias, Athens sent a fleet to the Cycladic island of Melos, which had remained neutral and was originally a Spartan colony. The Athenian commanders held talks with the Melian magistrates behind closed doors, and Thucydides presents the discussion as a dialogue.

The Athenians say they will not argue about justice, since justice is relevant only between equals: 'the strong do what they can, the weak suffer what they must'. They add that Melos will be reasonable to submit, and that Sparta will not come to rescue them. The Melians reply that their neutrality harms no one, that surrender is slavery, and that they trust to the gods and to Sparta. Athenian warnings that hope is a comfort in danger and a ruin when it fails come to nothing.

The Melians refused. Athens besieged the town, which fell the following winter with some treachery from within; the men were put to death and the women and children enslaved. Athens then settled five hundred colonists. The passage is placed just before the Sicilian expedition, and many readers have seen it as a sign of Athenian overreach.
