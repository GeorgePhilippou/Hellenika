<!-- field: summary -->
The Ptolemies founded a Museum and Library that salaried scholars and attempted to collect every book in the world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The creation of the ancient world's greatest centre of scholarship and science.

<!-- field: body -->
Around 295–283 BC Ptolemy I, and then his son Ptolemy II, founded the Museum and Library in the royal quarter of Alexandria, the new capital of Ptolemaic Egypt. The adviser behind the project was probably Demetrius of Phalerum, a former ruler of Athens and student of Aristotle's school.

The Museum — a "shrine of the Muses" — was a research institution. Its scholars received salaries, free lodging and meals, and exemption from taxes, and were free to pursue their work. The Library set out to collect every Greek book. Ships in the harbour reportedly had their books seized and copied, with the owners getting the copies; Ptolemy III is said to have borrowed Athens' official texts of the great tragedians against a huge deposit, and kept the originals. The collection's size is unknown; ancient figures range up to 700,000 scrolls.

Its scholars created the tools of textual scholarship. Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus produced critical editions of Homer, marking doubtful lines with signs still used by editors. The poet Callimachus compiled the *Pinakes*, a catalogue of Greek literature in 120 books. The librarian Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference, and Alexandria became the centre of Greek mathematics, astronomy and medicine.

The Library did not disappear in one great fire. A fire during Julius Caesar's war in Alexandria in 48 BC destroyed some books; royal support faded; and Ptolemy VIII drove out many scholars in 145 BC. It declined over centuries, and when it finally ceased to exist is unknown.
