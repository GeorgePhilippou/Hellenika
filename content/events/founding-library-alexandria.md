<!-- field: summary -->
The Ptolemies founded a Museum and Library that salaried scholars and attempted to collect every book in the world.

<!-- field: body -->
The Museum, whose name literally means a shrine to the Muses, was conceived as a genuine research institution rather than a library in the narrow modern sense: resident scholars received a state salary, communal meals, free lodging and exemption from taxation, and were expected in return only to pursue their scholarship — arguably the earliest state-funded research institute anywhere in the world, centuries before any comparable model reappeared.

Acquisition of texts for the attached Library was aggressive to the point of officially sanctioned theft. Ships docking in Alexandria's harbour reportedly had any books aboard confiscated and copied by state scribes, with the copies handed back to their owners and the superior originals kept for the Library's own collection. Ptolemy III is said to have gone further still, borrowing the official Athenian state copies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides against an enormous financial deposit as a guarantee of their return, only to have them copied, keep the valuable originals, and simply forfeit the deposit rather than give the texts back.

The scholarship produced there was genuinely serious rather than merely acquisitive: the librarians Zenodotus and later Aristarchus produced critical editions of Homer that compared variant manuscripts against one another, inventing in the process the textual apparatus — symbols like the obelus and asterisk marking doubtful or suspect lines — still used by editors of ancient texts today. Callimachus, himself a noted poet, catalogued the entire collection in a 120-volume reference work organised by subject and author, effectively founding the discipline of library science outright. Eratosthenes, one of the Library's own directors, used its resources and correspondence network to calculate the Earth's circumference from the differing angles of the sun's shadow at two known locations, arriving at a figure strikingly close to the modern value using nothing but geometry and careful measurement.

The Library's eventual disappearance was not the single dramatic destruction popular legend often imagines, but a slow decline through repeated fires — including one accidentally started during Julius Caesar's own campaign in Alexandria — shrinking royal funding, and the periodic expulsion of foreign scholars under later, more xenophobic Ptolemaic rulers.
