<!-- field: summary -->
The treaty of 421 BC that halted the first phase of the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta — an uneasy truce that soon broke down.

<!-- field: significance -->
The failed peace that divided the Peloponnesian War into two halves and led to the Sicilian Expedition.

<!-- field: body -->
After ten years of war, both Athens and Sparta were exhausted. In 422 BC the leading hawks on each side, Cleon of Athens and Brasidas of Sparta, were both killed in battle at Amphipolis. The Athenian general Nicias then led negotiations, and in spring 421 BC the two sides agreed a peace meant to last fifty years, named after him.

Each side was to return the places and prisoners it had taken, among them the Spartan hoplites captured at Sphacteria and the Athenian colony of Amphipolis in Thrace, captured by Brasidas. But Sparta's allies Corinth, Thebes and Megara refused to accept the treaty, and the Spartan commander in Thrace refused to hand back Amphipolis. Athens and Sparta even made a separate alliance, but trust quickly evaporated.

In Athens the young Alcibiades worked against the peace. He organised an alliance of Athens with Argos, Mantinea and Elis against Sparta, which Sparta defeated at Mantinea in 418 BC. Fighting continued at the edges, Athens destroyed Melos in 416 BC, and in 415 BC it launched the Sicilian Expedition. Open war resumed in 414–413 BC.

Thucydides insisted that the "peace" was no real peace, and that the whole conflict from 431 to 404 BC was a single war.
