The temple of Athena Parthenos was built on the Acropolis in fifteen years, funded substantially by imperial tribute.

<!-- field: significance -->
The building of the greatest temple of Classical Greece, and the defining monument of Athens at the height of its power.

<!-- field: body -->
The Parthenon, the temple of Athena Parthenos on the Acropolis of Athens, was built between 447 and 432 BC, as the centrepiece of Pericles' building programme. Its architects were Iktinos and Kallikrates, and the sculptor Pheidias directed its sculpture and its great gold-and-ivory statue of Athena. It was paid for largely from the tribute of Athens' allies in the Delian League.

It replaced an earlier, unfinished marble temple destroyed when the Persians sacked the Acropolis in 480 BC; drums from its columns were built into the Acropolis wall as a reminder. Around 22,000 tonnes of marble were brought from Mount Pentelicus, 16 km away. The temple is a Doric building of 8 by 17 columns, with Ionic features such as the continuous frieze around its inner building. Its sculptures included the frieze of a procession, 92 metopes showing mythical battles, and pediments showing the birth of Athena and her contest with Poseidon for Athens.

Almost nothing in it is straight. The platform curves up about 11 cm in the middle, the columns lean slightly inwards and swell gently along their height (*entasis*), and the corner columns are thicker. These refinements, cut into every block in advance, are usually explained as corrections for optical illusions, though no ancient source confirms it.

The building later became a church and then, under Ottoman rule, a mosque. In 1687, during a Venetian siege, a shell ignited gunpowder stored inside and blew out its centre. Between 1801 and 1812 agents of Lord Elgin removed about half the surviving sculpture, now in the British Museum; Greece has long sought its return.
