<!-- field: summary -->
Hadrian's creation, in AD 131/2, of a league of Greek cities meeting at Athens to celebrate their common Greek identity.

<!-- field: significance -->
An imperial attempt to define and honour "Greekness" itself, centred on Athens as the capital of Greek culture.

<!-- field: body -->
In AD 131/2, during his last visit to Athens, Hadrian dedicated the temple of Olympian Zeus and founded the Panhellenion, "the council of all the Greeks". Delegates from member cities met at Athens, where they organised festivals, including new Panhellenic games, and honoured the emperor, who was worshipped as Zeus Panhellenios.

Membership was a matter of pedigree. Cities had to prove that they were truly Greek, descended from old foundations such as Athens, Sparta or Argos, and some produced elaborate genealogies to qualify. Inscriptions name about thirty members, from mainland Greece, the Aegean islands, western Asia Minor, Crete and Cyrene; the great cities of the Hellenistic east, such as Alexandria and Antioch, do not appear.

The Panhellenion had no political power: Greece was firmly governed by Rome. But it expressed the outlook of the Second Sophistic, when Greeks of the Roman empire looked back to the classical past as the source of their identity, and Athens enjoyed renewed prestige. It is last attested in the mid-3rd century AD.
