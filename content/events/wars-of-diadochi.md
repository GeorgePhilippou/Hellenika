<!-- field: summary -->
Forty years of war among Alexander's generals that carved the empire into the Hellenistic kingdoms.

<!-- field: significance -->
The wars that divided Alexander's empire into the kingdoms of the Hellenistic world.

<!-- field: body -->
The wars fought between about 322 and 281 BC by the Diadochi ("successors") — Alexander the Great's generals — over his empire after his death at Babylon in 323 BC.

At first the generals claimed to govern for Alexander's heirs: his half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus, who was mentally unfit to rule, and his infant son Alexander IV. Perdiccas, the first regent, was murdered in 320 BC, and the empire was divided among governors who soon fought each other. One by one Alexander's family were killed: Philip III, his mother Olympias, Alexander IV and his mother Roxana, and Alexander's sister Cleopatra. From 306 BC, starting with Antigonus and his son Demetrius, the generals took the title of king.

Antigonus the One-Eyed came closest to reuniting the empire, but the other kings allied against him, and he was killed at Ipsus in Phrygia in 301 BC, one of the largest battles of the age, decided by Seleucus' war elephants. The last of Alexander's original generals fought at Corupedium in 281 BC, where Seleucus killed Lysimachus, only to be murdered himself months later.

By then the shape of the Hellenistic world was fixed: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Syria, Mesopotamia and Iran, and the Antigonid descendants of Demetrius in Macedon, with smaller kingdoms such as Pergamon and Bactria emerging later. These kingdoms lasted until Rome absorbed them in the 2nd and 1st centuries BC. Our main narrative source is Diodorus, who followed the lost history of Hieronymus of Cardia.
