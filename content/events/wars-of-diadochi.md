<!-- field: summary -->
Forty years of war among Alexander's generals that carved the empire into the Hellenistic kingdoms.

<!-- field: body -->
The generals first claimed only to be governing on behalf of Alexander's legitimate heirs — his mentally unfit half-brother Arrhidaeus and his infant son by Roxana — and only gradually stopped pretending. From 306 BC, starting with Antigonus and his son Demetrius, they began taking royal titles outright, formally declaring themselves kings rather than regents or governors, a step that made explicit what had really been happening since Alexander's death.

The wars were fought on an enormous scale — the battle of Ipsus in 301 BC alone is thought to have involved somewhere around 150,000 men and roughly 475 war elephants, brought in from as far as India — and were settled, in the most literal sense, by the near-total elimination of Alexander's own bloodline. Roxana and her young son Alexander IV, the half-brother Arrhidaeus, Alexander's mother Olympias, and his sister Cleopatra were all killed over the course of the fighting, each death removing a potential rallying point for reunifying the empire under a single legitimate heir rather than dividing it permanently among the generals. Antigonus the One-Eyed came closer than anyone else to reuniting Alexander's empire under his own rule, only to be killed at Ipsus in his eighties, reportedly still fighting on foot surrounded by enemy soldiers after his own cavalry, commanded that day by his son Demetrius, chased a retreating enemy force too far to return in time to save him.

By 281 BC, with the death of the last of the original diadochi at the battle of Corupedium, the map had effectively settled: Ptolemies ruling Egypt, Seleucids ruling a vast and unwieldy territory stretching across Asia, and Antigonids ruling Macedon itself, with smaller successor states at Pergamon and in distant Bactria emerging somewhat later from the same general collapse of central authority. This three-way division held, in rough outline, for roughly a century and a half, until Rome dismantled the successor kingdoms one after another over the following two centuries.
