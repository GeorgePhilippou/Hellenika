<!-- field: summary -->
The sack of Athens in AD 267 by the Heruli, seaborne raiders from the Black Sea, during the crisis of the Roman empire.

<!-- field: significance -->
The worst blow to Athens since Sulla; the city shrank behind a new wall and never fully regained its Roman-era splendour.

<!-- field: body -->
In the mid-3rd century AD the Roman empire was shaken by civil wars, plague and invasions. Germanic peoples settled north of the Black Sea built fleets and raided the coasts of Asia Minor and the Aegean. In 267 a large fleet of the Heruli sailed through the Bosporus into the Aegean and sacked Athens, as well as Corinth, Argos and Sparta.

Athens had few defences. The Athenian historian Dexippus, a leading citizen, gathered about 2,000 men in the hills and harassed the raiders; fragments of his account, the *Scythica*, survive, and new pages were identified in a palimpsest in Vienna in 2014. The raiders were eventually driven off.

Archaeology shows the damage: burnt layers and destroyed buildings across the Agora, the Library of Hadrian and the Odeon of Herodes Atticus. Afterwards the Athenians built a new, much shorter wall, often called the post-Herulian wall, enclosing a small area north of the Acropolis and using stones from ruined monuments. Athens recovered as a university town, home of Neoplatonist philosophers, but the classical city had shrunk for good.
