<!-- field: summary -->
The wars in which Sparta conquered its western neighbour Messenia in the 8th and 7th centuries BC and reduced its people to helots.

<!-- field: significance -->
The conquest that made Sparta what it was: a small citizen elite living off a huge enslaved population, and organised for war to keep it down.

<!-- field: body -->
Messenia, the fertile south-western region of the Peloponnese west of Mount Taygetus, was conquered by its neighbour Sparta in two wars. Their history is known mainly from later and legendary sources, such as Pausanias, so the dates are traditional.

The First Messenian War, traditionally around 743–724 BC, gave Sparta control of the rich Messenian plain. The Second, traditionally around 685–668 BC, was a great revolt, led in legend by the hero Aristomenes, which Sparta crushed only after a long struggle. The Spartan poet Tyrtaeus, whose war songs survive in fragments, wrote during it, urging Spartans to stand firm in the ranks and die for their city.

The defeated Messenians became helots, a subject population bound to the land who farmed it for their Spartan masters and handed over half their harvest. This had lasting consequences. Freed from farming, Spartan citizens became full-time soldiers, and the strict system credited to Lycurgus — the agoge, common messes and constant military training — developed largely to control the helots, who greatly outnumbered them. Fear of helot revolt shaped Spartan policy for centuries; a great rising after an earthquake in 464 BC took years to suppress.

Messenia regained its freedom only in 369 BC, when Epaminondas of Thebes invaded after Leuctra and founded the new city of Messene. Without the helots, Spartan power never recovered.
