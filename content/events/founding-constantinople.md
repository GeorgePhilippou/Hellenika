<!-- field: summary -->
On 11 May 330 Constantine dedicated his new capital on the site of Byzantion: Constantinople, the New Rome.

<!-- field: significance -->
Conventionally the start of the Byzantine Empire, and the moment the centre of the Roman world moved to the Greek east.

<!-- field: body -->
After defeating Licinius in 324 and becoming sole ruler of the Roman empire, Constantine chose Byzantion, an old Greek city on the Bosporus, as his new capital. It lay close to the dangerous Danube and Persian frontiers, on the main route between Europe and Asia, and could be supplied and defended by sea.

He enlarged the city several times over, laying out a new wall, a forum with a porphyry column topped by a statue of himself, an enlarged hippodrome, a palace, and churches. Statues and treasures were brought from all over the empire, especially from Greek cities and sanctuaries, among them the serpent column from Delphi that had commemorated the Greek victory over Persia at Plataea. Settlers were attracted with free grain and privileges, and a senate was created on the Roman model.

The city was dedicated on 11 May 330 with ceremonies that combined Christian and traditional rites. It was officially called New Rome, but soon became known after its founder as Constantinople, "the city of Constantine". Its anniversary was celebrated every year with races in the hippodrome.
