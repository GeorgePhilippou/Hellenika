<!-- field: summary -->
The long dispute, from the 720s to 843, over whether Christians might venerate images of Christ and the saints.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its outcome made icons central to Orthodox Christianity, and deepened the split between the Byzantine and western churches.

<!-- field: body -->
By the 7th century icons — painted images of Christ, the Virgin and the saints — had become central to Byzantine devotion. In the early 8th century, after terrible defeats by the Arabs, some bishops and the emperor Leo III came to believe that God was punishing Christians for idolatry. Later tradition says that in 726 Leo removed an icon of Christ from the Chalke Gate of the palace; modern historians doubt the story and date official iconoclasm to the 730s.

His son Constantine V held a council in 754 that condemned the making and veneration of images, and monks who defended them were persecuted. In 787 the empress Irene, regent for her son, summoned the Second Council of Nicaea, which declared that icons should be venerated — though not worshipped, which belongs to God alone. Iconoclasm was revived by Leo V in 815, and finally ended in 843 when the empress Theodora, widow of Theophilos, restored the icons, an event still celebrated by the Orthodox Church as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

Most of the evidence comes from the winning side, who destroyed iconoclast writings and painted their opponents as monsters. Few religious images from before the controversy survive in the Byzantine heartlands.
