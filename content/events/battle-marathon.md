<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian and Plataean hoplites defeated a larger Persian landing force in 490 BC — the first Greek victory over Persia.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first Greek victory over a Persian army on land: it kept Athens independent, and became the founding memory of Athenian democracy.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought in 490 BC on the coastal plain of Marathon, about 40 km north-east of Athens. A Persian expedition under Datis and Artaphernes, sent by Darius I to punish Athens and Eretria for supporting the Ionian Revolt, had already sacked Eretria. It landed at Marathon with the exiled Athenian tyrant Hippias, whom it meant to restore to power.

About 10,000 Athenian hoplites and 1,000 Plataeans — the only allies to come — faced a larger Persian force, now usually estimated at around 25,000. At the urging of the general Miltiades the Greeks attacked, advancing at a run across the last stretch of open ground to limit their exposure to Persian archers. Their centre was deliberately thinned and their wings strengthened; the wings broke the Persians opposite and turned inward on the centre. Herodotus gives 6,400 Persian dead against 192 Athenians, who were buried in the mound (the Soros) that still stands on the plain.

The Athenians then marched back to the city in time to deter the Persian fleet, which had sailed round Cape Sounion. The story of a runner dying after announcing the victory is a later legend; Herodotus' runner, Pheidippides, ran to Sparta before the battle to ask for help. The legend inspired the marathon race at the first modern Olympics in 1896.

Marathon did not end the Persian threat, which returned on a far larger scale under Xerxes ten years later. But it showed that a hoplite phalanx could beat Persian infantry in the open, and Athenians remembered it ever after as their finest hour.
