<!-- field: summary -->
Athenian and Plataean hoplites defeated a larger Persian landing force in 490 BC — the first Greek victory over Persia.

<!-- field: body -->
The Persian expedition, commanded by the general Datis and Darius's nephew Artaphernes, sailed with the elderly former Athenian tyrant Hippias aboard, hoping to restore him to power in a friendlier, Persian-aligned Athens. They landed at Marathon, a plain roughly 40 km from the city, on ground Hippias reportedly chose because it suited Persian cavalry. Athens marched out to meet them with about 10,000 hoplites, joined by 1,000 men from Plataea — the only ally to send help — against a Persian landing force usually estimated today at somewhere around 25,000, far below Hippias's or Herodotus's more dramatic figures.

Before the battle Herodotus places the Athenian generalship in dispute between caution and attack; it was Miltiades, arguing that delay only gave pro-Persian factions in Athens more time to act, who prevailed and, on his day in command, ordered the assault. He thinned the Greek centre to extend the line and match the wider Persian front, concentrating strength instead on the wings, and had the hoplites advance at a run over the final stretch of open ground to minimise the time spent exposed to Persian archery — reportedly the first time a Greek army had charged into contact at a run. The weakened Greek centre gave ground as expected while both wings broke their opposite numbers and wheeled inward, trapping the Persian centre.

Herodotus records 6,400 Persian dead against just 192 Athenians, whose fallen were buried together in a mound, the Soros, still visible on the plain today. With the battle won, the Athenian army is said to have marched the roughly 40 km back to the city the same day, arriving in time to head off the Persian fleet, which had sailed around Cape Sounion apparently hoping to reach an undefended Athens before the hoplites could. Finding the army already returned, the Persians withdrew. The famous image of a lone runner, Pheidippides, dashing from Marathon to Athens purely to announce the victory before dying of exhaustion is a later literary embellishment rather than anything Herodotus describes — he does have Pheidippides run from Athens to Sparta before the battle to request help, a roughly 240 km errand completed reportedly in about two days — but it was this embellished legend that directly inspired the marathon footrace introduced at the 1896 Athens Olympics.

The result mattered beyond the immediate casualty count: it was the first demonstration that Persian infantry, fighting in the open without the advantage of overwhelming numbers or terrain, could be beaten decisively by a hoplite phalanx, a lesson the Greeks would draw on again a decade later.
