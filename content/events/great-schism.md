<!-- field: summary -->
The mutual excommunications of 1054 between Rome and Constantinople, traditionally taken as the split between Catholic and Orthodox Christianity.

<!-- field: significance -->
The symbolic date of the division of Christianity into a Latin west and a Greek Orthodox east, which still endures.

<!-- field: body -->
The churches of Rome and Constantinople had long been drifting apart, divided by language, by the pope's claim to authority over the whole Church, and by disputes over doctrine and practice: the western addition of *filioque* ("and the Son") to the Creed, the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist, and clerical celibacy. Photius' quarrel with Rome in the 9th century had been one sharp episode.

In 1054 Pope Leo IX sent legates led by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida to Constantinople to negotiate with the patriarch Michael Keroularios, who had closed the Latin churches in the city. The talks went badly. On 16 July 1054 Humbert walked into Hagia Sophia during the liturgy and laid a bull of excommunication against the patriarch on the altar. Keroularios and his synod responded by excommunicating the legates.

At the time few thought the breach permanent; the pope who had sent the legates had already died. The decisive estrangement came later, especially with the Crusades and the sack of Constantinople in 1204. The excommunications of 1054 were formally lifted by Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras in 1965, but the churches remain separate.
