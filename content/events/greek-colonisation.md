<!-- field: summary -->
Between roughly 750 and 550 BC Greeks founded hundreds of settlements from Spain to the Black Sea.

<!-- field: body -->
Colonies were founded by a mother city, led by an appointed *oikistes* who held quasi-heroic status afterward and was often worshipped at his tomb once dead, usually after consulting Delphi — which accumulated remarkable geographic knowledge as a result, since returning colonists and traders fed it information other Greek cities lacked. The new city was politically independent from the start, keeping cultic and sentimental ties but not obedience: a colony could, and sometimes did, go to war against its own mother city.

The earliest and most secure western foundation was Cumae, on the Bay of Naples, settled by Euboean Greeks from Chalcis and Eretria in the eighth century BC, decades before the wave of colonisation that produced most of Sicily's major cities. Chalcis and Eretria went on to found colonies across Sicily and the Italian mainland; Miletus alone is credited with founding dozens of settlements ringing the Black Sea, from Sinope to Olbia; Phocaea reached as far as Massalia in southern Gaul and Emporion in Spain; and Corinth founded Syracuse, which quickly grew into the largest and richest of all Greek colonies, and Corcyra, whose quarrels with its own mother city later helped trigger the Peloponnesian War.

Not every settlement fits the same pattern. Naucratis, in the Nile Delta, operated more as a licensed multi-city Greek trading enclave under Egyptian pharaonic oversight than a fully sovereign apoikia, and similar mixed arrangements existed at emporia like Al Mina in Syria, where Greek traders lived alongside a mostly non-Greek population rather than displacing it. Drivers of the wider movement included land shortage at home, factional conflict (stasis) that made losing political factions willing to leave, and the straightforward pursuit of trade.

The consequences were enormous: the Greek language and alphabet spread around the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts, the alphabet reached the Etruscans and through them the Romans, and the cultivation of the wine grape and olive was carried into France, Spain and North Africa on a scale that reshaped the agriculture of the entire region for millennia afterward.
