<!-- field: summary -->
The removal of much of the Parthenon's sculpture by Lord Elgin's agents in 1801–1812, now in the British Museum.

<!-- field: significance -->
It brought classical Greek sculpture to the eyes of western Europe, and began the longest-running dispute over the return of cultural heritage.

<!-- field: body -->
Thomas Bruce, seventh Earl of Elgin, became British ambassador to the Ottoman sultan in 1799. He first planned to have drawings and casts made of the monuments of Athens, then under Ottoman rule. In 1801 he obtained a document (*firman*) from the Ottoman government; it survives only in an Italian translation, and whether it permitted the removal of sculptures from the buildings, rather than from the ground, is still disputed.

Elgin's agents, led by the painter Giovanni Battista Lusieri, removed about half of the surviving sculptures of the Parthenon — some 75 metres of the frieze, 15 metopes and 17 pediment figures — as well as a caryatid from the Erechtheion and parts of the temple of Athena Nike. Some pieces were sawn off the buildings. One ship, the *Mentor*, sank off Kythera in 1802, and its cargo was raised by divers. The last shipments reached Britain in 1812.

The poet Byron attacked Elgin as a vandal. Elgin, deep in debt, sold the collection to the British government, which after an inquiry by Parliament bought it in 1816 for £35,000. The marbles, displayed in the British Museum, transformed European ideas of Greek art. Greece formally requested their return in 1983, and the new Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009, has a gallery waiting for them.
