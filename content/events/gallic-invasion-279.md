<!-- field: summary -->
The invasion of Macedon and Greece by Celtic warriors in 280–279 BC, which ended at the sanctuary of Delphi.

<!-- field: significance -->
A shock that Greeks remembered as a new Persian War, which reshaped the Hellenistic kingdoms and created the Galatians of Asia Minor.

<!-- field: body -->
In 280 BC bands of Celtic warriors, whom the Greeks called Galatai or Keltoi, moved south from the Danube into the Balkans. In 279 BC one army defeated and killed the Macedonian king Ptolemy Keraunos, leaving Macedon in chaos, and another, led by a chieftain the Greeks called Brennus, invaded Greece.

The Greeks gathered an army at Thermopylae, but, like the Persians before them, the Celts found a way round the pass. Brennus then marched on Delphi, hoping to plunder its treasures. The Aetolians and Phocians, defending the sanctuary, drove the attackers back. Later tradition said that Apollo himself saved his shrine with an earthquake, thunder and snowstorms, and the Delphians founded a festival, the Soteria, to celebrate the deliverance. Brennus was wounded and the Celtic army retreated with heavy losses.

The invasion had lasting consequences. In 277 BC Antigonus II Gonatas, son of Demetrius Poliorcetes, defeated a Celtic army at Lysimachia and won the throne of Macedon, founding the Antigonid dynasty that ruled it until 168 BC. Other Celtic groups crossed into Asia Minor, where they raided the Greek cities until they settled in central Anatolia as the Galatians. The kings of Pergamon built their prestige on victories against them, celebrated in monuments such as the Dying Gaul.

Greeks remembered the defeat of the Gauls as a defence of civilisation, like the Persian Wars.
