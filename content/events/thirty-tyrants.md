<!-- field: summary -->
The pro-Spartan oligarchy that ruled Athens by terror for about eight months after its defeat in 404 BC, until democracy was restored.

<!-- field: significance -->
The bloodiest episode in the history of Athenian politics, whose memory haunted the restored democracy — and shaped the trial of Socrates.

<!-- field: body -->
In 404 BC, after Athens surrendered to Sparta at the end of the Peloponnesian War, the Spartan commander Lysander backed the appointment of a board of thirty Athenians to draw up new laws. Led by Critias, a relative of Plato and former associate of Socrates, they instead seized power as an oligarchy, later called the Thirty Tyrants.

With a Spartan garrison on the Acropolis, the Thirty limited citizenship to 3,000 supporters, disarmed everyone else, and carried out a reign of terror. They executed political opponents and wealthy citizens and resident foreigners whose property they wanted — some 1,500 people according to ancient sources — and drove thousands into exile. When Theramenes, a more moderate member, protested, Critias had him condemned and forced to drink hemlock.

The democratic exiles struck back. Thrasybulus seized the fortress of Phyle on the border of Attica with a small force, then occupied Piraeus. In the fighting at Mounychia, Critias was killed. Sparta, under King Pausanias, chose not to back the oligarchs to the end, and in 403 BC the democracy was restored.

The restored democracy declared an amnesty forbidding prosecutions for past political crimes, one of the first recorded amnesties in history. But the bitterness remained, and many historians think it lay behind the trial of Socrates in 399 BC, since several of his followers, Critias above all, had been among the Thirty.
