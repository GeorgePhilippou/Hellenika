<!-- field: summary -->
The two Assembly debates of 427 BC in which Athens first ordered, and then cancelled, the massacre of the people of rebellious Mytilene.

<!-- field: significance -->
A set-piece in Thucydides on the limits of empire, passion and persuasion in democracy.

<!-- field: body -->
Mytilene, the largest city of Lesbos, revolted from the Athenian alliance in 428 BC with Spartan encouragement. It surrendered in 427 BC after a siege. Cleon, then the leading Athenian politician, persuaded the Assembly to decide that all grown men be killed and the women and children sold, and a trireme was sent with the order.

The next day, many Athenians regretted the cruelty, and the Assembly met again. Cleon claimed that the empire was a tyranny and that mercy would be taken as weakness. Diodotus, about whom nothing else is known, replied that killing everyone would harden rebels' resistance and take away the incentive for the people to side with Athens, an argument based on interest rather than pity. The show of hands was close, and Diodotus won.

A second trireme set off with the new decree, and its rowers worked in shifts to catch the first, arriving just as the earlier order was being read. Athens then executed about a thousand leading rebels and took Mytilene's fleet and land. Thucydides' report of the speeches is his own reconstruction.
