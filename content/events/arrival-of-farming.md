<!-- field: summary -->
Around 7000 BC farmers bringing crops and herds from Anatolia settled in Greece, founding the first farming villages in Europe.

<!-- field: significance -->
The start of settled village life in Europe, and of the Greek landscape of grain, flocks and villages that lasted into modern times.

<!-- field: body -->
Farming began in the Near East, in the "Fertile Crescent" of south-west Asia, from around 9500 BC. Around 7000 BC it reached Greece, the first part of Europe to adopt it. The plants and animals — emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, sheep and goats — had no wild ancestors in Greece, so they must have been brought from outside. Ancient DNA confirms that the first farmers were largely descended from people who came from Anatolia, though they mixed with the hunter-gatherers already living in Greece.

The newcomers settled first in the most fertile lowlands, especially the plain of Thessaly, where hundreds of village mounds are known, and in Macedonia, the Peloponnese and Crete, where Knossos was founded around 7000 BC. They built houses of mudbrick or wattle and daub, and within a few centuries were making pottery. At Franchthi Cave in the Argolid, the whole change, from hunting and fishing to farming, is recorded in a single sequence of deposits.

From Greece and the Balkans farming spread north and west, reaching Britain and Scandinavia around 4000 BC.
