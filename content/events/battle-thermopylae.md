<!-- field: summary -->
A Greek force under Leonidas held the pass for three days against Xerxes' army before being outflanked and destroyed.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous last stand in history: a defeat that became a lasting symbol of resistance against overwhelming odds.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought over three days in August 480 BC at the pass of Thermopylae in central Greece, between a Greek force under King Leonidas of Sparta and the invading army of the Persian king Xerxes. At the same time the Greek fleet fought the Persian navy off Artemisium nearby.

About 7,000 Greeks held the pass where it narrowed to a few metres between the mountains and the sea, behind an old Phocian wall. There Persian numbers counted for little, and for two days every assault failed, including attacks by the royal guard, the Immortals. Later tradition gives Leonidas famous replies — "come and take them" when told to surrender his arms — that may be invented.

A local man, Ephialtes, then showed the Persians a mountain path that led behind the Greek position. Learning they were about to be surrounded, Leonidas sent most of the army away and stayed with his 300 Spartans, about 700 Thespians, who chose to remain, and around 400 Thebans. On the third day they were surrounded and killed on a small hill, shot down by archers. In 1939 Spyridon Marinatos found many Persian arrowheads there.

Thermopylae was a defeat, and the Persians went on to burn Athens. But the stand delayed Xerxes, let the fleet withdraw safely, and became the most famous example of courage against overwhelming odds. The poet Simonides wrote the epitaph for the Spartan dead: "Stranger, tell the Spartans that here we lie, obedient to their words."
