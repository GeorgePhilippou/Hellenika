<!-- field: summary -->
A Greek force under Leonidas held the pass for three days against Xerxes' army before being outflanked and destroyed.

<!-- field: body -->
Around 7,000 Greeks blocked a pass only a few metres wide at its narrowest, behind a rebuilt Phocian defensive wall, where Persian numerical superiority counted for almost nothing. For two days repeated assaults failed to break the line, including attacks by Xerxes' own elite guard, the Immortals, who Herodotus says the king watched fail from a throne set up nearby. When Xerxes reportedly demanded the Greeks surrender their weapons, Leonidas is said to have answered with the famously terse *molon labe* — "come and take them" — though, like many of the battle's most quotable lines, this may owe more to later tradition than to a verified transcript.

A local man, Ephialtes, betrayed the Greek position by showing the Persians the Anopaia path over the mountain, offering to guide them in exchange for a reward. Learning he was about to be surrounded, Leonidas dismissed most of the allied army to preserve it for future fighting and stayed behind with 300 Spartans, 700 volunteer Thespians and around 400 Thebans held largely as hostages to their city's loyalty. The Thespians' choice to remain and die alongside the Spartans, entirely voluntary and driven by no comparable military tradition of their own, is one of the battle's most striking and most often overlooked details.

Cut off, the defenders were pushed back to a small hillock and killed there by massed Persian archery rather than in close combat — a detail confirmed archaeologically when the Greek excavator Spyridon Marinatos found Persian bronze arrowheads scattered across the hill in 1939. Simonides later composed a spare, widely quoted epigram for the Spartan dead, inscribed at the site: that passers-by should tell the Spartans that "we lie here, obedient to their commands." The delay the stand bought allowed the Greek fleet to withdraw from nearby Artemisium in good order rather than in a rout, and gave the southern Greek states crucial extra time to organise the defence that would culminate at Salamis and Plataea.
