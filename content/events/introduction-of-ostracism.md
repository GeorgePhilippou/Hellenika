<!-- field: summary -->
The Athenian practice of banishing a citizen for ten years by a vote scratched on potsherds, introduced after Cleisthenes' reforms and used from 487 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
A distinctive device of Athenian democracy, intended to control powerful men without killing or trying them.

<!-- field: body -->
Each year, in the sixth prytany, the Athenian Assembly was asked whether an ostracism should be held. If it voted yes, then two months later citizens wrote the name of a man they wished to see exiled on a fragment of pottery (*ostrakon*) and left it at the Agora. If at least 6,000 votes were cast, the man named most often had to leave Attica for ten years, though he did not lose his property or citizenship.

Ancient writers link the law to Cleisthenes, but it was not used until 487 BC, when Hipparchus, son of Charmus, a relative of the Peisistratids, was banished. Later targets included Aristides, Themistocles, Cimon and Thucydides son of Melesias. About 416 BC the Assembly used the procedure against Hyperbolus, a demagogue, and it fell out of use afterwards.

Thousands of ostraka have been found, in the Agora and at other sites, and these show how voters spelt names and abused their targets. Some are inscribed by the same hand, which suggests that the voting was organised. Modern scholars debate whether ostracism was meant to prevent tyranny or to resolve political deadlock.
