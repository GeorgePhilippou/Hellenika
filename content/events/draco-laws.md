<!-- field: summary -->
The first written code of Athenian law, drawn up about 621 BC, remembered for its severity and for the homicide law that survived Solon's reforms.

<!-- field: significance -->
The beginning of written law in Athens and an early step in separating punishment from private revenge.

<!-- field: body -->
Around 621/620 BC, the Athenians appointed Draco to write down their laws. Before that, officials interpreted unwritten custom, usually in favour of the aristocracy. Draco's laws, were reported by later writers to have made death the penalty for almost every offence, so that they were 'written in blood'. Since most of the evidence is late, how many of the laws covered more than killing is uncertain.

The homicide law is the best documented part. A copy cut in stone in 409/408 BC, found in the Agora, restates it and shows it distinguished intentional killing from accidental and justified killing, and specified who could prosecute and when a killer might be accepted back into the community after exile. It set up the Ephetai, a panel of judges, to hear cases, thereby moving blood-feud out of private hands.

Solon repealed Draco's other laws in 594/593 BC, but the homicide law remained in force for centuries.
