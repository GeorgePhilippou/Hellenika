<!-- field: summary -->
On 29 May 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople after a siege of 53 days, ending the Byzantine Empire.

<!-- field: significance -->
The end of the Roman empire in the east after nearly fifteen centuries, and a turning point between the Middle Ages and the modern world.

<!-- field: body -->
By 1453 the Byzantine Empire was reduced to Constantinople and a few territories in Greece, surrounded by the Ottoman state. The young sultan Mehmed II, aged twenty-one, was determined to take the city. He built a fortress on the Bosporus to cut it off, and gathered a huge army and artillery, including giant bombards cast by the Hungarian engineer Orban.

The siege began on 6 April. The defenders — Greeks, with Genoese and Venetian volunteers — numbered only about 7,000 to hold 22 kilometres of walls. For weeks the cannon pounded the land walls, while the defenders repaired the breaches by night. A great chain kept the Ottoman fleet out of the Golden Horn, until on 22 April Mehmed had about seventy ships dragged over the hill behind Galata on greased logs and launched them into the harbour.

Before dawn on 29 May the Ottomans launched a final assault. After hours of fighting, the Genoese commander Giustiniani was wounded and carried away, his men faltered, and Janissaries broke through near the Gate of St Romanus. The emperor Constantine XI died in the fighting. The city was sacked; that day Mehmed rode to Hagia Sophia and had it turned into a mosque. Constantinople became the Ottoman capital. Mystras fell in 1460 and Trebizond, the last Byzantine state, in 1461.
