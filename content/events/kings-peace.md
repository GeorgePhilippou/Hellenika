<!-- field: summary -->
The peace settlement of 387/386 BC, dictated by the Persian king, that ended the Corinthian War and handed the Greek cities of Asia back to Persia.

<!-- field: significance -->
A humiliating reversal of the Persian Wars: a century after Salamis, the Persian king was arbiter of Greek affairs.

<!-- field: body -->
By 387 BC the Corinthian War had become a stalemate. The Spartan envoy Antalcidas negotiated with the Persian king Artaxerxes II, who had funded first Sparta's enemies and now, again, Sparta. The resulting terms, announced in the king's name and accepted by the Greek states at Sparta in 387/386 BC, became known as the King's Peace or the Peace of Antalcidas.

Xenophon preserves its text. The king claimed the Greek cities of Asia Minor, and Cyprus, as his own. All other Greek cities, large and small, were to be autonomous, except for three islands that remained under Athens. Anyone who refused the peace would face the king's war.

The autonomy clause served Sparta, which became the enforcer of the peace. It used it to break up alliances and federations that threatened it: the Boeotian League under Thebes was dissolved, the union of Corinth and Argos was ended, and Mantinea was split back into villages. Meanwhile the Ionian cities, freed from Persia after 479 BC, returned to Persian rule.

To many Greeks, the King's Peace was a disgrace — the Persian king now dictated terms to Greece. The resentment fed calls, voiced by Isocrates and others, for the Greeks to unite against Persia, an idea later taken up by Philip II and Alexander.
