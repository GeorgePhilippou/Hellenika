<!-- field: summary -->
The supposed treaty of about 449 BC between Athens and Persia that formally ended the Persian Wars, whose historicity is still disputed.

<!-- field: significance -->
A test case in the study of how far Greek traditions can be trusted.

<!-- field: body -->
According to Diodorus, Plutarch and orators such as Isocrates, Athens and Persia made a formal peace about 449 BC, after the Athenians' last great expedition to Cyprus, in which Cimon died. The negotiator was Callias, son of Hipponicus, who was among the richest Athenians. The terms are said to have been that the Greek cities of Asia would be autonomous, that the Persian army would stay a day's ride from the coast and Persian warships would stay out of the Aegean, and that Athens would not attack the king's land.

The silence of Herodotus and Thucydides, who both discuss this period, is the main reason for doubt. Theopompus suggested that the inscription reported in Athens was a later invention. Some modern scholars accept a real agreement, perhaps informal, and others treat it as a fourth-century patriotic story, and the issue remains unresolved.

Whatever formal terms existed, there was no more large-scale fighting between Athens and Persia for decades. The Persian king soon became involved again in Greek affairs when he funded Sparta.
