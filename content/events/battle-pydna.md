<!-- field: summary -->
Rome destroyed the Macedonian army in under an hour, abolishing the kingdom of Alexander's successors.

<!-- field: significance -->
The battle that ended the kingdom of Macedon and showed the Roman legion's superiority over the phalanx.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought on 22 June 168 BC near Pydna, on the coast of southern Macedonia below Mount Olympus, between the Roman consul Lucius Aemilius Paullus and King Perseus of Macedon, the last of the Antigonid kings. It was the decisive battle of the Third Macedonian War.

The night before, a lunar eclipse frightened the Roman troops until the officer Gaius Sulpicius Gallus, who had predicted it, explained that it was a natural event. The next day the Macedonian phalanx advanced and at first drove the Romans back; Paullus later said that the oncoming wall of pikes was the most frightening sight of his life.

But the ground was uneven, and as the phalanx moved forward its line broke up and gaps opened. Paullus sent his flexible maniples into them. Once inside the reach of the pikes, Roman legionaries with short swords had every advantage over men holding six-metre poles in both hands. The battle was over in about an hour, with heavy Macedonian losses.

Perseus fled, was captured and was led in chains in Paullus' triumph in Rome. The Macedonian kingdom was abolished and divided into four republics; after a revolt it became a Roman province in 148 BC. Much of the detailed account comes from Polybius, who knew Paullus' family in Rome. Pydna showed the superiority of the legion over the phalanx and ended Macedon as an independent power.
