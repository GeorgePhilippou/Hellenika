<!-- field: summary -->
The ten-year siege of Troy by a Greek coalition under Agamemnon — the central event of Greek legend, and possibly of Greek memory.

<!-- field: body -->
Greek tradition placed the war firmly in history. Eratosthenes dated it to 1194–1184 BC, and Greeks reckoned their heroic past from it.

The evidence divides. Troy VIIa was destroyed by fire around 1180 BC with arrowheads and unburied bodies in the ruins. Hittite archives name a city Wilusa in the region, a king Alaksandu (compare Alexandros, Paris' other name), and repeated friction with Ahhiyawa — a power across the sea generally identified as Mycenaean. A conflict at this place involving Greeks is entirely plausible.

What the evidence does not support is the *Iliad*'s war: a thousand ships, a ten-year siege, a coalition of named kings. The poems reached their form four centuries later, and their world is a composite. The honest position is that something happened at Troy, and the Iliad is not a report of it.
