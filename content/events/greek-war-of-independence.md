<!-- field: summary -->
The war of 1821–1830 in which the Greeks won independence from the Ottoman Empire, with the help of European volunteers and powers.

<!-- field: significance -->
It created the modern Greek state, inspired by the memory of both ancient Greece and Byzantium, and made Athens its capital.

<!-- field: body -->
After the fall of Constantinople in 1453 most Greeks lived under Ottoman rule for nearly four centuries. In the late 18th century a merchant class, the Enlightenment and the example of the French Revolution fed a movement for freedom, and a secret society, the Philiki Etaireia, prepared a revolt.

In February 1821 Alexandros Ypsilantis led a rising in the Danubian principalities, which failed, and in March the Peloponnese rose; 25 March, the Feast of the Annunciation, became the national day. The Greeks took Tripolitsa in the autumn, with a massacre of its Muslim inhabitants, and the Ottomans answered with the massacre of Chios in 1822, painted by Delacroix. The Acropolis was besieged by both sides; legend says the Greeks sent the Ottoman garrison lead to stop them melting the Parthenon's clamps for bullets.

European philhellenes, steeped in classical education, came to fight; the poet Byron died of fever at Missolonghi in 1824. When an Egyptian army under Ibrahim Pasha was close to crushing the revolt, Britain, France and Russia intervened, destroying the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at Navarino in 1827. The London Protocol of 1830 recognised an independent Greece. A Bavarian prince became King Otto, and in 1834 Athens, then a small town, was made the capital; the Acropolis was cleared of its later buildings.
