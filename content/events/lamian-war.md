<!-- field: summary -->
The revolt of Athens and other Greek states against Macedonian rule after Alexander's death, crushed in 322 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The last great bid for Greek independence from Macedon, whose failure ended Athenian democracy.

<!-- field: body -->
When news of Alexander the Great's death reached Greece in 323 BC, Athens, led by the orator Hyperides and the general Leosthenes, rose against Macedonian rule. It was joined by the Aetolians, the Thessalians and other Greek states. Demosthenes, in exile, returned to support the cause.

At first the Greeks did well. Leosthenes defeated Antipater, the Macedonian regent in Europe, and besieged him in the town of Lamia in Thessaly, which gave the war its name. But Leosthenes was killed during the siege, and Macedonian reinforcements arrived from Asia under Leonnatus and then Craterus. In 322 BC the Macedonian fleet destroyed the Athenian navy off the island of Amorgos, ending Athenian sea power for good, and Antipater and Craterus defeated the Greek army at Crannon in Thessaly.

The Greek states surrendered one by one. Antipater imposed harsh terms on Athens: a Macedonian garrison was placed at Mounychia in Piraeus, and the democracy was replaced by an oligarchy in which only men with property of 2,000 drachmas or more had political rights, excluding most citizens. The leaders of the revolt were hunted down; Hyperides was executed and Demosthenes took poison on Calauria.

Athens never again held real independence, though it remained a great centre of culture.
