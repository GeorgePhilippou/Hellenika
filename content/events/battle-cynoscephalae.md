<!-- field: summary -->
The Roman legion defeated the Macedonian phalanx for the first time, on broken ground in Thessaly.

<!-- field: significance -->
The first clear defeat of the Macedonian phalanx by the Roman legion, which made Rome the arbiter of Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought in 197 BC in the hills of Cynoscephalae ("Dog's Heads") in Thessaly, between the Roman army of Titus Quinctius Flamininus, with Aetolian allies, and Philip V of Macedon. It was the decisive battle of the Second Macedonian War.

Neither side planned to fight that day. The two armies, marching on either side of the same ridge in thick fog, collided by accident on rough, broken ground. Philip's right wing formed up on high ground and drove the Roman left downhill. But his left wing was still in marching column when the Romans, with war elephants, attacked it before it could deploy, and it broke.

Then an unnamed Roman tribune took about twenty maniples from the victorious Roman right and led them into the rear of Philip's successful right wing. A phalanx, packed tight with long pikes pointing forward, could not turn to face an attack from behind, and it was cut down where it stood.

Polybius drew the lesson: the phalanx was almost unbeatable head-on on level ground, but helpless once it lost that ground or its unbroken line, while the Roman maniples could adapt. Philip kept his throne but lost his fleet, his possessions in Greece and most of his army. In 196 BC Flamininus proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks" at the Isthmian Games to huge applause — a freedom that in practice meant Roman oversight.
