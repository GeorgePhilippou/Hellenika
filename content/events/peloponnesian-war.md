<!-- field: summary -->
Twenty-seven years of war between Athens and Sparta that exhausted Greece and ended the Athenian empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Thucydides identified the underlying cause plainly, in one of the most quoted single sentences to survive from ancient historiography: the growth of Athenian power, and the fear that growth caused in Sparta, made war inevitable regardless of the immediate disputes that formally triggered it.

The first decade, sometimes called the Archidamian War after the Spartan king who led it, set a land power against a sea power with no way for either to reach the other decisively. Pericles' strategy — bring Attica's rural population inside the city walls, refuse open battle, rely on the fleet and the Long Walls to Piraeus to keep Athens supplied by sea while Spartan armies burned crops they could not otherwise touch — was sound in principle and ruined in practice when overcrowding the entire population inside the walls produced a devastating plague in 430 BC, killing perhaps a quarter of Athens' population over the following years, Pericles among them. A fragile truce, the Peace of Nicias, was agreed in 421 BC, but it satisfied neither side's actual war aims and broke down within a few years amid renewed proxy fighting across the Greek world, including the notorious siege of the small neutral island of Melos, where Thucydides has Athenian envoys tell the Melians bluntly that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" shortly before Athens starved the island into surrender, executed its adult men and enslaved its women and children — a passage many readers have taken as Thucydides' own bleakest judgement on what the war had done to Athenian democracy's moral self-image.

The war's real turning point was self-inflicted. The Sicilian Expedition of 415–413 BC, an ambitious and badly commanded attempt to conquer Syracuse and extend Athenian power into the western Mediterranean, ended in the destruction of an entire Athenian army and fleet, with survivors worked to death in Syracusan stone quarries — a single strategic catastrophe from which Athens' manpower and treasury never fully recovered. Sparta, which had spent the war's first decade with no real navy of its own, then took the step that ultimately decided the whole conflict: accepting Persian gold in exchange for territorial concessions in Ionia, funding did what Spartan hoplites alone never could, building and sustaining a fleet capable of meeting Athens at sea. Under the Spartan admiral Lysander that Persian-funded navy destroyed the last Athenian fleet at Aegospotami in 405 BC, cutting Athens off from the Black Sea grain routes its now-overcrowded population depended on for survival, and forcing an unconditional surrender in 404 BC that dissolved the Athenian empire outright and briefly installed a pro-Spartan oligarchy, the Thirty Tyrants, before democracy was restored within a year.

Both sides emerged exhausted from twenty-seven years of intermittent war, and the real long-term winner was neither of them: Persia, having bankrolled Sparta's eventual victory, used its restored influence to recover effective control of the Greek cities of Asia Minor within a decade of the war's end — the same cities the Greek alliance had originally been formed to liberate from Persian rule two generations earlier.
