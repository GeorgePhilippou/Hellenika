<!-- field: summary -->
Twenty-seven years of war between Athens and Sparta that exhausted Greece and ended the Athenian empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Thucydides identified the underlying cause plainly, in one of the most quoted sentences of ancient historiography: the growth of Athenian power, and the fear it caused in Sparta, made war inevitable whatever the immediate disputes that formally triggered it.

The first decade, sometimes called the Archidamian War after the Spartan king who led it, set a land power against a sea power with no way for either to reach the other decisively. Pericles' strategy was to bring Attica's rural population inside the city walls, refuse open battle, and rely on the fleet and the Long Walls to Piraeus to keep Athens supplied by sea while Spartan armies burned crops they could not otherwise touch. It was sound in principle. In practice, crowding the entire population inside the walls produced a devastating plague in 430 BC, which killed perhaps a quarter of Athens' population over the following years, Pericles among them.

A fragile truce, the Peace of Nicias, was agreed in 421 BC. It satisfied neither side's war aims and broke down within a few years amid renewed proxy fighting across the Greek world. One episode was the siege of the small neutral island of Melos. Thucydides has the Athenian envoys tell the Melians bluntly that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must". Athens then starved the island into surrender, executed its adult men and enslaved its women and children. Many readers take the passage as Thucydides' bleakest judgement on what the war had done to Athenian democracy's moral self-image.

The war's real turning point was self-inflicted. The Sicilian Expedition of 415–413 BC was an ambitious and badly commanded attempt to conquer Syracuse and extend Athenian power into the western Mediterranean. It ended in the destruction of an entire Athenian army and fleet, with the survivors worked to death in Syracusan stone quarries. Athens' manpower and treasury never fully recovered.

Sparta, which had spent the war's first decade with no real navy, then took the step that ultimately decided the conflict. It accepted Persian gold in exchange for territorial concessions in Ionia. That funding did what Spartan hoplites alone never could: it built and sustained a fleet capable of meeting Athens at sea. Under the admiral Lysander, this navy destroyed the last Athenian fleet at Aegospotami in 405 BC, cutting Athens off from the Black Sea grain its overcrowded population depended on. Athens surrendered unconditionally in 404 BC. Its empire was dissolved, and a pro-Spartan oligarchy, the Thirty Tyrants, was briefly installed before democracy was restored within a year.

Both sides emerged exhausted from twenty-seven years of intermittent war, and the real long-term winner was neither. Persia had bankrolled Sparta's victory, and used its restored influence to recover control of the Greek cities of Asia Minor, a settlement formalised in the King's Peace of 387 BC. They were the same cities the Greek alliance had originally been formed to liberate from Persian rule two generations earlier.
