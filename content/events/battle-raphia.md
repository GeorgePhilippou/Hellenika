<!-- field: summary -->
The great battle of 217 BC near Gaza in which Ptolemy IV defeated Antiochus III, with Egyptians fighting in the phalanx for the first time.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the largest battles of the Hellenistic age; arming the Egyptians saved Ptolemaic Syria but, Polybius thought, encouraged native revolt.

<!-- field: body -->
In the Fourth Syrian War the young Seleucid king Antiochus III invaded Ptolemaic southern Syria and Palestine. Egypt's ministers, short of Greek and Macedonian troops, took an unusual step: they trained about 20,000 native Egyptians to fight in the Macedonian phalanx.

The armies met on 22 June 217 BC near Raphia (modern Rafah), south of Gaza. Polybius, our main source, gives Ptolemy IV about 70,000 infantry, 5,000 cavalry and 73 African elephants, and Antiochus 62,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry and 102 Indian elephants. The battle was a rare clash of elephant against elephant: the smaller African forest elephants of the Ptolemies fled from the larger Indian ones. Antiochus won on his right wing and pursued too far, while in the centre the Ptolemaic phalanx, with the Egyptian contingent, broke the Seleucid infantry.

Ptolemy kept southern Syria, and the Egyptian priests celebrated the victory in a trilingual decree, the Raphia Decree. Polybius believed that the Egyptians, proud of their success, soon began to resist Greek rule, and from 206 BC a great revolt broke out in Upper Egypt.
