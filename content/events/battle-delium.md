<!-- field: summary -->
Thebes led a Boeotian army to a decisive victory over an invading Athenian force at Delium in 424 BC, in which Socrates served and reportedly distinguished himself in the retreat.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens attempted to knock Boeotia, Thebes' regional league, out of the war by seizing and fortifying the sanctuary of Delium as a forward base. A Boeotian army caught the Athenian force as it withdrew, and won decisively — one of the largest hoplite battles of the war's first phase and a serious check on Athenian ambitions on land.

Among the Athenian hoplites that day was Socrates, then in his mid-forties; Plato and other later sources describe him as remaining calm and orderly during the rout, a detail later writers used to illustrate the same self-possession he showed throughout his life, on the battlefield as much as in the agora.

The Theban infantry's notably deep formation at Delium is sometimes read by modern historians as an early ancestor of the much deeper, more radical phalanx Epaminondas would use to break Sparta at Leuctra half a century later — a continuous Theban tactical tradition of concentrating force on one flank rather than spreading it evenly along the line.
