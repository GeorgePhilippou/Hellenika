Alexander's hardest-fought victory, against Porus and his war elephants in monsoon rain on the Jhelum river.

<!-- field: significance -->
Alexander's hardest battle and his last great victory, fought at the eastern limit of his conquests.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought in May 326 BC on the Hydaspes (modern Jhelum) river in the Punjab, between Alexander the Great and Porus, an Indian king who ruled the land beyond the river. It was Alexander's last great battle.

The river was swollen by monsoon rains, and Porus held the far bank with perhaps 200 war elephants, which horses will not face. Alexander spent weeks staging noisy movements along the bank at night until Porus' scouts stopped responding. Then he crossed for real some 27 km upstream, hidden by a violent storm.

The battle was among the hardest of his career. Alexander's cavalry drew off and defeated the Indian horse. The phalanx opened lanes to let the charging elephants through, then attacked their drivers and their trunks; maddened, wounded elephants trampled soldiers on both sides. Eventually the Indian army was surrounded and broke.

Porus fought on bravely until captured. Asked how he wished to be treated, he is said to have replied "like a king". Alexander restored him to his kingdom and enlarged it. Alexander's horse Bucephalus died around this time, and Alexander founded the city of Bucephala in its memory, along with another, Nicaea.

Soon afterwards, at the Hyphasis river, Alexander's exhausted army refused to go further east, and he turned back.
