<!-- field: summary -->
The defeat of the Byzantine army by the Seljuk Turks in 1071 in eastern Anatolia, followed by the loss of most of Asia Minor.

<!-- field: significance -->
The beginning of the Turkish settlement of Anatolia, the heartland of Byzantium for centuries, and an indirect cause of the Crusades.

<!-- field: body -->
In the 11th century the Seljuk Turks, recent converts to Islam from Central Asia, took Persia and Baghdad and began raiding the eastern provinces of the Byzantine Empire. In 1071 the emperor Romanos IV Diogenes, a soldier, marched east with a large but poorly trained army to secure the frontier in Armenia.

Near the fortress of Manzikert (modern Malazgirt, north of Lake Van) he met the sultan Alp Arslan. On 26 August, after a day of pursuing the Turkish horse archers, Romanos ordered a withdrawal to camp; the order was misunderstood, the lines broke, and the Turks attacked. The rearguard under Andronikos Doukas, a member of a rival family, withdrew without fighting. Romanos was wounded and captured. Alp Arslan treated him honourably and released him after a week in return for a ransom and a treaty.

The real disaster followed. In Constantinople the Doukas family deposed Romanos, who was blinded and died, and civil war raged for a decade. Turkish bands moved into the undefended interior of Asia Minor, and by the 1080s they had reached the Aegean coast. The emperor Alexios I Komnenos appealed to the west for help, which led to the First Crusade in 1096.
