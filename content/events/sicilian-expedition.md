<!-- field: summary -->
Athens sent its largest ever expedition against Syracuse; it was annihilated, and the empire never recovered.

<!-- field: body -->
Alcibiades persuaded the Athenian assembly to attack Syracuse — a city roughly as large and wealthy as Athens itself, on the far side of the Mediterranean, while Athens was still actively at war with Sparta at home, an extraordinary strategic overreach even by the standards of a state already committed to a long war. Nicias, a far more cautious general, opposed the whole venture, and then tried to talk the assembly out of it by deliberately describing an enormous force as the bare minimum needed to have any chance of success, hoping the sheer scale would frighten them into abandoning the plan. Instead the assembly enthusiastically voted him exactly that enormous force, and put him in joint command of an operation he himself still believed, right up to its launch, was a serious mistake.

Alcibiades was recalled to Athens for trial on serious religious charges almost immediately after the fleet sailed, and rather than return to face near-certain execution, he defected to Sparta, where he offered the Spartans pointed strategic advice against his own former city. The siege of Syracuse stalled without his energetic leadership; Sparta sent an effective general, Gylippus, who reorganised the city's defence and broke the Athenian blockade. Substantial Athenian reinforcements arrived under Demosthenes and failed in turn, unable to reverse a campaign that had already lost its momentum.

When the Athenian fleet was finally destroyed trying to break out of the Great Harbour, the surviving soldiers attempted a desperate overland retreat and were cut down or captured almost to the last man. Something like 40,000 men were lost in total across the whole campaign, a catastrophic blow from which the Athenian empire never fully recovered its former strength. Captured prisoners were held for weeks in the open stone quarries outside the city with minimal food or water; Plutarch reports, whether reliably or not, that some individual captives won noticeably better treatment from their Sicilian captors by reciting passages of Euripides, whose poetry was apparently much admired even among ordinary Syracusans.
