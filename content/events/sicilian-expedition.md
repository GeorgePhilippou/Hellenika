<!-- field: summary -->
Athens sent its largest ever expedition against Syracuse; it was annihilated, and the empire never recovered.

<!-- field: significance -->
The disaster that turned the Peloponnesian War against Athens.

<!-- field: body -->
The Athenian expedition against Syracuse in Sicily in 415–413 BC, during a lull in the Peloponnesian War. Athens hoped to conquer the richest Greek city of the west and add Sicily's wealth and grain to its empire.

Alcibiades urged the attack; the cautious general Nicias opposed it and, trying to deter the Assembly, said a huge force would be needed. The Assembly voted him exactly that: about 130 warships and over 5,000 hoplites, under Nicias, Alcibiades and Lamachus. Soon after the fleet sailed, Alcibiades was recalled to face charges of sacrilege, and instead fled to Sparta, where he advised the Spartans against Athens.

The Athenians besieged Syracuse but moved slowly. Lamachus was killed, and the Spartan general Gylippus arrived to organise the defence and break the Athenian siege wall. Large reinforcements under Demosthenes in 413 BC failed to turn the tide. The Athenian fleet was defeated in the Great Harbour of Syracuse, and an attempt to break out by land ended in disaster. Nicias and Demosthenes were executed; thousands of prisoners were held in the stone quarries of Syracuse, where many died. Plutarch says some won better treatment by reciting Euripides.

Athens lost perhaps 40,000 men in all, including allies, and some 200 ships. Thucydides called it the greatest event of the war. Persia and Athens' subject allies saw their chance, Sparta built a fleet, and although Athens fought on for nine more years, the Sicilian disaster turned the war decisively against it.
