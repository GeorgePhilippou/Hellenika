<!-- field: summary -->
Demetrius Poliorcetes destroyed a Ptolemaic fleet off Cyprus — a victory that pushed the Successors to start calling themselves kings.

<!-- field: significance -->
A naval victory that led Antigonus and Demetrius to take the title of king — and the other Successors to follow, formally ending the fiction of Alexander's united empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Fought in 306 BC off Salamis on the east coast of Cyprus, between Demetrius Poliorcetes, son of Antigonus I, and the fleet of Ptolemy I of Egypt.

Demetrius invaded Cyprus, then held by Ptolemy, defeated Ptolemy's brother Menelaus and besieged him in the city of Salamis. Ptolemy sailed to the rescue with about 140 warships. Demetrius met him in open water outside the harbour and won an overwhelming victory: most of Ptolemy's fleet was sunk or captured, along with thousands of soldiers. Ptolemy escaped to Egypt, and Menelaus surrendered the city and the whole island.

The battle had a political consequence far beyond Cyprus. On hearing the news, Antigonus had himself and Demetrius proclaimed kings (*basileus*) — the first of Alexander's successors to take the title openly. All of Alexander's own family had by then been killed. Within about a year Ptolemy, Seleucus, Lysimachus and Cassander had all done the same. The pretence that they were governing a single empire for Alexander's heirs was over; from now on there were rival Hellenistic kingdoms.

The next year Demetrius turned on Rhodes, Ptolemy's ally, with a vast siege that failed. Ptolemy recovered Cyprus in 295 BC, and it remained a Ptolemaic possession until the Roman period.
