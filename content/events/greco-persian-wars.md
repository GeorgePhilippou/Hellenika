<!-- field: summary -->
Two Persian invasions of Greece, repelled at Marathon and then at Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea, that reshaped the Aegean world for a century.

<!-- field: body -->
The wars opened with the Ionian Revolt of 499–494 BC, a failed uprising by Greek cities on the Anatolian coast against Persian rule, in which Athens' brief involvement gave Darius I a pretext for direct action against the mainland. His 490 BC expedition was stopped at Marathon; a decade later his son Xerxes returned with a vastly larger force, winning at Thermopylae and burning Athens before losing the fleet at Salamis and, the following year, the army at Plataea, with the simultaneous naval victory at Mycale finishing Persian sea power in the Aegean.

Fighting continued at a lower intensity for another generation. Athens and its allies formed the Delian League in 478 BC explicitly to keep fighting Persia and liberate remaining Greek cities under its rule, winning a further major victory under Cimon at the Eurymedon river around 466 BC. Ancient tradition holds that the conflict was formally ended by the Peace of Callias in 449 BC, in which Persia recognised the autonomy of the Ionian Greek cities — though the treaty's existence and exact terms are disputed by modern historians, since no Greek source before the fourth century mentions it directly.

The war's most lasting consequence was institutional rather than territorial: the alliance built to fight Persia did not disband once Persia was contained, and within a generation had become the Athenian empire.
