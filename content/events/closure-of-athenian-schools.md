<!-- field: summary -->
In 529 Justinian banned pagans from teaching philosophy at Athens, ending the Neoplatonist school that claimed descent from Plato's Academy.

<!-- field: significance -->
Often taken as the symbolic end of ancient Greek philosophy, and of classical antiquity itself.

<!-- field: body -->
In the 5th century Athens was still a centre of pagan philosophy. A Neoplatonist school, which saw itself as the heir of Plato's Academy, flourished under Proclus (died 485), whose house has been excavated south of the Acropolis. Its last head was Damascius.

Justinian was determined to root out paganism. In 529, the chronicler John Malalas reports, he sent a decree to Athens forbidding anyone to teach philosophy or interpret the law there. Other laws of the same years barred pagans from teaching and from public posts and confiscated their endowments.

Around 531 Damascius and six other philosophers, including Simplicius, left for Persia, hoping to find in King Khosrow I a philosopher-king. They were disappointed, and in 532 a peace treaty between Rome and Persia guaranteed that they could return and live by their beliefs. Simplicius went on writing commentaries on Aristotle, perhaps in Harran in Syria, which preserve many fragments of earlier philosophers.

Historians debate whether the school was closed by force or simply lost its funds and students. Either way, 529 has become a symbolic date for the end of the ancient world, coinciding with the founding of Benedict's monastery at Monte Cassino.
