<!-- field: summary -->
The visit of the apostle Paul to Athens around AD 50, when he debated philosophers in the Agora and spoke on the Areopagus.

<!-- field: significance -->
The symbolic meeting of Christianity and Greek philosophy, at the start of the Christianisation of the Greek world.

<!-- field: body -->
Paul, a Greek-speaking Jew from Tarsus who had become a follower of Jesus, travelled through Asia Minor and Greece in the late 40s and 50s AD founding Christian communities. On his second journey, after preaching at Philippi and Thessalonica, he came to Athens around AD 50.

According to the Acts of the Apostles, he was disturbed by the city's many statues of gods. He argued in the synagogue and daily in the Agora, where Epicurean and Stoic philosophers called him a "babbler" and brought him to the Areopagus, the ancient council named after the Hill of Ares. There he told them that he had seen an altar inscribed "to an unknown god", and that this god, who made the world and does not live in temples, he now proclaimed to them, quoting a Greek poet. When he spoke of the resurrection of the dead, some laughed; a few, including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris, joined him.

Paul moved on to Corinth, where he stayed eighteen months, and later wrote his letters to the Corinthians. An inscription found at Delphi, naming Gallio as governor of Achaea, dates Paul's stay at Corinth to about AD 51–52. The speech is commemorated by a bronze plaque on the Areopagus.
