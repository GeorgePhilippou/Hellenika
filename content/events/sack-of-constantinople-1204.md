<!-- field: summary -->
In April 1204 the knights of the Fourth Crusade stormed Constantinople, sacked it for three days and divided the Byzantine Empire.

<!-- field: significance -->
The greatest disaster in Byzantine history; the empire never fully recovered, and the bitterness it caused poisoned relations between Greek and Latin Christians.

<!-- field: body -->
The Fourth Crusade set out in 1202 to attack Egypt, but the crusaders could not pay the Venetians for the fleet they had ordered. They agreed to help a Byzantine prince, Alexios Angelos, regain his father's throne in return for money and troops. In 1203 they put him in power in Constantinople, but he could not pay, and was murdered by a rival. Resolved to take what they were owed, the crusaders and Venetians attacked the city from the sea walls along the Golden Horn.

On 12 April 1204 they broke in, and for three days they plundered the richest city in Christendom. Churches and palaces were looted, Hagia Sophia was stripped, and countless ancient bronze statues were melted down for coin. Relics were sent to churches across western Europe, and the Venetians carried off the four bronze horses of the Hippodrome, which still stand at St Mark's in Venice. The Byzantine historian Niketas Choniates, who fled the city, wrote a lament for it.

The crusaders crowned a Latin emperor and divided the empire among themselves; Venice took key islands and ports. Byzantine successor states survived at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond, and in 1261 the emperor of Nicaea, Michael VIII Palaiologos, recovered Constantinople.
