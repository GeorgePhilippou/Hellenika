<!-- field: summary -->
In 1822 Champollion showed how to read Egyptian hieroglyphs, using the Greek text of the Rosetta Stone as his key.

<!-- field: significance -->
It opened three thousand years of Egyptian history, and showed how a Greek translation could unlock a lost script.

<!-- field: body -->
The Rosetta Stone, found by French soldiers in 1799, carried the same decree of 196 BC in hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek. Scholars could read the Greek, which honoured Ptolemy V, and so knew what the Egyptian texts said; the problem was how the scripts worked. Many believed hieroglyphs were purely symbolic pictures.

The English polymath Thomas Young recognised that the oval rings (cartouches) contained royal names, and identified some signs in the name of Ptolemy. The French scholar Jean-François Champollion, who had studied Coptic, the last stage of the Egyptian language, went further. Comparing the cartouche of Ptolemy on the Rosetta Stone with that of Cleopatra on an obelisk from Philae, he worked out the sound values of several signs, and then read the names of other rulers, including the pharaohs Ramesses and Thutmose — proving that the script was used for Egyptian names, not just Greek ones.

On 27 September 1822 he presented his results in a letter to Bon-Joseph Dacier, secretary of the Académie des Inscriptions in Paris. In his *Précis* of 1824 he showed that hieroglyphs combined sound-signs and meaning-signs. Young and Champollion's supporters long disputed the credit. Later trilingual decrees such as the Canopus Decree confirmed his system.
