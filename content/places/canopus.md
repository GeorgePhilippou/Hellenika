<!-- field: summary -->
A western Delta port and pilgrimage centre later absorbed into Alexandria’s maritime hinterland.

<!-- field: significance -->
A major meeting place for Egyptian and Greek religious traditions near the Canopic mouth of the Nile.

<!-- field: body -->
Canopus lay east of Alexandria near the westernmost mouth of the Nile and was linked by canals to neighbouring Thonis-Heracleion. Its Egyptian sacred landscape centred on Osiris, while Greek tradition named the place for Canopus, the helmsman of Menelaus who was supposedly buried there after dying from a snakebite. The story is an aetiology, not a recoverable foundation history.

Under the Ptolemies the city became part of Alexandria’s maritime and religious hinterland. The annual Mysteries of Osiris involved processions and ritual vessels moving between sanctuaries around the bay. Greek and Egyptian inscriptions, images and architectural forms occur together, showing interaction without requiring that the two religious traditions simply merged into one.

Earthquakes, liquefaction, subsidence and rising water gradually submerged much of the settlement. Underwater survey in Abu Qir Bay has recovered colossal statuary, temple blocks, inscriptions, coins and ritual equipment. These finds restore Canopus as a substantial cult and harbour centre rather than the pleasure resort emphasised by some later Greek and Roman writers.
