<!-- field: summary -->
A large Neolithic village in Thessaly, occupied from about 6800 BC, which gives its name to the Middle Neolithic culture of the region.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the earliest and largest farming villages in Europe.

<!-- field: body -->
Sesklo lies in the hills west of Volos, in the fertile plain of Thessaly, which had the densest population of Neolithic Greece. It was excavated by Christos Tsountas in 1901–02 and again by Dimitrios Theocharis between 1956 and 1977.

The settlement began early in the Neolithic, around 6800 BC, and grew into one of the largest villages of its time. It had two parts: a small hill, the "acropolis", with closely packed houses, and a much larger surrounding settlement. The houses had stone foundations and walls of mudbrick or wattle and daub, and some were two-storeyed. A larger building on the hilltop has been interpreted as a chief's house or a communal hall. How many people lived there at its height is disputed, with estimates ranging from a few hundred to several thousand.

The villagers grew wheat, barley and pulses and kept sheep and goats. Sesklo gives its name to the Middle Neolithic culture of Thessaly (c. 6000–5300 BC), known for fine pottery painted with red geometric patterns on a white surface, and for clay figurines. Parts of the settlement were burned around the middle of the 6th millennium, and in the Late Neolithic the nearby village of Dimini became more important.
