<!-- field: summary -->
The unfortified Palace of Nestor, whose burned archive of over a thousand Linear B tablets is the richest record of Mycenaean administration.

<!-- field: significance -->
The fire that destroyed the palace baked its clay tablets hard, preserving a detailed snapshot of a Bronze Age state's final year.

<!-- field: body -->
The Palace of Nestor stands on the hill of Ano Englianos in Messenia, in the south-western Peloponnese, near the Bay of Navarino. It flourished around 1400–1180 BC as the centre of a Mycenaean kingdom, and is named after Nestor, the aged king of Pylos in Homer.

Unusually for a Mycenaean palace, it had no fortification wall. At its heart was a throne room (megaron) with a great round hearth ringed by four columns and a painted floor; nearby a bathroom still holds its clay bathtub. Storerooms held thousands of pots for wine and olive oil, the products of an economy run by the palace.

Carl Blegen found the palace in 1939, and on the first day of digging uncovered its archive room, holding more than a thousand Linear B tablets. Excavation resumed in 1952, the year Michael Ventris showed that Linear B was Greek; a Pylos tablet listing tripods soon confirmed his decipherment. The tablets, baked hard when the palace burned, record land holdings, rations, bronze for smiths, offerings to gods including Poseidon and Zeus, and the posting of coastal watchers, as if a threat from the sea was expected. They cover only the palace's last months, and do not say who or what destroyed it around 1180 BC.

In 2015 Jack Davis and Sharon Stocker found the unlooted grave of the "Griffin Warrior" near the palace, dating to around 1450 BC. It held more than 1,400 objects, including gold rings and the finely carved Pylos Combat Agate, showing the wealth of the elite before the palace was built.
