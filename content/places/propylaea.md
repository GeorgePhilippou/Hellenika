<!-- field: summary -->
The marble gateway to the Acropolis of Athens, built by Mnesicles in 437–432 BC and never completed.

<!-- field: significance -->
The grandest entrance building of classical Greece and a key part of Pericles' Acropolis.

<!-- field: body -->
After the Parthenon, Pericles' programme turned to a new entrance for the Acropolis, replacing an older gateway damaged by the Persians. Mnesicles' Propylaea was built of Pentelic marble from 437 to 432 BC. Its accounts, inscribed on stone, record a cost of over 2,000 talents for the whole building, though the figure is debated.

The central building had a Doric portico of six columns facing west, a passage flanked by Ionic columns rising up the slope, and a wall pierced by five doors, the central one wide enough for sacrificial animals and the chariots of the Panathenaic procession. Two wings projected forward; the north one held paintings, described by Pausanias. A planned south-eastern hall was never built, and some blocks still carry their unremoved lifting bosses, showing that the work stopped unfinished when war came in 431.

In later centuries the Propylaea became part of a palace of the Frankish dukes of Athens and then an Ottoman fortress. In 1656 lightning ignited gunpowder stored inside, causing a devastating explosion. Restoration has continued since the 19th century.
