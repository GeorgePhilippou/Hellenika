<!-- field: summary -->
Aristotle's school in a gymnasium sacred to Apollo, from which the Peripatetics carried out organised research.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Lyceum turned Aristotle's programme of collecting and classifying knowledge into a lasting institution.

<!-- field: body -->
The Lyceum lay on the eastern side of Athens, outside the city walls, near a shrine of Apollo Lykeios. It was a public gymnasium, and Aristotle, who was not an Athenian citizen and could not own property, used it when he returned to Athens in 335 BC after tutoring Alexander. He taught in the covered walk, the peripatos, which gave his followers the name Peripatetics.

The school was more research institute than teaching circle. Aristotle and his associates gathered information on a large scale: descriptions of animals and plants, constitutions of Greek cities, records of dramatic contests, and histories of earlier philosophers. Diogenes Laertius records that the members ate together and held a monthly banquet, with rules for the conduct of the school. When Aristotle left Athens in 322 BC, Theophrastus took over and inherited the books.

The Lyceum flourished under Theophrastus and then declined. Strato of Lampsacus, who led it after 288, was interested in physics and natural science, but later heads were less distinguished. By the first century BC Athens was more famous for the Stoics and Epicureans, and a story about the loss and recovery of Aristotle's library in a cellar in Asia Minor, told by Strabo and Plutarch, explains why his lecture-notes were published only in the first century BC by Andronicus of Rhodes.

The gymnasium's remains were discovered in 1996 during the construction of a museum in central Athens. The excavated area includes a palaestra, a courtyard for wrestling, with rooms for changing and bathing. It has been open to the public as an archaeological park since 2014.
