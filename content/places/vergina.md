<!-- field: summary -->
The old royal capital of Macedon, where an unlooted royal tomb was found in 1977 under a great mound.

<!-- field: significance -->
The richest unplundered royal burial ever excavated in Greece, and the strongest candidate for the tomb of Philip II.

<!-- field: body -->
Vergina, in the foothills south-west of Thessaloniki in northern Greece, is the site of Aigai, the first capital of the kingdom of Macedon. Even after the court moved to Pella around 400 BC, Aigai remained the ceremonial centre where Macedonian kings were buried. Philip II was assassinated in its theatre in 336 BC.

The site holds a large palace, a theatre and a huge cemetery of burial mounds. Léon Heuzey excavated part of the palace in 1861. In 1977 Manolis Andronikos excavated the Great Tumulus, a mound 110 m across long thought to be natural. Beneath it were several royal tombs, probably covered deliberately around 270 BC to protect them after Celtic raiders had looted the cemetery.

Tomb I, already robbed in antiquity, preserves a fine wall painting of Hades carrying off Persephone. Tomb II was untouched. Behind its painted façade, with its frieze of a royal hunt, the main chamber held a gold casket (larnax) with the sixteen-rayed Macedonian star on the lid, containing cremated bones wrapped in purple and gold cloth and a gold oak wreath. With them were armour, a ceremonial shield, silver drinking vessels and small ivory portrait heads, one resembling Philip II. The antechamber held a second casket with the remains of a young woman.

The main burial is most often identified as Philip II, father of Alexander the Great; others argue for Alexander's half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus, buried in 316 BC, and scientific studies of the bones have reached opposite conclusions. Either way it is the richest unlooted royal tomb ever found in Greece. The finds are displayed in a museum built inside the mound, and the site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.
