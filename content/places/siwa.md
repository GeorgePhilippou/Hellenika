<!-- field: summary -->
A remote oasis in the Egyptian desert with an oracle of Amun, identified with Zeus, which Alexander the Great visited in 331 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The one non-Greek oracle that the Greeks regularly consulted, and the place most closely linked with Alexander's claim to divine descent.

<!-- field: body -->
Siwa is a green oasis in the western desert of Egypt, about 50 kilometres from the Libyan border. Its oracle was dedicated to Amun, an Egyptian god whom the Greeks identified with Zeus, and its temple stood on a rock at Aghurmi. The Greek colonists of nearby Cyrene made use of it from the sixth century BC, and Pindar wrote a hymn to Ammon.

Herodotus tells how Cambyses sent an army from Thebes to destroy the oracle, and how it was lost in a sandstorm. Alexander, having occupied Egypt, made a difficult journey to Siwa in 331 BC. Accounts of what happened differ, but writers such as Plutarch and Arrian say that the priest hailed him as son of Amun, and that Alexander afterwards treated the god as his father. Modern historians debate how much it mattered to him.

The oasis kept its native Berber-speaking population, and its remains include the Temple of the Oracle and the nearby Temple of Amun at Umm Ubayd.
