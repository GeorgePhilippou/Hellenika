<!-- field: summary -->
The triple land walls of Constantinople, built in 413 and strengthened in 447, which protected the city for over a thousand years.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most successful fortifications of the Middle Ages, which saved Constantinople — and arguably Europe — from many invaders.

<!-- field: body -->
Constantinople was protected by the sea on two sides, but its western side, facing Thrace, needed strong defences. In 413, under the young emperor Theodosius II, the prefect Anthemius built a new line of walls about 1.5 kilometres west of Constantine's, running some 6 kilometres from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn.

In 447 an earthquake brought down much of the wall just as Attila's Huns approached. The people of the city, organised by the circus factions, rebuilt it in about two months, as an inscription records, and added an outer wall and a broad moat. The result was a triple barrier: a moat about 20 metres wide, an outer wall, and an inner wall about 12 metres high with 96 towers.

The walls defeated Avars and Persians in 626, Arabs in 674–678 and 717–718, and Bulgars and Rus' later. In 1204 the Crusaders entered the city from the sea walls along the Golden Horn, not through the land walls. Only in 1453 did Mehmed II's huge cannon batter breaches in them, and the Ottomans broke through on 29 May. Long stretches, partly restored, still run across Istanbul.
