<!-- field: summary -->
Island power in the Saronic Gulf, an early coin-issuer and sea trader, and Athens's bitter neighbour.

<!-- field: significance -->
Aegina shows that Athens did not have a naval monopoly before 480 BC, and that maritime rivalry drove the building of the Athenian fleet.

<!-- field: body -->
Aegina lies in the Saronic Gulf, within sight of Athens. Its soil is poor, and its people turned to the sea: by the sixth century it was among the most prosperous trading states in Greece, with contacts across the Aegean and in Egypt. Its silver coins, stamped with a sea turtle, were among the first in mainland Greece, and the Aeginetan weight standard was widely used, notably in the Peloponnese.

Aegina was Athens's oldest rival. Herodotus tells a tangled story of a feud that began with a dispute over statues of two goddesses and turned into raids and reprisals, and he gives it as part of the background to Athenian naval building. When Persia demanded submission in 491, Aegina complied; Athens complained to Sparta, and King Cleomenes came to arrest the medisers. Yet when Xerxes arrived in 480 the Aeginetans fought bravely at Salamis and were named the best-performing state.

The Temple of Aphaia, built around 500 BC on a ridge above the sea, is one of the best-preserved Doric temples in Greece, and its pedimental sculptures, taken to Munich in 1811, show the shift from the archaic to the early classical style in the two gables. Pindar composed odes for Aeginetan victors in athletic contests, which give a sense of the island's cultural self-image, one based on descent from the heroes Aiakos and Achilles.

Athens brought Aegina into its empire in 457 and evicted its population in 431, settling the island with Athenians. Sparta settled the exiles at Thyrea. Aegina never recovered its earlier standing.
