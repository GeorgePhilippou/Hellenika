<!-- field: summary -->
One of the largest cities of the Late Bronze Age Mediterranean, its harbour trade reaching from Scandinavia to Mesopotamia, now largely invisible above ground beside a salt lake near Larnaca.

<!-- field: significance -->
Long-running Swedish excavations and geophysical survey have mapped a city on a scale that reframes how urbanised Late Bronze Age Cyprus actually was.

<!-- field: body -->
Hala Sultan Tekke -- named for a later Ottoman-era mosque nearby, unrelated to the Bronze Age remains beneath the modern name -- lay on what was then a natural harbour beside the Larnaca Salt Lake, since silted and cut off from the sea. At an estimated 25 to 50 hectares it ranks among the largest urban settlements known anywhere in the Late Bronze Age Mediterranean.

Decades of excavation by the University of Gothenburg, combined with georadar and magnetometer survey of unexcavated areas, have identified multiple distinct city quarters, a cemetery, and probable fortification walls. The material recovered is strikingly international: imports and exports connect the city to Sardinia and Scandinavia in the west and Mesopotamia and Egypt in the east, built on a local economy of copper working and purple-dye textile production.

Like many Late Bronze Age centres across the eastern Mediterranean, Hala Sultan Tekke was destroyed or abandoned during the twelfth century BC, the period of widespread disruption conventionally associated with the "Sea Peoples" and the broader Bronze Age collapse. Unlike Enkomi, its population's fate afterward is not clearly tied to any specific successor settlement.
