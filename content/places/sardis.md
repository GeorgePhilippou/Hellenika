<!-- field: summary -->
The capital of the kingdom of Lydia in western Asia Minor, city of Croesus and birthplace of coinage, and later a Persian provincial capital.

<!-- field: significance -->
The rich Lydian capital where coinage began, whose fall to Persia brought the Persian Empire into contact with the Greeks.

<!-- field: body -->
Sardis lay in the valley of the river Hermus in western Asia Minor, below a steep acropolis. It was the capital of the kingdom of Lydia, which under the Mermnad dynasty of the 7th and 6th centuries BC dominated western Anatolia and the Greek cities of Ionia. Its wealth came partly from the gold in the Pactolus river, which flowed through the city. Excavations have uncovered the workshops where Lydians refined gold and silver, and it was in Lydia, in the late 7th century BC, that the first coins were struck.

Sardis was the city of King Croesus, whose wealth was legendary. Around 546 BC Cyrus the Great captured it, ending the Lydian kingdom, and it became the capital of the Persian province of Lydia and the western end of the Royal Road to Susa. In 498 BC Ionian rebels, with Athenian help, burned the city, an act Persia remembered when it invaded Greece.

Alexander took Sardis without a fight in 334 BC. Under the Seleucids and Romans it remained an important city; the great temple of Artemis was built in the Hellenistic period, and in Roman times it had one of the largest synagogues of the ancient world.

The Archaeological Exploration of Sardis, led by Harvard and Cornell, has excavated the city since 1958.
