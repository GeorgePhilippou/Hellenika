<!-- field: summary -->
A cave on the coast of the Argolid used for over 30,000 years, whose deposits trace the change from hunting to farming in Greece.

<!-- field: significance -->
The longest sequence of prehistoric occupation in Greece, and the earliest evidence of sea travel in the Aegean.

<!-- field: body -->
Franchthi Cave lies on a bay of the southern Argolid, opposite the village of Kilada. It was excavated from 1967 to 1979 by Thomas W. Jacobsen of Indiana University, and its deposits, many metres deep, revealed a history of use stretching from about 38,000 BC, in the Upper Palaeolithic, to the end of the Neolithic around 3000 BC.

In the Ice Age, when the sea was much lower, the cave looked out over a coastal plain. Its early inhabitants hunted wild horse, deer and wild cattle. Around 11,000 BC flakes of obsidian, a volcanic glass, appear; analysis has shown it came from the island of Melos, more than 100 kilometres away across open sea, so these hunter-gatherers must have travelled by boat. In the Mesolithic, after about 8000 BC, the people of Franchthi caught large fish such as tuna.

Around 7000 BC domesticated sheep and goats, wheat, barley and lentils appear, along with pottery soon afterwards — part of the arrival of farming in Greece. A farming village grew up on the shore in front of the cave, now partly under the sea. Burials, including that of a young man of the Mesolithic, were found inside the cave.
