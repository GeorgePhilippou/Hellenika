<!-- field: summary -->
The grandest Mycenaean beehive tomb, built around 1250 BC outside Mycenae, whose stone dome stayed the largest in the world for over a thousand years.

<!-- field: significance -->
The supreme achievement of Mycenaean architecture and engineering, and the clearest sign of the wealth and power of Mycenae's kings.

<!-- field: body -->
The Treasury of Atreus is a *tholos*, or beehive tomb, cut into a hillside just outside the citadel of Mycenae in the Argolid, and built around 1250 BC. Pausanias, who saw it in the 2nd century AD, repeated the local belief that it was the treasury of Atreus, father of Agamemnon; it is also called the Tomb of Agamemnon. Both names are legend; whose tomb it was is unknown, and it was robbed long ago.

A passage (*dromos*) 36 m long, lined with fine masonry, leads to a doorway more than 5 m high. Above it, a triangular opening relieves the weight on the lintel — the same device used over the Lion Gate. The inner lintel is a single block estimated at about 120 tonnes. The façade was once decorated with columns and carved stone panels of green and red stone, some now in the British Museum.

Inside, a round chamber is roofed by a corbelled dome, built of 33 rings of stone blocks, each ring projecting slightly inward over the one below, about 13.5 m high and 14.5 m across. It was the largest dome in the world until Roman times. A small side chamber cut into the rock probably held the burials.

The Treasury of Atreus and the other tholos tombs at Mycenae show the immense resources Mycenaean rulers could command at the height of their power.
