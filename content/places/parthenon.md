<!-- field: body -->
The Parthenon is the temple of Athena Parthenos ("the Virgin") on the Acropolis of Athens. It was built between 447 and 432 BC as the centrepiece of Pericles' rebuilding programme, on the site of an unfinished temple burned by the Persians in 480 BC. Its architects were Iktinos and Kallikrates, and the sculptor Pheidias directed its decoration. It was paid for largely from the tribute of Athens' allies.

It is a Doric temple of white Pentelic marble, with 8 columns across the ends and 17 along the sides, about 69.5 by 30.9 m. Inside stood Pheidias' statue of Athena, about 12 m tall, of gold and ivory over a wooden core; it is lost. The building is famous for its refinements: the platform curves upward, the columns lean slightly inward and swell along their height, and almost no line is truly straight.

Its sculptures were the most ambitious of any Greek temple: 92 metopes showing mythical battles, a continuous frieze 160 m long of a great procession, and pediments showing the birth of Athena and her contest with Poseidon for Athens. All were brightly painted.

The Parthenon became a church, then a mosque. In 1687, during a Venetian siege, gunpowder stored inside exploded, destroying its centre. Between 1801 and 1812 Lord Elgin removed many sculptures, now in the British Museum, whose return Greece seeks. The rest are in the Acropolis Museum.
