<!-- field: summary -->
The rocky citadel at the heart of Athens, crowned by the Parthenon and the other temples of Athena built under Pericles.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most famous ancient site in the world and the symbol of Classical Greek civilisation and of Athenian democracy at its height.

<!-- field: body -->
The Acropolis is a flat-topped limestone hill rising about 70 m above Athens. In the Bronze Age it held a Mycenaean palace, defended by massive walls built in the 13th century BC. Later it became the city's main sanctuary, dedicated above all to Athena, its patron goddess, though it remained a fortress too.

In the Archaic period wealthy Athenians filled it with temples and statues, including the marble korai. When the Persians took Athens in 480 BC they burned the Acropolis. The Athenians buried the damaged statues in the rubble — which is why so many survived — and for a generation left the ruined temples as a memorial.

From 447 BC Pericles rebuilt it on a grand scale, paid for largely by tribute from the Delian League. The Parthenon (447–432 BC) housed Pheidias' gold-and-ivory statue of Athena; the Propylaia (437–432 BC) formed a monumental gateway; the small temple of Athena Nike stood on the bastion beside it; and the Erechtheion (421–406 BC), with its porch of Caryatids, housed the oldest and holiest cults. The great Panathenaic procession climbed to the Acropolis every four years.

The buildings later served as churches, a mosque and an Ottoman fortress. In 1687 a Venetian shell ignited gunpowder stored in the Parthenon, blowing out its centre, and between 1801 and 1812 Lord Elgin removed many sculptures. Restoration has continued since 1975, and the site has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987.
