<!-- field: summary -->
The sanctuary of Apollo on Parnassus, seat of the most influential oracle in the Greek world, and its self-declared navel.

<!-- field: significance -->
For a thousand years no Greek state founded a colony, changed a law or started a war without consulting Delphi.

<!-- field: body -->
The Pythia, a local woman serving as Apollo's medium, gave responses from a chamber in the temple, and priests rendered them into verse. The answers were famously ambiguous: Croesus was told he would destroy a great empire; he destroyed his own.

Ancient sources describe vapours rising from a chasm beneath the temple. Nineteenth-century excavators found no chasm and dismissed it. In the 1990s a geological team identified two intersecting faults directly beneath the temple and detected ethylene — a sweet-smelling gas producing euphoria and trance — in the spring water and travertine. The mechanism is contested but the geology is real.

Greek states built treasuries here to display their wealth; the Athenian Treasury, rebuilt from its own blocks, still stands. The Sacred Wars fought over control of the sanctuary gave Philip II his opening into central Greek politics. Theodosius closed it in AD 393.
