<!-- field: summary -->
The greatest Greek city of the west, which destroyed an Athenian invasion force and was later defended by Archimedes.

<!-- field: significance -->
Demonstrated that the Greek world's centre of gravity was not confined to the Aegean.

<!-- field: body -->
Syracuse lies on the east coast of Sicily, around a fine double harbour and the small island of Ortygia, with its freshwater spring of Arethusa. Settlers from Corinth founded it in 734 BC, and it grew into the largest and richest Greek city in the west. Cicero, centuries later, still called it the greatest and most beautiful of Greek cities.

It was often ruled by tyrants. Gelon and Hieron I made it a great power in the early 5th century BC, defeating the Carthaginians at Himera in 480 BC and the Etruscans at sea at Cumae in 474 BC; poets such as Pindar and Aeschylus came to their court. In 415–413 BC Athens sent a huge expedition to conquer Syracuse. It ended in disaster: the Athenian fleet was destroyed in the Great Harbour, and the captured survivors were left to die in the city's stone quarries. Thucydides judged it the greatest event of the war.

Under Dionysius I (405–367 BC) Syracuse ruled much of Sicily and southern Italy and built the vast Euryalus fortress; his engineers are credited with an early catapult. Plato visited the court of his son, Dionysius II, in a failed attempt to make him a philosopher-king. In the 3rd century BC King Hieron II kept the city prosperous as an ally of Rome.

When Syracuse sided with Carthage in the Second Punic War, Rome besieged it in 213–212 BC. The mathematician Archimedes designed machines that held the Roman fleet at bay, but the city fell to a night attack during a festival, and Archimedes was killed in the sack — by tradition while drawing geometric figures in the sand. Syracuse then became the capital of the Roman province of Sicily.
