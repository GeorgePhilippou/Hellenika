<!-- field: summary -->
Alexander's greatest foundation: capital of Ptolemaic Egypt, home of the Library, the Museum and the Pharos lighthouse.

<!-- field: significance -->
The intellectual capital of the ancient Mediterranean for three centuries, where most Greek science was done.

<!-- field: body -->
Alexander the Great founded Alexandria in 331 BC on the Mediterranean coast of Egypt, at the western edge of the Nile Delta, on a strip of land between the sea and Lake Mareotis with two good harbours. Ptolemy I and his son Ptolemy II built it up and made it the capital of Ptolemaic Egypt in place of Memphis. Its grid of streets, laid out by the architect Dinocrates, caught the cooling sea breeze.

The Ptolemies made the city the intellectual centre of the Greek world. The Museum — a "shrine of the Muses" and the origin of the word — was in effect a royal research institute that paid scholars to live and work there. Euclid wrote his *Elements* in the city, Eratosthenes measured the circumference of the Earth, Herophilus dissected human bodies, and poets such as Callimachus and Apollonius worked at court. The attached Library tried to collect every Greek book; ships in the harbour reportedly had their scrolls seized and copied. Estimates of its holdings, from 40,000 to 700,000 scrolls, are guesses.

The city's people were mixed: Macedonians and Greeks, native Egyptians, and a large Jewish community that produced the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible. The Pharos, a lighthouse over 100 m tall on the island at the harbour mouth, was one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world and stood until earthquakes destroyed it in the 14th century AD.

Alexandria was the last Ptolemaic capital. Cleopatra VII ruled from it, and Octavian took it in 30 BC, ending Egyptian independence. The Library was not destroyed in one great fire, as legend says: a fire during Caesar's war there in 48 BC damaged some holdings, and it declined over centuries as royal patronage ended. Under Rome the city remained the second city of the empire and a centre of learning.
