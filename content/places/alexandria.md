<!-- field: summary -->
Alexander's greatest foundation: capital of Ptolemaic Egypt, home of the Library, the Museum and the Pharos lighthouse.

<!-- field: significance -->
The intellectual capital of the ancient Mediterranean for three centuries, where most Greek science was done.

<!-- field: body -->
Alexander chose the site himself in 331 BC, reportedly picking a strip of land between the sea and Lake Mareotis specifically for its two harbours and reliable fresh water, then left before construction had properly begun; it was Ptolemy I and his son who actually built the city and made it Egypt's capital in place of the ancient inland city of Memphis. Its grid plan, laid out by the architect Dinocrates, was deliberately oriented to catch the prevailing sea breeze off the Mediterranean, a detail of urban planning meant to make the city liveable in Egyptian heat in a way few older Mediterranean cities had ever been designed for.

The Museum — literally a "shrine of the Muses," the origin of the modern word museum despite functioning much more like a state-funded research institute — salaried resident scholars, housed and fed them at communal expense, and left them largely free to pursue their own research rather than teach or perform administrative duties. Euclid systematised geometry there, Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference, Aristarchus proposed a heliocentric model centuries before Copernicus, Herophilus pioneered human dissection, and Apollonius of Perga wrote the definitive ancient treatment of conic sections — a concentration of theoretical achievement no other single institution in the ancient world came close to matching. Its attached Library pursued acquisition as aggressively as the Museum pursued research: ships docking in Alexandria's harbour were reportedly required to surrender any scrolls on board to be copied, with the ship's owner receiving only the copy back while the original stayed in the Library's own collection, and Ptolemaic agents are said to have gone still further, borrowing official Athenian master copies of the great tragedians against an enormous security deposit and then simply forfeiting the deposit and keeping the originals. Modern estimates of the Library's total holdings range anywhere from 40,000 to as many as 700,000 scrolls, and every figure in that range is ultimately an informed guess rather than a documented count.

The city's population was never simply Greek. Distinct and substantial Greek, native Egyptian and Jewish communities lived alongside each other, each with its own quarter, and the city's Jewish community was large and Greek-speaking enough that it produced the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible undertaken specifically because most Alexandrian Jews could no longer read Hebrew fluently — one of Alexandria's most consequential intellectual products, and one owing nothing to the Museum's Greek scholars at all.

The Pharos lighthouse, over 100 metres tall and counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, guided ships into the harbour for some seventeen centuries until a sequence of earthquakes finally brought it down in the fourteenth century AD; underwater archaeologists have been recovering its fallen masonry, along with sphinxes and obelisks, from the harbour floor since systematic diving surveys began there in 1994. The Library's own end was far less dramatic than its popular reputation suggests: rather than vanishing in a single catastrophic fire, as later legend generally has it, it declined gradually across several centuries through a harbour fire during Julius Caesar's occupation in 48 BC that damaged some holdings, the slow withdrawal of Ptolemaic and then Roman royal patronage, and the eventual departure or expulsion of the scholars who had once been paid to stay — attrition rather than a single catastrophe, though a far less satisfying story than the one that survives in popular memory.
