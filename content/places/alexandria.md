<!-- field: summary -->
Alexander's greatest foundation: capital of Ptolemaic Egypt, home of the Library, the Museum and the Pharos lighthouse.

<!-- field: significance -->
The intellectual capital of the ancient Mediterranean for three centuries, where most Greek science was done.

<!-- field: body -->
Alexander chose the site himself in 331 BC, reportedly picking a strip of land between the sea and Lake Mareotis for its two harbours and reliable fresh water, and left before construction had properly begun. Ptolemy I and his son actually built the city and made it Egypt's capital in place of the ancient inland city of Memphis. Its grid plan, laid out by the architect Dinocrates, was oriented to catch the sea breeze off the Mediterranean, a detail meant to make the city liveable in Egyptian heat in a way few older Mediterranean cities had been designed for.

The Museum, literally a "shrine of the Muses" and the origin of the modern word, functioned much more like a state-funded research institute. It salaried resident scholars, housed and fed them at communal expense, and left them largely free to pursue their own research without teaching or administrative duties. Euclid systematised geometry there, Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference, and Herophilus pioneered human dissection. Apollonius of Perga wrote the definitive ancient treatment of conic sections, and Aristarchus, whose ties to the Museum are less secure, proposed a heliocentric model centuries before Copernicus. No other single institution in the ancient world came close to this concentration of theoretical achievement.

The attached Library pursued acquisition as aggressively as the Museum pursued research. Ships docking in the harbour were reportedly required to surrender any scrolls on board to be copied; the owner received the copy, and the original stayed in the Library. Ptolemaic agents are said to have gone further, borrowing official Athenian master copies of the great tragedians against an enormous deposit and then forfeiting the deposit to keep the originals. Modern estimates of the Library's holdings range from 40,000 to as many as 700,000 scrolls, and every figure is an informed guess, not a documented count.

The city's population was never simply Greek. Substantial Greek, native Egyptian and Jewish communities lived alongside each other, each with its own quarter. The Jewish community was large and Greek-speaking enough to produce the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made because most Alexandrian Jews could no longer read Hebrew fluently. It was one of Alexandria's most consequential intellectual products, and owed nothing to the Museum's scholars.

The Pharos lighthouse, over 100 metres tall and counted among the Seven Wonders, guided ships into the harbour for some sixteen centuries, until a series of earthquakes brought it down in the fourteenth century AD. Underwater archaeologists have been recovering its fallen masonry, along with sphinxes and obelisks, from the harbour floor since systematic diving surveys began in 1994.

The Library's end was far less dramatic than its reputation suggests. It did not vanish in a single catastrophic fire, as legend has it. It declined gradually over several centuries: a harbour fire during Julius Caesar's occupation in 48 BC damaged some holdings, royal patronage withdrew under the late Ptolemies and then Rome, and the scholars who had once been paid to stay eventually left or were expelled. It was attrition rather than catastrophe, a far less satisfying story than the one in popular memory.
