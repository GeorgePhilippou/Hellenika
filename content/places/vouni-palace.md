<!-- field: summary -->
A hilltop palace built, it seems, to keep watch on a rebellious neighbour -- and a showcase for the finest Achaemenid-period goldwork found on Cyprus.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its mixed Persian and Greek material culture is some of the clearest physical evidence for how Cypriot elites navigated Achaemenid overlordship.

<!-- field: body -->
Vouni occupies a defensible hill on Cyprus's northwest coast, close enough to Soloi to have overlooked it directly. The palace's construction is generally connected to the aftermath of the Ionian Revolt-era Cypriot uprising against Persian rule around 497 BC, in which Soloi held out longer than any other Cypriot city under siege -- the likeliest explanation for why a pro-Persian ruler, possibly installed at nearby Marion, would build a fortified residence positioned to watch it.

Excavated by the Swedish Cyprus Expedition, the palace went through several building phases across the fifth and fourth centuries BC before its abandonment or destruction around 380 BC. Its most celebrated find, the so-called Vouni Treasure, includes gold bracelets ranked among the finest examples of Achaemenid-period goldwork found anywhere, alongside silver vessels. The wider pottery assemblage mixes Persian and Greek forms and styles, physical evidence of a Cypriot elite operating between two cultural and political worlds rather than belonging cleanly to either.
