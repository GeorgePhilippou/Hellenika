<!-- field: summary -->
The easternmost Minoan palace, a trade gateway to Egypt and the Levant, destroyed suddenly and never looted.

<!-- field: significance -->
Because it was abandoned in the destruction and never reoccupied, its treasury was found intact — unique among Minoan palaces.

<!-- field: body -->
Zakros faces east towards Egypt and the Levant, and its finds show it: elephant tusks, copper ingots from Cyprus, and Egyptian stone vessels. Its harbour and position at Crete's eastern end made exchange across the eastern Mediterranean central to the settlement rather than incidental.

Nikolaos Platon excavated it from 1961. The destruction c. 1450 BC was sudden and accompanied by fire, and the site was never resettled or robbed — so ritual vessels of rock crystal, obsidian and veined stone were still in place in the treasury. The rock crystal rhyton, reassembled from more than 300 fragments, is among the finest surviving Minoan objects.

Unlike Knossos or Phaistos, Zakros lacks the extensive grain-storage magazines that anchored those palaces' role as agricultural redistribution centres for their surrounding territories, which has led excavators to argue that Zakros functioned primarily as a commercial gateway managing seaborne exchange rather than as a hub collecting and redistributing local farm produce.
