<!-- field: summary -->
A Graeco-Roman town in Middle Egypt whose rubbish dumps yielded the largest find of Greek papyri ever made.

<!-- field: significance -->
The richest single source of lost Greek literature and of everyday life in Roman Egypt.

<!-- field: body -->
Oxyrhynchus, "town of the sharp-nosed fish", named after a fish sacred to the local Egyptians, lay on a canal west of the Nile about 160 kilometres south of Cairo, at modern el-Bahnasa. Under the Ptolemies and the Romans it was a prosperous provincial capital with Greek-speaking officials, a theatre for some 11,000 spectators, temples, and later churches and monasteries.

For a thousand years its people threw old papers — tax returns, letters, school exercises, worn-out books — onto rubbish mounds at the edge of the desert, where the dry sand preserved them. In 1896 two young Oxford scholars, Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt, began digging the mounds, and over eleven seasons recovered hundreds of thousands of fragments. They began publishing them in 1898 in *The Oxyrhynchus Papyri*, a series that has passed eighty volumes and is still continuing, with much still unread.

Most of the texts are documents of daily life, but the literary finds are extraordinary: poems of Sappho, Alcaeus and Pindar, plays of Menander and Sophocles' satyr play *The Trackers*, lost histories, and early Christian texts including sayings of Jesus later identified as part of the Gospel of Thomas. New techniques such as multispectral imaging are revealing more.
