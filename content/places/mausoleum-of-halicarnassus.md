<!-- field: summary -->
The monumental tomb of Mausolus, ruler of Caria, built in the 350s BC — one of the Seven Wonders and the origin of the word "mausoleum".

<!-- field: significance -->
A meeting of Greek and Persian-world art, decorated by the leading Greek sculptors of the 4th century BC, which gave its name to every later mausoleum.

<!-- field: body -->
Mausolus ruled Caria, in south-western Asia Minor, as a satrap of the Persian king, but in practice as an independent prince. He made Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum) his capital and began a vast tomb for himself there. After his death in 353 BC his widow and sister, Artemisia II, continued the work.

The Mausoleum stood about 45 m tall. A high rectangular base supported a colonnade of Ionic columns, topped by a stepped pyramid roof crowned with a marble four-horse chariot. Its architects were Pytheos and Satyros, and according to Pliny four of the most famous Greek sculptors — Scopas, Bryaxis, Timotheus and Leochares — each carved one side. Friezes showed battles of Greeks and Amazons and of Lapiths and centaurs, and there were many free-standing statues. It mixed Greek, Egyptian and Near Eastern elements and was counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

It stood for many centuries until damaged by earthquakes in the Middle Ages. The Knights of St John then used its stones to build their castle at Bodrum, finishing by 1494. In 1856–57 the British archaeologist Charles Newton excavated the site and took sculptures, including the great statues long identified as Mausolus and Artemisia, to the British Museum.

The name of Mausolus became the general word for a grand tomb.
