<!-- field: summary -->
The fortified harbour town of Athens, joined to the city by the Long Walls — the base of Athenian sea power.

<!-- field: significance -->
Piraeus and the Long Walls made Athens effectively an island, able to survive any siege by land as long as it ruled the sea.

<!-- field: body -->
Piraeus is a rocky peninsula about 7 km south-west of Athens with three natural harbours: Kantharos, the main commercial port, and Zea and Mounychia, used for warships. Around 493 BC Themistocles began to fortify it as Athens' port in place of the open beach at Phaleron. In the 5th century BC it was laid out as a planned town on a grid of streets, a design credited to Hippodamus of Miletus. It held the shipsheds that housed hundreds of triremes, the grain market and a cosmopolitan population of traders, resident foreigners and sailors.

In the mid-5th century BC the Athenians built the Long Walls, two parallel walls some 6 km long linking Athens to Piraeus, with a third wall to Phaleron. They turned the city and its harbour into a single fortress. In the Peloponnesian War, Pericles relied on them: the people of Attica moved inside, and Athens was supplied by sea while Spartan armies ravaged the countryside. The crowding also helped spread the plague of 430 BC, which entered through the port.

When Athens surrendered in 404 BC, Sparta forced it to pull down the Long Walls to the music of flute-girls. The Athenian general Conon rebuilt them in the 390s with Persian money. Sulla destroyed the port's fortifications in 86 BC. Piraeus remains Greece's largest port.
