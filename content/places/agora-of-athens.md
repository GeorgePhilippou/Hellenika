<!-- field: summary -->
The great open square of ancient Athens, where citizens met to do business, run the democracy, worship and talk.

<!-- field: significance -->
The everyday heart of Athenian democracy — and the best-excavated civic centre of any Greek city.

<!-- field: body -->
The Agora was the main public square of ancient Athens, an open space north-west of the Acropolis laid out by the 6th century BC. It was surrounded and filled over the centuries by public buildings: the council house (*bouleuterion*) for the Council of 500; the round *tholos* where its standing committee ate and slept; law courts; offices of the magistrates; temples, such as the well-preserved Temple of Hephaestus on the hill above; and long colonnaded halls (*stoas*) such as the Painted Stoa, where Zeno taught and gave the Stoics their name. The Panathenaic Way, the route of the great procession, crossed it to the Acropolis.

It was also a marketplace, full of stalls, workshops and moneychangers, and a place to meet and argue. Socrates spent his days questioning people there.

The American School of Classical Studies at Athens has excavated the Agora since 1931, clearing a whole modern neighbourhood. The finds give a vivid picture of how the democracy worked: ostraka from ostracism votes, a *klērotērion* (allotment machine) for picking jurors at random, bronze juror's tickets, official weights and measures, and a water clock for timing speeches in court. In 1953–56 the Hellenistic Stoa of Attalos, a gift of King Attalos II of Pergamon, was rebuilt as the Agora Museum.
