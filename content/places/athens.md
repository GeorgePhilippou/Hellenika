<!-- field: summary -->
The city of democracy, tragedy, philosophy and the Parthenon — and of an empire funded by allied tribute and sustained by slavery.

<!-- field: significance -->
No other ancient city produced so much that shaped later thought, and none has been so thoroughly mythologised in the process.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens occupies a plain in Attica with a defensible acropolis at its centre and access to the sea a few kilometres away at Piraeus, which would eventually grow into one of the best-fortified and busiest harbours in the Aegean. Mycenaean fortification walls survive on the Acropolis itself, and unlike almost every other Bronze Age palace centre, Athens was not destroyed in the general collapse around 1200 BC — a continuity of occupation that later Athenians turned into a point of civic pride, claiming to be *autochthonous*, sprung directly from the soil of Attica rather than having migrated in from anywhere else, unlike Dorian Sparta or the various colonies that traced their founding back to a specific expedition.

The road to democracy was neither quick nor smooth. By the early sixth century BC, deep debt among poorer Athenian farmers had produced a real crisis: citizens were being sold into slavery for defaulting on loans, and the city was close to open civil conflict between the wealthy few and an indebted majority. Solon, given emergency powers as archon in 594 BC, cancelled existing debts, abolished debt slavery for Athenian citizens outright, and reorganised political rights around four property classes rather than birth alone — a reform that stopped short of full democracy but broke the automatic link between aristocratic descent and political power. Decades of instability followed regardless, and the tyrant Peisistratos eventually seized control of the city, ruling on and off from the 560s to his death in 527 BC. Tyranny in the Greek sense did not necessarily mean cruelty: Peisistratos used revenue from Athens' state-owned silver mines at Laurion to fund public building and patronise festivals, leaving the city's institutions largely intact for his sons to inherit — until the Athenians expelled the last of them in 510 BC.

It was Cleisthenes, in 508 BC, who built the system usually meant by "Athenian democracy." He reorganised the citizen body into ten new tribes deliberately cutting across old regional and family loyalties, created a Council of 500 chosen by lot to prepare business for the citizen Assembly, and introduced ostracism, an annual vote in which the Assembly could exile any citizen, without formal charge, for ten years — a safety valve against any individual accumulating too much personal power. Two decades later, the same silver mines that had financed Peisistratos financed something more consequential: on Themistocles' proposal, a windfall strike at Laurion was used to build a fleet of roughly two hundred triremes rather than being distributed to citizens as a cash bonus, and that fleet went on to win the decisive naval battle of the Persian invasion at Salamis in 480 BC, the same year the Persians burned the Acropolis to the ground in reprisal for Athens' part in the Ionian Revolt.

The fifth century that followed is the one everybody remembers. Pericles rebuilt the burned Acropolis on a scale meant to be unmistakable: the Parthenon, the Propylaea gateway, the Erechtheion and the small temple of Athena Nike, largely funded by tribute extracted from the Delian League, the alliance Athens had turned from a voluntary defensive pact against Persia into something much closer to an empire of subject allied states. Down in the Agora below the hill, the actual daily mechanics of the democracy played out: the Assembly met, juries of ordinary citizens tried cases by the hundreds, and ostracism votes were cast on broken pieces of pottery called ostraka, thousands of which have been recovered by excavation, several bearing the same names — Themistocles among them — scratched by different hands, suggesting organised political campaigning rather than spontaneous individual choice.

None of this extended to everyone living in Attica. Athenian citizenship, and the political rights that came with it, belonged only to free adult men born of two citizen parents; women were excluded from formal politics regardless of status; something like 40,000 metics, free non-citizen residents who paid taxes and could be conscripted but could not vote or own land, lived and worked in the city; and a substantial enslaved population, plausibly somewhere in the range of 60,000 to 80,000 people across Attica though the exact figure remains genuinely disputed among historians, did much of the labour, including in the Laurion mines whose silver had built the navy and the Parthenon alike. Both halves of that picture are true at once: the most participatory system of government the ancient world produced, resting on the unfree and disenfranchised labour of a majority of the people actually living under it.

Athens' fifth-century ascendancy did not last. War with Sparta and its allies, fought intermittently from 431 BC, ended in Athenian surrender in 404 BC, followed by a brief and violent oligarchic coup known as the Thirty Tyrants before democracy was restored within a year. The city executed Socrates in 399 BC on charges of impiety and corrupting the young, a verdict later generations have generally treated as democracy convicting its own most searching critic. Political independence effectively ended at the battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC, when Philip II of Macedon defeated a coalition of Greek states including Athens, though the city's cultural prestige if anything increased under Macedonian and then Roman rule, as Plato's Academy, Aristotle's Lyceum, and later the Stoic and Epicurean schools kept Athens the most important centre of philosophical teaching in the Mediterranean world. That prestige did not protect the city physically: the Roman general Sulla besieged and sacked Athens in 86 BC during the Mithridatic Wars, and his forces felled the sacred cypress groves surrounding the Academy and Lyceum for siege engines and fortification timber, a scar on the philosophical schools' physical setting even as their teaching continued.

Athens' long afterlife as what the text above calls a revered university town lasted for centuries under Roman rule and beyond, with a specifically Neoplatonist revival of the Academy re-established around AD 410. That final incarnation of continuous, organised philosophical teaching in the city, stretching back in an unbroken line to Plato himself roughly eight and a half centuries earlier, was brought to an end in AD 529, when the Christian emperor Justinian ordered the pagan philosophical schools of Athens closed as part of a broader campaign against non-Christian religious and intellectual institutions across the empire — a single administrative decision that is often treated, fairly or not, as a symbolic closing bracket on the classical world Athens had done more than any other city to create.
