<!-- field: summary -->
The city of democracy, tragedy, philosophy and the Parthenon — and of an empire funded by allied tribute and sustained by slavery.

<!-- field: significance -->
No other ancient city produced so much that shaped later thought, and none has been so thoroughly mythologised in the process.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens occupies a plain with a defensible acropolis and access to the sea at Piraeus. Mycenaean walls survive on the Acropolis; unlike most palace centres it was not destroyed around 1200 BC, and Athenians claimed to be *autochthonous* — sprung from the soil, never having migrated.

The fifth century is the famous one. After Persia burned the Acropolis in 480 BC, Pericles rebuilt it: the Parthenon, Propylaea, Erechtheion and Athena Nike temple, largely funded by Delian League tribute. In the Agora below, citizens voted, sued, argued and were ostracised — thousands of the pottery sherds used as ballots have been excavated.

The democracy excluded women, roughly 40,000 metics and perhaps 60,000–80,000 enslaved people. Both facts are true simultaneously: the most participatory government in the ancient world, resting on an unfree majority.

Athens lost the Peloponnesian War in 404 BC, executed Socrates in 399, fell to Macedon in 338, and was sacked by Sulla in 86 BC — after which it survived as a revered university town.
