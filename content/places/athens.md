<!-- field: summary -->
The city of democracy, tragedy, philosophy and the Parthenon — and of an empire funded by allied tribute and sustained by slavery.

<!-- field: significance -->
No other ancient city produced so much that shaped later thought, and none has been so thoroughly mythologised in the process.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens occupies a plain in Attica with a defensible acropolis at its centre and, a few kilometres away, access to the sea at Piraeus, which grew into one of the best-fortified and busiest harbours in the Aegean. Mycenaean fortification walls survive on the Acropolis. Unlike almost every other Bronze Age palace centre, Athens was not destroyed in the general collapse around 1200 BC. Later Athenians turned that continuity into a point of civic pride. They claimed to be *autochthonous*, sprung from the soil of Attica itself instead of having migrated in, unlike Dorian Sparta or the colonies that traced their founding to a specific expedition.

The road to democracy was neither quick nor smooth. By the early sixth century BC, deep debt among poorer farmers had produced a real crisis. Citizens were being sold into slavery for defaulting on loans, and the city was close to civil conflict between the wealthy few and an indebted majority. Solon, given emergency powers as archon in 594 BC, cancelled existing debts, abolished debt slavery for Athenian citizens, and reorganised political rights around four property classes instead of birth alone. It stopped short of democracy, but it broke the automatic link between aristocratic descent and political power.

Instability followed regardless, and the tyrant Peisistratos eventually seized control, ruling on and off from the 560s until his death in 527 BC. Tyranny in the Greek sense did not necessarily mean cruelty. Peisistratos funded public building and patronised festivals, and left the city's institutions largely intact for his sons to inherit, until the Athenians expelled the last of them in 510 BC.

It was Cleisthenes, in 508 BC, who built the system usually meant by "Athenian democracy". He reorganised the citizen body into ten new tribes, deliberately cutting across old regional and family loyalties. He created a Council of 500, chosen by lot, to prepare business for the Assembly. And he introduced ostracism, an annual vote in which the Assembly could exile any citizen for ten years without formal charge, as a safety valve against any one person accumulating too much power.

Two decades later the silver mines at Laurion financed something more consequential. On Themistocles' proposal, a windfall strike was spent on a fleet of roughly two hundred triremes instead of being distributed to citizens as a cash bonus. That fleet won the decisive naval battle of the Persian invasion at Salamis in 480 BC, the same year the Persians burned the Acropolis, in reprisal for Athens' part in the Ionian Revolt.

The fifth century that followed is the one everybody remembers. Pericles rebuilt the Acropolis on a scale meant to be unmistakable: the Parthenon, the Propylaea gateway, the Erechtheion and the small temple of Athena Nike. It was largely funded by tribute from the Delian League, an alliance Athens had turned from a voluntary pact against Persia into something much closer to an empire of subject states. In the Agora below, the daily mechanics of the democracy played out. The Assembly met, juries of ordinary citizens tried cases by the hundreds, and ostracism votes were cast on broken pieces of pottery, *ostraka*. Thousands have been recovered. Several bear the same names, Themistocles among them, scratched by different hands, which suggests organised campaigning rather than spontaneous individual choice.

None of this extended to everyone living in Attica. Citizenship and its political rights belonged only to free adult men born of two citizen parents. Women were excluded from formal politics regardless of status. Something like 40,000 metics, free non-citizen residents who paid taxes and could be conscripted but could not vote or own land, lived and worked in the city. A substantial enslaved population, plausibly 60,000 to 80,000 across Attica though the figure is genuinely disputed, did much of the labour, including in the Laurion mines whose silver built the fleet. Both halves of the picture are true at once: the most participatory system of government the ancient world produced, resting on the unfree and disenfranchised labour of a majority of the people living under it.

Athens' fifth-century ascendancy did not last. War with Sparta and its allies, fought intermittently from 431 BC, ended in Athenian surrender in 404 BC. A brief and violent oligarchic coup, the Thirty Tyrants, followed before democracy was restored within a year. The city executed Socrates in 399 BC on charges of impiety and corrupting the young, a verdict later generations have generally treated as democracy convicting its most searching critic.

Political independence effectively ended at Chaeronea in 338 BC, when Philip II defeated a coalition of Greek states including Athens. Cultural prestige if anything increased under Macedonian and then Roman rule, as Plato's Academy, Aristotle's Lyceum, and later the Stoic and Epicurean schools kept Athens the leading centre of philosophical teaching in the Mediterranean. That did not protect the city physically. Sulla besieged and sacked Athens in 86 BC during the Mithridatic Wars, and his troops felled the sacred groves around the Academy and Lyceum for siege timber, though teaching continued.

Athens' long afterlife as a revered university town lasted for centuries under Roman rule, with a Neoplatonist revival of the Academy from around AD 410. That last incarnation of organised philosophical teaching in the city, stretching back to Plato some eight and a half centuries earlier, ended in AD 529, when the Christian emperor Justinian ordered the pagan philosophical schools of Athens closed. It is often treated, fairly or not, as a symbolic closing bracket on the classical world that Athens had done more than any other city to create.
