<!-- field: summary -->
The city of democracy, tragedy, philosophy and the Parthenon — and of an empire funded by allied tribute and sustained by slavery.

<!-- field: significance -->
No other ancient city produced so much that shaped later thought, and none has been so thoroughly mythologised in the process.

<!-- field: body -->
Athens lies on a plain in Attica, around the rocky Acropolis, with its harbour at Piraeus a few kilometres away. A Mycenaean citadel stood on the Acropolis, and unlike most Bronze Age centres Athens was not destroyed around 1200 BC; later Athenians boasted they had always lived in Attica. By the 8th century BC it controlled the whole region.

Its road to democracy ran through crisis. In 594 BC Solon cancelled debts, ended debt slavery for citizens and based political rights on wealth rather than birth. The tyrant Peisistratus and his sons ruled from the 560s to 510 BC, building temples and festivals. In 508/7 BC Cleisthenes created the democracy: ten new tribes, a Council of 500 chosen by lot, and ostracism to exile an over-powerful citizen.

In the Persian Wars Athens won at Marathon in 490 BC and, with a fleet paid for by the silver of Laurion, at Salamis in 480 BC, though the Persians burned the Acropolis. Athens then turned the Delian League, an alliance against Persia, into an empire of tribute-paying allies. Under Pericles that wealth paid for the Parthenon and the other Acropolis buildings, while the Assembly, citizen juries and theatre flourished. Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes wrote for its festivals, and Socrates taught in its streets.

The democracy had clear limits. Only free men born of two citizen parents could vote; women, some tens of thousands of resident foreigners (metics) and a large enslaved population, perhaps 60,000–80,000, were excluded, and slaves worked the silver mines.

Defeat by Sparta in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) ended the empire, and a brief oligarchy, the Thirty, followed before democracy returned. Athens executed Socrates in 399 BC. After Philip II's victory at Chaeronea in 338 BC the city lost real independence, but as home of Plato's Academy, Aristotle's Lyceum and the Stoic and Epicurean schools it remained the leading centre of philosophy. Sulla sacked it in 86 BC. Its pagan philosophical schools were finally closed by the emperor Justinian in AD 529.
