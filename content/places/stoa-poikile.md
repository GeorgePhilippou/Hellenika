<!-- field: summary -->
Painted colonnade in the Athenian Agora where Zeno of Citium taught, and from which Stoicism takes its name.

<!-- field: significance -->
The stoa was a civic monument to Athenian memory that gave its name to one of antiquity's most influential schools.

<!-- field: body -->
The Stoa Poikile stood on the north side of the Agora, facing the marketplace. It was built around 460 BC, in the time of Cimon, and was decorated with large paintings on wood panels. Pausanias, who saw them in the second century AD, lists four scenes: the Athenians and Spartans at Oenoe, the Athenians against the Amazons, the aftermath of the fall of Troy, and the battle of Marathon. Polygnotus and Mikon are named as painters.

It was a public building, used for meetings, trials and displays. Captured shields, including some from the Spartans at Sphacteria, were hung in it. Vendors, jugglers and beggars gathered under the roof. Around 300 BC, Zeno of Citium, a Phoenician from Cyprus, began to teach there. His hearers were first called Zenonians, and then Stoics, from the place.

For more than a century the stoa's foundations were not identified. Excavations by the American School in 1981 located part of the building, and confirmed the outline of a long colonnade with an inner wall that could hold panels. The paintings themselves are lost, and the stoa seems to have been dismantled in late antiquity.

That a Stoic philosophy came out of a building of Athenian public memory reflects the kind of school it was. Stoicism taught that a person is part of a rational community, and its meeting-place was a public space, not a private garden or an enclosed grove.
