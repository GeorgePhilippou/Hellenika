<!-- field: summary -->
The vast Ionic temple of Artemis at Ephesus, one of the largest Greek temples ever built and one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

<!-- field: significance -->
A Wonder of the ancient world and a meeting point of Greek and Anatolian religion; its foundation deposit preserves the earliest dated coins.

<!-- field: body -->
The sanctuary of Artemis at Ephesus, on the coast of Ionia in western Asia Minor, was among the most important in the Greek world. Its goddess, Artemis of Ephesus, combined the Greek huntress with an older Anatolian mother goddess.

Around 560 BC the Ephesians began a huge marble temple in the Ionic order, about 115 by 55 m, surrounded by a double row of columns, more than a hundred in all. King Croesus of Lydia paid for many of its columns. It was one of the first great marble temples and one of the largest Greek temples ever built.

In 356 BC a man named Herostratus burned it down to make his name famous; the Ephesians decreed his name should never be spoken. Later tradition said the fire happened on the night Alexander the Great was born. The Ephesians rebuilt it on the same plan, refusing Alexander's offer to pay for it, and the new temple was listed among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

It was damaged by Gothic raiders in the AD 260s and its stone was later reused. Its site, buried by silt, was found in 1869 by John Turtle Wood. Beneath the temple, David Hogarth in 1904–05 found a foundation deposit of electrum coins, among the earliest known. Today a single re-erected column marks the spot.
