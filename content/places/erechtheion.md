<!-- field: summary -->
An irregular Ionic temple on the Acropolis, housing Athens' holiest cults and famous for its porch of Caryatids.

<!-- field: significance -->
The religious heart of the Acropolis, and the source of the Caryatids, among the most copied figures in architecture.

<!-- field: body -->
The Erechtheion stands on the north side of the Acropolis, opposite the Parthenon. It was built between about 421 and 406 BC, partly during the Peloponnesian War, and housed the oldest and most sacred cults of Athens: above all the ancient olive-wood statue of Athena Polias, guardian of the city, which received the new robe at the Panathenaia. It also honoured Poseidon and the legendary king Erechtheus, after whom it is named.

Unlike the Parthenon, it is irregular in plan, built across uneven ground and around sacred spots: the marks left by Poseidon's trident when he struck the rock, and Athena's olive tree, her gift that won her the city in the contest with Poseidon. It is in the Ionic order, with finely carved decoration, and has porches on three sides.

The most famous is the south porch, whose roof is held up by six statues of young women, the Caryatids, instead of columns. One was removed by Lord Elgin and is in the British Museum; the others were replaced by copies in the 1970s and are now in the Acropolis Museum.

Inscriptions record the building accounts in detail, listing the wages of the citizens, resident foreigners and slaves who worked side by side, each paid the same daily rate. The temple was later a church, a palace and, under the Ottomans, a harem.
