<!-- field: summary -->
A major Late Bronze Age copper-trading city a few kilometres from where Salamis later rose, and a strong candidate for the elusive kingdom of Alashiya.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its bronze cult statuettes, Cypro-Minoan tablets and dense ashlar architecture are central evidence for how thoroughly Late Bronze Age Cyprus was integrated into the wider eastern Mediterranean copper trade.

<!-- field: body -->
Enkomi stood a few kilometres inland from Cyprus's east coast, near modern Famagusta, at the head of a lagoon that then gave it direct harbour access. Excavations, principally French and British missions through the twentieth century, uncovered dense ashlar-built architecture, a sanctuary yielding the bronze "Ingot God" and "Horned God" statuettes, and roughly two thousand tombs.

Egyptian and other Near Eastern diplomatic correspondence of the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BC repeatedly mentions a copper-rich land called Alashiya. Enkomi is one of the strongest candidates for its capital, though the identification rests on circumstantial evidence -- the city's scale, its metalworking industry and its trade connections -- rather than a text found on Cyprus itself naming the place.

Tablets in the undeciphered Cypro-Minoan script, including the longest example yet found, show that Enkomi's administration used writing, though what the script actually records remains largely unknown. The city was abandoned around 1050 BC, in the same period of disruption across the eastern Mediterranean associated with the Bronze Age collapse; its population is generally thought to have relocated to nearby Salamis, though whether an earthquake, the silting of its harbour, or some other cause forced the move is not settled.
