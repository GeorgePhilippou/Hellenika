<!-- field: summary -->
A north-coast kingdom whose deep cemetery record stretches back to the Chalcolithic, though its place among Cyprus's "ten kingdoms" is a separate, later tradition rather than the Assyrian tribute list most of the others share.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its necropoleis anchor the pottery sequence archaeologists use to date the transition from Bronze to Iron Age Cyprus.

<!-- field: body -->
Lapithos lies on Cyprus's north coast, in the shadow of the Kyrenia mountain range. The Vrysi tou Barba and Kastros cemeteries, excavated by the Swedish Cyprus Expedition, yielded more than 75 Bronze Age chamber tombs whose Bichrome Red pottery sequence remains a key reference point for dating the wider transition from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age on Cyprus.

Ancient lists of Cyprus's city-kingdoms are not fully consistent with one another. The best-known list, ten kings named on a prism of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon recording tribute paid around 673 BC, does not include Lapithos. It appears instead in a separate, later tradition recorded by the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus, describing nine Cypriot kingdoms in the context of events centuries after Esarhaddon's prism was inscribed. The two traditions are sometimes run together in popular accounts of "the ten kingdoms of Cyprus," but they describe different moments and, in places, different sets of cities.

Like several other independent kingdoms, Lapithos lost its autonomy in 312 BC when Ptolemy I moved against Cypriot rulers who had backed his rival Antigonus in the wars following Alexander's death.
