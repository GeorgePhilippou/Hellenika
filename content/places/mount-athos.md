<!-- field: summary -->
The mountainous peninsula in northern Greece that has been the centre of Orthodox monasticism since the 10th century.

<!-- field: significance -->
A living survival of Byzantium, whose monasteries preserve Byzantine art, liturgy and manuscripts.

<!-- field: body -->
Mount Athos is the easternmost of the three peninsulas of Chalcidice in northern Greece, ending in a marble peak over 2,000 metres high. In antiquity Xerxes cut a canal across its neck in 480 BC to spare his fleet the dangerous voyage round it. Hermits lived there from at least the 9th century.

In 963 the monk Athanasios, with the support of his friend the general and future emperor Nikephoros II Phokas, founded the Great Lavra, the first large organised monastery. Others followed, founded by Byzantine emperors and later by Georgian, Serbian, Russian and Bulgarian rulers, making Athos a centre for the whole Orthodox world. Imperial charters gave it self-government; by tradition, women and even female animals are barred.

Athos flourished in the late Byzantine period, when it was the home of Hesychasm, a movement of contemplative prayer. After 1430 it submitted to the Ottomans and kept its privileges. Today twenty ruling monasteries remain, governing the peninsula as a self-governing region of Greece; it was made a World Heritage Site in 1988. Its monasteries preserve icons, frescoes, treasures and thousands of manuscripts, including copies of classical texts.
