<!-- field: summary -->
A Late Neolithic village near Volos in Thessaly, of about 4800–4500 BC, surrounded by concentric stone walls.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-known Neolithic settlement in Greece, and the type site of the Late Neolithic Dimini culture.

<!-- field: body -->
Dimini stands on a low hill a few kilometres from Sesklo, near the shore of the Pagasetic Gulf at Volos. In 1886 Valerios Stais found a Mycenaean tholos tomb nearby, and from 1901 Christos Tsountas excavated the Neolithic village on the hilltop. Later excavations have continued there.

The village was occupied mainly in the Late Neolithic, around 4800–4500 BC. Its most striking feature is a series of up to six low stone walls running in rough rings around the hill, with narrow passages between them. Early excavators took these for fortifications, but they are too low and too many to be defensive, and are now usually seen as enclosures dividing groups of houses and work areas. At the centre was a courtyard with a larger house, once compared to the Mycenaean megaron. Excavators found hearths, storage pits, and evidence of pottery-making and shell-working.

Dimini gives its name to the Late Neolithic culture of Thessaly, known for pottery decorated with spirals and meanders in dark paint. Long after the village was abandoned, the site was reoccupied in the Mycenaean period, when a settlement and two tholos tombs were built; some scholars identify it with ancient Iolcos.
