<!-- field: summary -->
A volcanic Cycladic island, rich in obsidian, whose conquest by Athens in 416 BC is the subject of the Melian dialogue.

<!-- field: significance -->
The source of the Aegean's obsidian, the findspot of the Venus de Milo, and the scene of one of the most notorious atrocities of the Peloponnesian War.

<!-- field: body -->
Melos is a volcanic island at the south-western end of the Cyclades. Its obsidian, a glassy rock which can be chipped to a razor edge, was traded across the Aegean from the Neolithic period, and the island has been settled since the seventh millennium BC. The Bronze Age town of Phylakopi, on the north coast, shows successive Cycladic, Minoan and Mycenaean phases.

In the Peloponnesian War, Melos stayed neutral, though it was a Spartan colony. In 416 BC Athens sent a force and demanded its submission. Thucydides stages the negotiation as a debate in which the Melians appeal to justice and the Athenians answer that the strong do what they can and the weak accept what they must. The Melians refused, the city fell after a siege, and the Athenians killed the men of military age and enslaved the rest.

In 1820 a farmer unearthed the statue now known as the Venus de Milo near the ancient theatre. The island is also known for its catacombs, which are early Christian burial chambers.
