<!-- field: summary -->
A Doric temple above the Athenian Agora, begun around 449 BC and the best-preserved ancient Greek temple in existence.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-preserved Doric temple surviving from the ancient world, kept intact by centuries of use as a church.

<!-- field: body -->
The Hephaisteion stands on Kolonos Agoraios, the low hill on the west side of the Agora of Athens. It was begun about 449 BC, in the years when Pericles' building programme was reshaping the city, and was completed in the following decades. The temple was dedicated to Hephaestus, god of the forge, and to Athena, and the surrounding district housed metalworkers and potters.

The building is a Doric peripteral temple of six by thirteen columns, in Pentelic marble with sculpted metopes and a frieze. Its design is close in spirit to the Parthenon, and the architect, who is unknown, appears to have worked on other temples in Attica.

It was long called the Theseion, from a mistaken belief that it housed the bones of Theseus. In the seventh century AD it was turned into the church of St George Akamates and remained in use until 1834. This continuous use explains its remarkable state of preservation. The American excavations of the Agora from 1931 cleared its surroundings and established its real dedication.
