<!-- field: summary -->
Plato's school outside Athens, in a sacred grove, which shaped Western thought for nine centuries.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Academy is the model for the university: an organised community for teaching and research that outlived its founder by centuries.

<!-- field: body -->
Around 387 BC Plato bought or rented ground near a public gymnasium about a kilometre and a half north-west of Athens, in a grove dedicated to the hero Akademos. There he taught for some forty years. Unlike the Sophists, he did not charge for lessons, and the school was organised as a community of scholars united by shared inquiry into mathematics, dialectic and politics. Aristotle joined at seventeen and stayed for twenty years.

After Plato's death in 347 his nephew Speusippus took over, followed by Xenocrates. The "Old Academy" concentrated on metaphysics and mathematics. In the third century, under Arcesilaus, the school turned sceptical, defending the view that nothing can be known with certainty. This "New Academy" argued against the Stoics until the first century BC, when Antiochus of Ascalon returned to a more dogmatic Platonism.

The grounds were damaged in 86 BC, when Sulla besieged Athens and cut down the groves for siege engines. Teaching went on, however, in the city. In the fifth century AD a Neoplatonist school led by Plutarch of Athens and later Proclus revived the name and claimed descent from Plato. Justinian's edict of AD 529 forbade pagans to teach, and is traditionally seen as the end of the Academy, though historians debate how far it affected the school and whether teaching continued.

The site has been excavated since the 1930s in what is now a public park. The remains are of a gymnasium, a palaestra and associated buildings, which show that the Academy was a physical place used for exercise and teaching as well as philosophy. No building specifically identified as Plato's school has been found.
