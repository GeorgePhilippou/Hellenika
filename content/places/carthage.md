<!-- field: summary -->
The great Phoenician city of North Africa, rival of the western Greeks and of Rome, destroyed in 146 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The main rival of the Greeks in the western Mediterranean for three centuries, and of Rome in the Punic Wars.

<!-- field: body -->
Carthage stood on a peninsula on the coast of North Africa, near modern Tunis. Phoenicians from Tyre founded it, traditionally in 814 BC; the oldest archaeological remains date to the later 9th or 8th century BC. It grew into the leading Phoenician city of the west, with a powerful navy and a trading network across the western Mediterranean, from Sardinia and Spain to the Atlantic coast.

Its expansion brought it into conflict with the Greek colonies. In the 6th century BC it fought the Phocaeans at sea off Alalia in Corsica. In Sicily, Carthaginians and Greeks fought for control of the island for over two centuries: Gelon of Syracuse defeated a Carthaginian army at Himera in 480 BC, by tradition on the same day as Salamis; Carthage destroyed Selinous and Akragas in 409–406 BC; and wars continued under Dionysius I, Timoleon and Agathocles, who invaded Africa itself in 310 BC.

Carthage was governed by a mercantile aristocracy with elected magistrates, the *suffetes*, and a council; Aristotle praised its constitution. Its gods included Baal Hammon and Tanit.

Its rivalry with Rome led to the three Punic Wars (264–146 BC), including Hannibal's invasion of Italy. At the end of the Third Punic War, in 146 BC, Rome destroyed Carthage, the same year it sacked Corinth.
