<!-- field: summary -->
The great lighthouse at the entrance to Alexandria's harbour, completed around 280 BC — one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

<!-- field: significance -->
The prototype of every lighthouse: its name, pharos, still means lighthouse in many languages, and it guided ships into the greatest port of the Hellenistic world for 1,500 years.

<!-- field: body -->
The Pharos stood on the eastern tip of the island of Pharos, which a causeway linked to the mainland at Alexandria, the capital of Ptolemaic Egypt. It was begun under Ptolemy I and completed around 280 BC under Ptolemy II. Its architect was Sostratus of Cnidus, whose dedication to the "Saviour Gods" was inscribed on it.

Ancient and medieval descriptions agree that it rose in three tiers: a square base, an octagonal middle and a round top, crowned by a statue, probably of Zeus. It stood perhaps 100 m tall, among the tallest structures of the ancient world. A fire burned at the top, and some sources say a mirror reflected its light out to sea. It guided ships along a flat, dangerous coast into one of the busiest ports of the Mediterranean, and was counted among the Seven Wonders. Its name became the word for lighthouse in Greek, Latin and many later languages, such as French *phare* and Italian *faro*.

The Pharos survived for some 1,500 years, repaired many times, until earthquakes in the 14th century AD brought it down. In 1477 the Mamluk sultan Qaitbay built a fort on its site, reusing some of its stone. Since 1994 underwater archaeologists led by Jean-Yves Empereur have found huge blocks, columns and colossal statues of the Ptolemies on the seabed nearby.
