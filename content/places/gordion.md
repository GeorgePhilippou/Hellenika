<!-- field: summary -->
The Phrygian capital in central Anatolia, home of the legendary King Midas and the site of Alexander's encounter with the Gordian knot.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-excavated Phrygian site, whose great tumulus tombs show the wealth of a kingdom known to Greeks mainly through myth.

<!-- field: body -->
Gordion stood on the Sakarya river in the Anatolian plateau, about 100 kilometres from modern Ankara. It was the capital of the Phrygians, whose kings, in Greek tradition named Gordias and Midas, ruled central Anatolia in the eighth and seventh centuries BC. A fortified citadel with monumental gateways, a series of large halls and workshops, and a cluster of burial mounds have been excavated.

The largest mound, Tumulus MM, covers a log chamber built about 740 BC in which a man was buried with a huge collection of bronze bowls, jugs and inlaid wooden tables. The site was damaged by fire in the early period and later rebuilt, and became part of the Lydian kingdom and then of the Persian Empire.

In 333 BC Alexander came to Gordion and was shown the wagon of Gordias, whose yoke was tied with a knot that, according to tradition, would give its undoer control of Asia. Accounts differ on whether he sliced through it with his sword or removed the pin. The University of Pennsylvania has excavated the site since 1950.
