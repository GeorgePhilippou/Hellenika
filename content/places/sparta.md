<!-- field: summary -->
A militarised society of full-time warrior-citizens sustained by an enslaved Messenian population many times their number.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its army was unmatched in Greece for as long as the enslaved Messenians who fed it stayed put — and no ancient society has been more systematically misunderstood.

<!-- field: body -->
Sparta lay in the valley of the Eurotas river in Laconia, in the southern Peloponnese. It grew from four or five villages settled in the 10th century BC, and in the 8th and 7th centuries BC it conquered neighbouring Messenia, turning its people into helots — state-owned serfs who farmed the land for Spartan citizens. By the 6th century Sparta led the Peloponnesian League and was the strongest land power in Greece.

Its constitution, credited to the legendary lawgiver Lycurgus, mixed two hereditary kings, a council of elders over sixty, an assembly of citizens, and five annually elected ephors who could prosecute even a king. Boys entered the *agoge*, a harsh state upbringing, at about seven, lived in barracks until about thirty and ate in common messes for life; a man who could not pay his mess dues lost his citizenship. Helots, who outnumbered citizens perhaps seven to one, did the farming, and Sparta held them down by terror: each year the ephors declared war on them, and young men of the *krypteia* hunted down those thought dangerous. Spartan women, unusually in Greece, owned property and exercised in public.

Sparta led the Greek land forces against Persia, at Thermopylae in 480 BC and Plataea in 479 BC, and with Persian money defeated Athens in the Peloponnesian War in 404 BC. But its citizen body shrank steadily, from perhaps 8,000 men to a few hundred. In 371 BC Thebes under Epaminondas crushed the Spartan army at Leuctra and freed Messenia, removing the helot labour the whole system depended on. Sparta never recovered.

Hellenistic kings such as Agis IV and Cleomenes III tried to restore the old system, without lasting success, and Sparta became a minor city under Rome, where the ritual flogging of boys at the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia was staged as a spectacle for visitors.

Much of the popular image of Sparta is the "Spartan mirage" — an idealised picture created by admiring outsiders such as Xenophon and later writers, rather than by the Spartans, who wrote almost nothing about themselves.
