<!-- field: summary -->
A militarised society of full-time warrior-citizens sustained by an enslaved Messenian population many times their number.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its army was unmatched in Greece for as long as the enslaved Messenians who fed it stayed put — and no ancient society has been more systematically misunderstood.

<!-- field: body -->
Sparta had no walls; Spartans said their men were the walls, and for most of the Classical period this was not empty boasting — Sparta remained the one major Greek city that genuinely never needed fortification, since no enemy army could get near it and survive the encounter. Its constitution, unusually for a Greek state so strongly associated with military discipline, was a mixed and carefully balanced one: two hereditary kings from separate royal houses who checked each other's power, a council of twenty-eight elders (the gerousia) who had to be over sixty to serve, a citizen assembly, and five annually elected ephors with the extraordinary authority to prosecute and even depose a sitting king.

The system that produced Spartan soldiers began early and lasted a lifetime. Male citizens entered the *agoge*, a state-run upbringing built around deliberate hardship, at around age seven, lived in communal barracks rather than with their families until roughly thirty, and even after formal training ended continued eating at communal messes, the *syssitia*, for the rest of their lives — a citizen who could not contribute his required share to his mess lost his citizenship outright. None of this labour of soldiering left time for farming, which was done instead by *helots*, an enslaved population drawn mostly from conquered Messenia who are generally reckoned to have outnumbered full Spartan citizens by something like seven to one. Sparta formalised its fear of that majority into state ritual: the ephors ceremonially declared war on the helots each year specifically so that killing one carried no religious pollution, and the *krypteia*, a body of young Spartan men sent out into the countryside to operate by night, existed largely to identify and kill helots judged capable of leading a revolt — a permanent, low-level campaign of terror aimed at Sparta's own subject population rather than any external enemy. Spartan foreign policy for two centuries was shaped as much by fear of what might happen at home in the army's absence as by any ambition abroad.

Set against this, Spartan women lived with a degree of personal and economic autonomy unmatched anywhere else in the Greek world: they owned and inherited property in their own right, trained and competed athletically in public rather than staying confined indoors, and by Aristotle's own hostile estimate — he thought the whole arrangement had ruined Spartan society — women eventually held control of as much as two-fifths of all Spartan land, largely because so many male heirs died in war or in the agoge and property passed to their sisters, widows and daughters instead.

The system that made Sparta briefly the dominant land power in Greece was also demographically self-destroying. The number of full citizens entitled to call themselves Spartiate fell relentlessly across the Classical period, from perhaps eight thousand adult male citizens in the early fifth century to only a few hundred within two centuries, as war losses, debt, and a citizenship system that stripped rank from anyone who could not pay his mess dues steadily narrowed the pool of men eligible to fight. The collapse became irreversible after the battle of Leuctra in 371 BC, where the Theban general Epaminondas broke the supposedly unbeatable Spartan phalanx in open battle and then marched into the Peloponnese to liberate Messenia outright, stripping Sparta at a stroke of the enslaved agricultural labour its entire social system depended on. Sparta never recovered either militarily or economically, and much of the modern popular image of disciplined, laconic Spartan virtue is now understood by historians as the *Spartan mirage*, a term coined by the French scholar François Ollier in the 1930s for a picture built up largely by admiring outsiders — above all Xenophon, an Athenian who had lived among Spartans and idealised what he saw — writing well after Sparta's real military and demographic decline had already set in, and by later Roman and modern observers who projected their own preoccupations with discipline and simplicity onto a society none of them had actually experienced firsthand.

Sparta's final transformation, under Roman rule, turned the city's own former severity into paying entertainment. The genuinely ancient ritual flogging of young Spartan men at the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, once a real ordeal meant to test endurance as part of the agoge, was restaged for Roman-era tourists as a violent public spectacle, sometimes fatal, watched from a purpose-built viewing structure erected beside the altar specifically to seat paying visitors from across the empire — a city that had spent centuries terrifying the rest of Greece ending its ancient life as a theme-park version of itself.
