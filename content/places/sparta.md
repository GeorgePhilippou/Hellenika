<!-- field: summary -->
A militarised society of full-time warrior-citizens sustained by an enslaved Messenian population many times their number.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its army was unmatched in Greece for as long as the enslaved Messenians who fed it stayed put — and no ancient society has been more systematically misunderstood.

<!-- field: body -->
Sparta had no walls. Spartans said their men were the walls, and for most of the Classical period this was not empty boasting: no enemy army could get near the city and survive the encounter. Its constitution was, unusually for a state so associated with discipline, a mixed and balanced one. There were two hereditary kings from separate royal houses who checked each other's power, a council of twenty-eight elders (the *gerousia*) who had to be over sixty, a citizen assembly, and five annually elected ephors. The ephors had the extraordinary authority to prosecute and even depose a sitting king.

The system that produced Spartan soldiers began early and lasted a lifetime. Male citizens entered the *agoge*, a state-run upbringing built on deliberate hardship, at around seven. They lived in communal barracks, not with their families, until roughly thirty, and afterwards ate in communal messes, the *syssitia*, for the rest of their lives. A citizen who could not pay his share of his mess lost his citizenship outright.

None of this left time for farming, which was done by *helots*, an enslaved population drawn mostly from conquered Messenia. They are generally reckoned to have outnumbered full citizens by something like seven to one. Sparta built its fear of that majority into state ritual. The ephors declared war on the helots each year so that killing one carried no religious pollution. The *krypteia*, a body of young Spartans sent into the countryside at night, existed largely to identify and kill helots judged capable of leading a revolt. This was a permanent low-level campaign of terror aimed at Sparta's own subjects. For two centuries, Spartan foreign policy was shaped as much by fear of what might happen at home in the army's absence as by ambition abroad.

Spartan women, by contrast, enjoyed a degree of personal and economic autonomy unmatched elsewhere in Greece. They owned and inherited property in their own right, and trained and competed athletically in public instead of staying indoors. Aristotle, who thought the arrangement had ruined Spartan society, estimated that women eventually controlled as much as two-fifths of Spartan land. So many male heirs died in war or in the agoge that property passed to sisters, widows and daughters.

The system that made Sparta briefly the dominant land power in Greece was also demographically self-destroying. Full Spartiate citizens fell from perhaps eight thousand adult men in the early fifth century to a few hundred within two centuries. War losses, debt, and a rule stripping rank from anyone who could not pay his mess dues steadily narrowed the pool. The collapse became irreversible after Leuctra in 371 BC, where Epaminondas broke the supposedly unbeatable Spartan phalanx and then marched into the Peloponnese to liberate Messenia. That stripped Sparta at a stroke of the enslaved labour on which its whole social system depended, and it never recovered militarily or economically.

Much of the popular image of disciplined, laconic Sparta is now understood as the *Spartan mirage*, a term coined by the French scholar François Ollier in the 1930s. The picture was built up by admiring outsiders, above all Xenophon, an Athenian who had lived among Spartans and idealised what he saw. They were writing after the real decline had already set in. Later Roman and modern observers, none of whom had experienced Sparta first-hand, projected their own preoccupations with discipline and simplicity onto it.

Under Roman rule, the city's former severity became paying entertainment. The ritual flogging of young Spartans at the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, once a real test of endurance within the agoge, was restaged as a violent public spectacle, sometimes fatal. A purpose-built viewing structure beside the altar seated paying visitors from across the empire. A city that had spent centuries terrifying the rest of Greece ended its ancient life as a theme-park version of itself.
