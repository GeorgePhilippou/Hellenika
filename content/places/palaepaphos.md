<!-- field: summary -->
Old Paphos, a Cypriot city-kingdom and the long-lived sanctuary centre of Aphrodite.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the principal Cypriot kingdoms and the cult centre most closely associated with Aphrodite.

<!-- field: body -->
Palaepaphos occupied the coastal plain around modern Kouklia, close to routes leading inland towards the copper-bearing Troodos foothills. It became the seat of one of Cyprus’s city-kingdoms and the island’s most renowned sanctuary of the goddess whom Greek visitors identified as Aphrodite. Worship at the sanctuary reaches back into the Late Bronze Age, although its rites and divine names changed across that long history.

The cult image was not the familiar nude goddess of later art but an aniconic stone. Kings of Paphos also served as priests, binding political authority to the sanctuary. Inscriptions and coinage show a Greek-speaking kingdom using the Cypriot syllabary, while its material culture remained connected to both the Aegean and the Levant.

Ancient foundation stories credited Agapenor, returning from Troy, with founding the city. They reveal how later communities explained their identity; they do not document a named Bronze Age founder. Near the end of the fourth century BC King Nicocles established Nea Paphos by a better harbour. Administration moved, but the old sanctuary remained powerful under the Ptolemies and Romans, and images of its sacred stone appeared on Roman Cypriot coinage.
