<!-- field: summary -->
An inland Cypriot kingdom built on the copper deposits that gave the island its ancient reputation and, some argue, its name.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its royal tombs are the best-preserved evidence for how a Cypriot city-kingdom's elite represented themselves in death, and its tribute to Assyria is directly documented rather than inferred.

<!-- field: body -->
Tamassos sat in the Troodos foothills southwest of modern Nicosia, directly over one of the richest copper-bearing districts on the island — the same metal that likely gives Cyprus its name in Greek. Excavations since the 1880s have uncovered fortifications, a sanctuary associated with Aphrodite or her Phoenician counterpart Astarte, copper-smelting workshops, and an extensive cemetery.

Two royal tombs from the sixth century BC, cut into rock and built with monumental stone facades, are among the best-preserved examples of pre-Hellenistic Cypriot funerary architecture; both were originally flanked by carved sphinxes and lions, echoes of Near Eastern royal imagery reaching a Cypriot kingdom through trade and diplomatic contact rather than conquest.

Tamassos is one of ten Cypriot kings named on a clay prism of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon, recording tribute paid around 673 BC — a rare case where a Cypriot ruler is named in a contemporary foreign document rather than known only from later Greek tradition or unattributed local inscriptions.
