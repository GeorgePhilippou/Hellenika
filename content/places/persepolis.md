<!-- field: summary -->
The Achaemenid ceremonial capital, built by Darius I and burned by Alexander in 330 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The finest surviving Achaemenid architecture, and its destruction is the symbolic end of the Persian Empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Persepolis lies on a plain in Fars, in south-western Iran, the homeland of the Persian royal house. Darius I began building it around 518 BC, and his successors Xerxes and Artaxerxes I added to it. It was a ceremonial capital rather than the seat of daily government: the kings moved between Susa, Ecbatana and Babylon, and used Persepolis above all for great state occasions such as the New Year festival.

The palaces stand on a huge stone terrace reached by broad double staircases. Their reliefs show delegations from 23 subject peoples bringing gifts to the king — Indians with gold dust, Ethiopians with an okapi, Armenians with a horse — each in national dress. The delegates appear as willing participants in an ordered empire, not as defeated captives, unlike the triumphal art of earlier Assyria. The great audience hall, the Apadana, had 72 columns nearly 20 m tall.

Thousands of clay tablets from the treasury and fortification archives record the workforce that built and ran the site, including women, paid in rations of grain and wine, with extra rations for new mothers.

Alexander the Great took Persepolis in 330 BC and burned the palaces. Arrian presents this as deliberate revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens in 480 BC; other ancient writers describe a drunken feast at which the courtesan Thais urged the king on. Burnt beams and thick ash confirm a great fire, but not the motive. The fire baked the clay tablets hard enough to survive.

The Oriental Institute of Chicago excavated the site from 1931. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the finest surviving monument of the Achaemenid Persian Empire.
