<!-- field: summary -->
The Achaemenid ceremonial capital, built by Darius I and burned by Alexander in 330 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The finest surviving Achaemenid architecture, and its destruction is the symbolic end of the Persian Empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Persepolis was not, in practice, a year-round seat of government in the way a modern capital would be, but a ceremonial centre used mainly for the Persian New Year festival and other great state occasions, when representatives of the empire's many subject peoples would formally attend the king in a display of unified imperial order — the Achaemenid kings actually spent much of the rest of the year moving between other residences at Susa, Ecbatana and Babylon depending on season.

The site stands on a raised stone terrace approached by monumental double staircases whose relief carvings show delegations from twenty-three separate subject peoples bringing tribute — Ethiopians leading an okapi, Indians carrying gold dust, Armenians presenting a fine horse, and many others each dressed in their own distinctive national costume. It is a carefully composed imperial statement of scale and order, and, notably, the delegates are shown as dignified representatives participating willingly in the ceremony rather than as humiliated, conquered subjects, a deliberate visual choice distinct from the more overtly triumphalist imperial art of contemporaries like Assyria.

Administrative tablets found sealed inside the site's fortification walls record wages paid to a large workforce, including women, in rations of grain and wine, with documented extra rations specifically provided for women who had recently given birth — an unusually detailed glimpse of ordinary labour conditions behind an otherwise purely ceremonial façade.

Alexander burned the palace complex in 330 BC. Arrian reports this as a deliberate act of policy, framed as revenge for Xerxes' earlier burning of Athens during the Persian invasion of Greece; other ancient sources instead describe a drunken victory celebration that got out of hand, with a courtesan named Thais said to have first suggested setting the palace alight. Excavation has found genuine burnt cedar roof beams and thick ash layers confirming a real, severe fire at this date, but the physical evidence cannot settle which account of the king's actual intentions is correct. Whatever the cause, that same fire is precisely what baked the site's clay administrative tablets hard enough to survive for archaeologists to recover intact more than two thousand years later.
