<!-- field: summary -->
The Achaemenid ceremonial capital, built by Darius I and burned by Alexander in 330 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
The finest surviving Achaemenid architecture, and its destruction is the symbolic end of the Persian Empire.

<!-- field: body -->
Persepolis stands on a terrace with monumental staircases whose reliefs show delegations from twenty-three subject peoples bringing tribute — Ethiopians with an okapi, Indians with gold, Armenians with a horse. It is an imperial statement of scale and order, and the figures are shown as dignified rather than subjugated.

Administrative tablets found in the fortifications record wages paid to workers, including women, in grain and wine, with extra rations for new mothers.

Alexander burned the palace complex in 330 BC. Arrian says it was deliberate revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens; other sources describe a drunken party and a courtesan named Thais suggesting it. Excavation found burnt cedar beams and ash confirming the fire, but cannot say which account is right. The fire baked the tablets and preserved them.
