<!-- field: summary -->
The richest Ionian city, mother of some ninety colonies, birthplace of Greek philosophy, and the spark of the Ionian Revolt.

<!-- field: significance -->
Where natural philosophy began, and where the grid city plan was systematised by Hippodamus.

<!-- field: body -->
Miletus lay on the coast of Ionia in western Asia Minor, near the mouth of the Maeander river. A Bronze Age settlement with Minoan and then Mycenaean links, it became in the Archaic period the richest of the Ionian Greek cities, with four harbours. Tradition credits it with founding as many as ninety colonies, above all around the Black Sea, from Sinope to Olbia.

Its wealth and its position between the Greek world and the kingdoms of Lydia and Persia made it the birthplace of Greek philosophy. In the 6th century BC Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes asked what the world was made of and answered without appeal to the gods. Thales was said to have predicted the eclipse of 585 BC; Anaximander drew the first Greek map of the world and suggested that life began in water. The historian and geographer Hecataeus also came from Miletus, as did Aspasia, companion of Pericles in Athens.

Under Persian rule from the 540s BC, Miletus led the Ionian Revolt in 499 BC. The Persians captured and destroyed it in 494 BC, killing or deporting its people. When the Athenian playwright Phrynichus staged *The Capture of Miletus*, the audience wept and he was fined for reminding them of the disaster.

Rebuilt after 479 BC on a regular grid of streets associated with the Milesian town planner Hippodamus, it became a model for planned cities across the Hellenistic world. Alexander besieged and took it in 334 BC. Over the centuries silt from the Maeander filled its harbours, and its ruins now stand several kilometres inland.
