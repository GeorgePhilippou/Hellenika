<!-- field: summary -->
A native Elymian city of western Sicily, deeply influenced by Greek culture, whose appeal to Athens in 416 BC helped bring about the Sicilian expedition.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its unfinished Doric temple is one of the most striking classical ruins in Sicily, and its feud with Selinus helped set off a major war.

<!-- field: body -->
Segesta, in the hills of north-west Sicily, belonged to the Elymians, a people the Greeks believed descended from Trojan refugees. By the fifth century BC the city used Greek architecture, coinage and, in part, the Greek language, though its alliances were often with Carthage.

Its best-known monument, a large Doric temple on a hill outside the town, was begun about 430–420 BC and left unfinished, perhaps because of the war with Selinus. Its columns still lack fluting. A theatre on the summit of the city hill, built in the Hellenistic period, looks over the Gulf of Castellammare.

Segesta fought a border war with Selinus, and in 416 BC asked Athens for aid. According to Thucydides, the Segestans promised money they did not have and deceived Athenian envoys with borrowed plate; Athens sent the great expedition of 415 BC. The city later passed between Carthaginian, Syracusan and Roman control.
