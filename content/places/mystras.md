<!-- field: summary -->
The hillside fortress-city above Sparta that was the capital of Byzantine southern Greece and a last centre of Byzantine culture, until 1460.

<!-- field: significance -->
Where Byzantine learning, art and the revival of Plato flourished in the empire's last century; its churches and palace survive.

<!-- field: body -->
Mystras rises on a steep foothill of Mount Taygetos, about 6 kilometres west of ancient Sparta. In 1249 William II of Villehardouin, the French prince of Achaea who ruled much of the Peloponnese after the Fourth Crusade, built a castle on its summit. Captured by the Byzantines, he handed it over in 1262 as part of his ransom.

A town grew on the slopes beneath the castle, and Mystras became the seat of the Byzantine governors and then, from 1349, of the Despots of the Morea, usually sons or brothers of the emperor. While the empire shrank to Constantinople, the Despotate expanded until it ruled almost all the Peloponnese. Mystras had a palace, monasteries and churches decorated with some of the finest late Byzantine frescoes, such as those of the Peribleptos and the Pantanassa.

It was also an intellectual centre, home to the philosopher Gemistos Plethon, who taught a revived Platonism. In 1449 Constantine XI Palaiologos was proclaimed emperor there before going to Constantinople. Mystras surrendered to the Ottomans in 1460, seven years after the fall of the capital. Abandoned in the 19th century when modern Sparta was built, it is now a World Heritage Site.
