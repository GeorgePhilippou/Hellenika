<!-- field: summary -->
A remote mountain temple by the architect of the Parthenon, containing the earliest known Corinthian capital.

<!-- field: significance -->
Combines all three Greek architectural orders in one building — the only ancient temple that does.

<!-- field: body -->
Bassae stands at 1,130 m in the Arcadian mountains, built c. 429–400 BC and attributed by Pausanias to Iktinos, architect of the Parthenon. It was reportedly a thank-offering after the plague spared the local community.

It is unusual in almost every respect: it faces north rather than east, has an unexplained side door, uses Doric outside, Ionic within, and a single Corinthian column on the axis — the earliest Corinthian capital known anywhere.

The interior frieze, showing Greeks fighting Amazons and centaurs, was excavated in 1811–1812, sold at auction in Zante in 1814, and acquired by the British Museum in 1815. The temple has stood under a protective tent since 1987.

Its remoteness, which helped preserve so much of the original structure intact, was also recognised formally in 1986, when Bassae became the very first Greek site added to UNESCO's newly created World Heritage List, ahead of far more famous monuments like the Acropolis, in acknowledgment of both its architectural singularity and its unusually well-preserved condition.
