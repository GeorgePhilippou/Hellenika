<!-- field: summary -->
The great temple of Serapis in Alexandria, built by Ptolemy III around 240 BC and destroyed by Christians in AD 391.

<!-- field: significance -->
The religious heart of Greek Alexandria and home of a second library, whose destruction in 391 marked the end of the pagan city.

<!-- field: body -->
The Serapeum stood on a rocky hill in the south-west of Alexandria, in the old Egyptian quarter of Rhakotis. A smaller shrine of Serapis may have existed there under the first Ptolemies, but the great temple was built by Ptolemy III, as shown by foundation plaques in Greek and hieroglyphs found in the excavations of Alan Rowe in the 1940s. It was rebuilt on a larger scale by the Romans in the 2nd century AD.

The temple housed a famous cult statue of Serapis, enthroned and bearded like Zeus, with the three-headed dog Cerberus beside him; ancient writers attributed it to the sculptor Bryaxis. Around it were colonnades, and rooms that held a "daughter" library, open more widely than the main Library in the palace quarter. Beneath the temple ran underground galleries, perhaps for the burial of sacred animals.

In AD 391, after the emperor Theodosius I banned pagan sacrifice, the bishop Theophilus led Christians in destroying the temple and its statue, a moment Christian writers celebrated as the triumph of the new faith. A great granite column set up for the emperor Diocletian in AD 297, misnamed "Pompey's Pillar", still stands on the site.
