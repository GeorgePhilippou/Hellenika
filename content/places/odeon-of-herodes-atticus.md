<!-- field: summary -->
The roofed theatre built by Herodes Atticus on the south slope of the Acropolis after AD 160, still used for performances.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best-preserved ancient theatre in Athens and a living stage for the Athens Festival.

<!-- field: body -->
The Odeon stands on the south-west slope of the Acropolis, west of the much older Theatre of Dionysus. Herodes Atticus built it after the death of his wife Regilla in 160 as a memorial to her. Pausanias, writing in the 170s, calls it the finest odeon in Greece and says it had a roof of cedar wood — an extraordinary span for a building of its size, though some scholars doubt the whole auditorium was covered.

An *odeon* was a hall for music, recitations and speeches, smaller and more intimate than an open-air theatre. Herodes' Odeon had a steep semicircular auditorium cut into the hillside, seating about 5,000, and a three-storey stone stage building with arched windows, whose tall façade still survives. The floor of the orchestra was paved with black and white marble.

The Odeon was probably destroyed when the Heruli sacked Athens in 267. Its ruins were later built into fortifications. In the 1950s the seating was restored in marble, and since then the Odeon has been the main stage of the Athens Festival, hosting ancient drama, opera and concerts.
