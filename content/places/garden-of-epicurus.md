<!-- field: summary -->
The house and garden in Athens where Epicurus taught a community of friends, women and slaves included.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Garden shows philosophy as a way of life lived in a community, and not as a public lecture series.

<!-- field: body -->
Epicurus moved to Athens in 307/6 BC and set up his school in a house with a garden, which the Greeks called a kepos. The Garden gave the school its name, and its members were called "those from the Garden". According to Diogenes Laertius, Epicurus bought the property for eighty minas, and he lived there until his death in 270 BC.

The school stood apart from others in Athens. The Academy and Lyceum met in public gymnasia and the Stoa in a civic colonnade, but the Epicureans lived together in a private community. Epicurus's students and friends, including women such as Leontion and slaves such as Mys, shared meals and studied his writings. They aimed at tranquillity, and tried to live without fear of the gods or of death, in the company of friends. A monthly feast celebrated the founder.

The Garden was a household as well as a school, and Epicurus's will, quoted by Diogenes Laertius, provided for its upkeep by his heirs and its use by his followers. The house remained a meeting place for Epicureans for centuries, with a succession of heads, and students such as Lucretius and Philodemus carried its doctrines to Rome.

The precise location of the garden is unknown. Ancient sources suggest that it was between the city and the Academy, and the house was said to be in the deme of Melite. No building or inscription has been securely identified as the school's, and the coordinates given here are only a rough indication of the district.
