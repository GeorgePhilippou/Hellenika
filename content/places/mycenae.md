<!-- field: summary -->
"Rich in gold" — the citadel that gave its name to an entire civilisation, with its Lion Gate, shaft graves and tholos tombs.

<!-- field: significance -->
The dominant Mycenaean centre and, in Greek tradition, the seat of Agamemnon and the launching point of the Trojan War.

<!-- field: body -->
Mycenae occupies a defensible hill between two mountains commanding the Argive plain. Its cyclopean walls, built of blocks so large that later Greeks credited them to the Cyclopes, enclose a citadel entered through the Lion Gate — a relieving triangle carved with two lionesses flanking a column, the oldest monumental sculpture in Europe.

Schliemann excavated Grave Circle A inside the walls in 1876, finding six shaft graves with nineteen bodies and roughly 15 kg of gold: masks, breastplates, inlaid daggers, and amber beads that had travelled all the way from the Baltic, evidence of exchange networks reaching far beyond anywhere Mycenaean ships are known to have sailed themselves. Schliemann, already flush from his self-promoted triumph at Troy, wired the Greek king a characteristically theatrical telegram claiming to have "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon" on lifting one of the gold masks — a claim that has not survived later dating any better than his identification of "Priam's Treasure" at Troy did, since the shaft graves are now placed roughly three to four centuries before any plausible date for a Trojan War, meaning whoever these richly buried men were, none of them could have been Agamemnon in any literal sense.

Outside the walls stand nine tholos tombs, of which the so-called "Treasury of Atreus" — a name as traditional and as unverifiable as Schliemann's mask — has a corbelled dome 13.5 metres across, the largest unsupported architectural span anywhere in the world for well over a thousand years afterward. Late in the citadel's life its occupants cut a concealed stairway down through the fortification walls to an underground cistern fed by a spring outside them, an engineering effort substantial enough to suggest they were actively preparing for the possibility of a prolonged siege rather than simply securing a convenient water supply.
