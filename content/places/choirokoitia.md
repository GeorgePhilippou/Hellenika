<!-- field: summary -->
A Neolithic village of circular stone houses, among the best-preserved prehistoric settlements anywhere in the eastern Mediterranean -- and, at up to nine thousand years old, far older than anything else this site maps.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its scale and preservation make it one of the most important sources anywhere for how people in the eastern Mediterranean lived before farming societies had cities, writing or metal tools.

<!-- field: body -->
Choirokoitia occupies a defensible hillside above the Maroni river in southern Cyprus. Excavations beginning with Porphyrios Dikaios in the 1930s, and continued by French missions from the 1970s onward, exposed a dense cluster of circular, stone-founded dwellings known as tholoi, some with upper storeys of mudbrick, connected by narrow paved streets and enclosed by defensive walls.

The dead were typically buried beneath the floors of the houses they had lived in, sometimes with a stone slab or grinding quern placed over the body -- one of several practices that, together with the settlement's isolation on an island already some 60 kilometres from the nearest mainland coast at this date, has made Choirokoitia a key case study for how early farming communities organised themselves.

Only a portion of the roughly 3-hectare site has been excavated. The remainder survives as an intact archaeological reserve, and the settlement's precise chronology -- whether occupation was continuous across its multi-millennium history or interrupted and resumed -- remains a subject of ongoing study. Choirokoitia was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998.

Its dates fall entirely before 3200 BC, the earliest year this site's timeline and maps track, so Choirokoitia does not appear as a marker on them -- it predates the Bronze Age world the rest of this dataset covers by millennia.
