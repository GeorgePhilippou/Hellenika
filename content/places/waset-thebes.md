<!-- field: summary -->
The great Upper Egyptian city of Karnak and Luxor, capital of the New Kingdom at its height.

<!-- field: significance -->
Egypt’s dominant religious centre and the monumental setting of New Kingdom power encountered by the wider Bronze Age world.

<!-- field: body -->
The Egyptian city called Waset—later Thebes to Greek speakers—occupied both banks of the Nile around modern Luxor. The living city and the great temples stood mainly on the east bank; royal mortuary temples and the cemeteries now called the Valleys of the Kings and Queens spread across the western desert edge.

Local rulers reunified Egypt at the beginning of the Middle Kingdom, giving Thebes its first period of national power. It became the ceremonial heart of the New Kingdom after another Theban dynasty expelled the Hyksos. Karnak’s temple of Amun grew through centuries of additions into a monumental record of royal competition, while Luxor Temple was central to the Opet festival that renewed divine and royal authority.

Political capitals later moved north, but Thebes remained wealthy through its temples, estates and priesthood. Akhenaten challenged that establishment; Tutankhamun restored it. Assyrian forces sacked the city in 663 BC, yet its sanctuaries continued to attract patronage under later Egyptian, Persian, Macedonian and Ptolemaic rulers. The visible landscape is therefore not one moment but the accumulation of more than a millennium of building, erasure and reuse.
