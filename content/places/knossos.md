<!-- field: summary -->
The largest Minoan palace complex — over a thousand interlocking rooms around a central court — and the source of the labyrinth tradition.

<!-- field: significance -->
Europe's first great architectural complex, the administrative heart of Minoan Crete, and the site where both Linear A and Linear B were first found.

<!-- field: body -->
Knossos sits on a tell occupied continuously from around 7000 BC, making it one of the longest-inhabited single sites in Europe long before any palace was ever built there. The first palace on the site went up around 1900 BC, was destroyed and substantially rebuilt around 1700 BC after what was probably a major earthquake, and was finally abandoned as a functioning palace around 1350 BC, by which point it had already outlived every other Minoan palace centre on Crete by roughly a century.

At its height Knossos was not simply a large building but the largest single settlement in the Bronze Age Aegean, with population estimates for the surrounding town running into the tens of thousands — a genuine city organised around the palace rather than a palace standing in isolation. The complex itself is built around a rectangular central court roughly 50 by 25 metres, with storage magazines to the west holding rows of giant storage jars called *pithoi* large enough to stand in, ceremonial rooms, workshops for craft production, and a light-well system that let air and daylight reach interior rooms across what may have been three or four storeys in places. Its plumbing — terracotta pipes, flushing systems, and a drainage network serving multiple floors — was not matched anywhere in Europe again until many centuries later.

The sheer density of interconnected rooms, corridors and light-wells is the most plausible source of the Greek word *labyrinthos*, and the double axe symbol, or *labrys*, carved repeatedly into the palace's stone blocks may be where that specific word for the maze came from. It was Arthur Evans himself who made the connection to the myth of the Minotaur and the labyrinth of King Minos explicit and public, and who named an entire Bronze Age civilisation "Minoan" after a legendary king on the strength of it — a piece of imaginative branding so successful that it is now impossible to discuss Aegean prehistory without his terminology.

Evans' relationship with the site went well beyond naming it. He bought the entire hill outright in 1900, using a substantial family fortune built on his father's paper-manufacturing business, and had workmen digging within weeks of the purchase. Over the following three decades he not only excavated but rebuilt large sections of the palace in poured concrete, directing his architect Christian Doll to reconstruct columns, staircases and upper storeys according to Evans' own reading of the fragmentary evidence, and commissioning heavily restored, sometimes substantially reimagined versions of the palace's frescoes. The result, still what most visitors to Knossos actually see today, has been increasingly criticised by later archaeologists as showing at least as much of Evans' own Edwardian imagination — his confident, largely unsupported picture of a peaceful, matriarchal, priest-king-led Minoan society — as it does secure evidence about what Bronze Age Knossos actually looked like or how it actually functioned. Disentangling the genuine Minoan palace from Evans' concrete and paint remains one of the harder ongoing problems in Aegean archaeology.

Whatever the palace's original appearance, its administrative history after around 1450 BC is unambiguous. While every other major Minoan centre on Crete was destroyed at roughly that date, in a wave of destructions still not fully explained, Knossos alone continued in use — now under administrators keeping records not in the still-undeciphered Linear A script used earlier on the island, but in Linear B, an early form of written Greek. That change in the palace's own written archive is the single clearest piece of evidence that Knossos had come under Mycenaean Greek control from the mainland, surviving as a subject administrative centre for perhaps another century before its final abandonment.
