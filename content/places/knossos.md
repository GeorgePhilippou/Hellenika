<!-- field: summary -->
The largest Minoan palace complex — over a thousand interlocking rooms around a central court — and the source of the labyrinth tradition.

<!-- field: significance -->
Europe's first great architectural complex, the administrative heart of Minoan Crete, and the site where both Linear A and Linear B were first found.

<!-- field: body -->
Knossos lies a few kilometres inland from Heraklion, on Crete's north coast. People lived on the site from about 7000 BC, making it one of the oldest settlements in Europe. The first palace was built around 1900 BC; after a destruction around 1700 BC, probably by earthquake, it was rebuilt on a grander scale. Around it grew the largest town of the Bronze Age Aegean.

The palace is a complex of more than a thousand rooms around a central court about 50 by 25 m, rising in places to three or four storeys. It held storerooms lined with giant jars (*pithoi*), shrines, workshops and grand reception rooms, lit by light-wells and served by clay drainage pipes. Its maze of rooms and the double-axe (*labrys*) symbol carved on its stones probably lie behind the Greek legend of the labyrinth of King Minos and the Minotaur. Archives in Linear A, the undeciphered Minoan script, show it was an administrative centre.

Around 1450 BC most Minoan palaces on Crete were destroyed, but Knossos continued — now keeping its records in Linear B, an early form of Greek. This is the clearest evidence that Mycenaean Greeks from the mainland had taken control of it. The palace was finally destroyed and abandoned around 1350 BC or somewhat later.

A local merchant, Minos Kalokairinos, dug the first trenches in 1878. Arthur Evans bought the site in 1900 and excavated it for three decades. He named the whole civilisation "Minoan" after King Minos, and in the 1920s rebuilt parts of the palace in reinforced concrete, with restored frescoes. What visitors see today is in large part his interpretation, shaped by his picture of a peaceful, priest-king-ruled society — an image modern archaeologists have done much to question.
