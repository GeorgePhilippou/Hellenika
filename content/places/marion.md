<!-- field: summary -->
A copper-trade city-kingdom on Cyprus's northwest tip, destroyed for backing the losing side in the wars after Alexander's death and refounded under a new name.

<!-- field: significance -->
Its identification as one of the ten kingdoms named in Esarhaddon's tribute list is a useful example of how much of Cyprus's early political geography rests on scholarly reconstruction of garbled foreign transliterations, not settled fact.

<!-- field: body -->
Marion controlled the northwest corner of Cyprus and its access to copper-bearing districts, prospering on a trade that shows up archaeologically in over 850 excavated tombs and centuries of sanctuary activity. A rural sanctuary at nearby Peristeries, dedicated to a female fertility goddess, was in continuous use from roughly the tenth to the early fifth century BC, and a probable palace building of the sixth century BC has also been identified.

A clay prism of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon lists ten Cypriot kings who paid him tribute around 673 BC, giving each a garbled Akkadian rendering of a Cypriot royal name and city. Modern scholars have matched most of these names to known kingdoms with reasonable confidence, but one entry, "Nuria," is only tentatively equated with Marion — a guess based on process of elimination rather than direct evidence, and this entity's status among the ten kingdoms should be read as contested rather than settled.

In 312 BC, during the wars between Alexander's former generals, Marion's king backed Antigonus against Ptolemy I, who took the city and had it destroyed. Ptolemy II later founded a new city nearby, naming it Arsinoe after his sister and wife — one of several Cypriot cities renamed for Ptolemaic royal women in this period.
