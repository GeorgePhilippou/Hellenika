<!-- field: summary -->
The potters' quarter and chief cemetery of ancient Athens, lying northwest of the Agora beyond the city wall.

<!-- field: significance -->
The best source for Athenian burial customs, from Geometric vases to classical grave monuments.

<!-- field: body -->
The Kerameikos takes its name from the Athenian potters (*kerameis*) who worked beside the Eridanos stream northwest of the Agora. It was also the city's main burial ground, used from the Bronze Age through to the Roman period, and the Dipylon and Sacred Gates of the city wall stood on the site. The Sacred Way, along which the Eleusinian procession passed, began here.

In the eighth century BC wealthy families marked graves with huge Geometric vases, the Dipylon amphorae, painted with funeral scenes. In the classical period grave monuments grew elaborate, with sculpted stelai and family plots lining the roads; later restrictions on display reduced their scale. Thucydides places the public burial of Athens' war dead nearby, where Pericles delivered the funeral speech he gives him.

The German Archaeological Institute has excavated the site since 1913, and the open-air museum there shows grave monuments in place. Finds from the area underpin much of the study of Athenian pottery and funerary art.
