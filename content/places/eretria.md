<!-- field: summary -->
Euboean city that helped to found colonies in the eighth century BC and paid for supporting the Ionians when Persia burned it in 490 BC.

<!-- field: significance -->
Eretria illustrates the early Greek expansion, and its destruction was the immediate prelude to Marathon.

<!-- field: body -->
Eretria stood on the western coast of Euboea, across from Attica, a few kilometres from the Bronze Age and Dark Age site at Lefkandi. Lefkandi was abandoned around 700 BC, just as Eretria grew, and many scholars argue that its people moved there; the evidence is circumstantial. Eretria and its neighbour Chalcis were among the most active Greek cities of the early eighth century, sending settlers and traders east and west, and many of the first Greek finds in Italy and the Levant have Euboean origins.

The two cities are said to have fought the Lelantine War for control of the fertile plain between them. Thucydides calls it a war in which the rest of Greece took sides, but he gives no details, and modern historians disagree on whether it was one long conflict, several, or something that never happened as later writers imagined it.

Eretria's sympathy with the Ionians brought disaster. In 498 it sent ships to help the revolt, which ended in the burning of Sardis. Darius resolved to punish Athens and Eretria, and in 490 the Persian force landed on Euboea, besieged Eretria for six days, and took it by treachery. The temples were burned, the citizens deported to Ardericca in Persia, and the fleet then crossed to Marathon. Herodotus adds that the exiles were still speaking Greek in his own day.

Excavation by the Swiss School at Athens has recovered a large part of the city, including a well-preserved late Archaic temple of Apollo with a pediment showing Theseus carrying off the Amazon Antiope. The city was rebuilt after 490 and survived until Roman times.
