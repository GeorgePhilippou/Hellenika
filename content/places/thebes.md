<!-- field: summary -->
Mycenaean palace, mythological centre of the Oedipus and Dionysus legends, and briefly the dominant power in Greece after Leuctra.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only city to break Spartan military supremacy — and the city Alexander destroyed as a warning to the rest of Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Thebes lies in Boeotia, in central Greece, north-west of Athens. It was a major Mycenaean palace centre, whose Linear B tablets record offerings and workers, but the modern town sits on top of the ancient one, so the Bronze Age city is known only from small rescue excavations.

No city is richer in myth. Thebes was founded by Cadmus, a Phoenician prince who sowed a dragon's teeth to raise its first warriors; Dionysus and Heracles were born there; and it was the city of Oedipus, of the Seven Against Thebes and of Antigone. Athenian tragedy repeatedly set its disasters in Thebes, a dark mirror of well-ordered Athens.

Historically Thebes led the Boeotian League. It sided with Persia when Xerxes invaded in 480 BC, a choice other Greeks long remembered. In the 4th century BC it rose to brief leadership of Greece. Its general Epaminondas massed his left wing fifty ranks deep to crush the Spartan army at Leuctra in 371 BC, ending Spartan supremacy, then invaded the Peloponnese and freed Messenia from Spartan rule. Thebes' elite Sacred Band, said to be formed of 150 pairs of lovers, was a key part of its army.

The Theban ascendancy ended with Epaminondas' death at Mantinea in 362 BC. In 338 BC Thebes and Athens were defeated by Philip II at Chaeronea, where the Sacred Band was wiped out. When Thebes revolted against Alexander in 335 BC, he razed the city and enslaved its people as a warning to the rest of Greece, sparing, by tradition, only the temples and the house of the poet Pindar. It was refounded twenty years later, but never regained its importance.
