<!-- field: summary -->
Mycenaean palace, mythological centre of the Oedipus and Dionysus legends, and briefly the dominant power in Greece after Leuctra.

<!-- field: significance -->
The only city to break Spartan military supremacy — and the city Alexander destroyed as a warning to the rest of Greece.

<!-- field: body -->
Thebes was a major Mycenaean centre whose Linear B tablets record offerings and personnel, though the modern town sits directly on top of the ancient site and has restricted almost all excavation to small rescue digs ahead of construction, leaving the Bronze Age city far less understood than Mycenae or Pylos.

In myth it is unmatched: the founding by Cadmus, a Phoenician prince who killed a dragon and sowed its teeth to raise the city's first warriors from the ground; the doomed royal line of Oedipus, who solved the Sphinx's riddle only to fulfil the prophecy he was trying to escape; the failed assault of the Seven Against Thebes; and the births of both Dionysus and Heracles. Greek tragedy consistently uses Thebes as the city where things go wrong, a dark mirror held up against well-governed Athens for an Athenian theatre audience to watch its own worst fears play out safely on someone else's stage.

Historically the city sided with Persia during Xerxes' invasion in 480 BC, a decision other Greek states did not forget for generations. It later rose to genuine Greek leadership under the general Epaminondas, whose unconventional deepened attack on one flank — massing troops fifty ranks deep against Spartan custom rather than spreading the line evenly — broke Sparta's previously unmatched hoplite reputation at Leuctra in 371 BC and briefly made Thebes the leading power in Greece, its influence backed by regular invasions of the Peloponnese and the permanent liberation of Sparta's helot-worked territory of Messenia. Its Sacred Band, an elite unit of 150 male couples bound by both military discipline and Eros, was annihilated fighting to the last man at Chaeronea in 338 BC; a mass grave of 254 skeletons found beneath the Lion of Chaeronea monument is almost certainly theirs. In 335 BC Thebes revolted against the young Alexander, who stormed and razed the city as a deliberate warning to the rest of Greece, sparing only its temples and, on record as a personal gesture to a poet he admired, the house said to have belonged to Pindar more than a century before.
