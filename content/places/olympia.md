<!-- field: summary -->
Sanctuary of Zeus and home of the Olympic Games, held every four years from 776 BC for over a millennium.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Games created a shared Greek calendar and a Panhellenic identity that transcended constant warfare between the cities.

<!-- field: body -->
The traditional first Olympiad of 776 BC became the basis of Greek chronology — historians and inscriptions alike dated events by counting Olympiad number, giving the fragmented Greek world one of its very few shared calendars. A sacred truce, the *ekecheiria*, proclaimed by heralds sent out from Elis before each festival, was supposed to guarantee safe passage to competitors and spectators travelling through territories technically at war, though how reliably rival states actually honoured it in practice varied.

The Temple of Zeus held Pheidias' chryselephantine cult statue, some twelve metres high and built up from gold plates and carved ivory over a wooden core, ranked among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world and long since lost to fire or looting. Pheidias' own workshop has been excavated just outside the temple, identified with unusual certainty by tools, ivory offcuts, moulds for the statue's drapery, and a drinking cup inscribed simply "I belong to Pheidias" — a rare direct physical link between an ancient artist and a known building.

Events included the *stadion* sprint, wrestling, boxing, the brutal near-unrestricted *pankration*, chariot and horse racing, and the pentathlon. Judges called Hellanodikai, drawn from Elis, oversaw the competitions and enforced eligibility rules restricting entrants to free Greek men. Competitors were male and, from the seventh century onward, competed naked; married women were barred even from attending as spectators on pain of death, according to tradition, though unmarried women faced no such restriction, and women could win the prestigious chariot race as owners without competing in person themselves — the Spartan princess Cynisca did so twice, becoming the first woman recorded as an Olympic victor. Women held a separate festival of their own at the same sanctuary, the Heraea, centred on a footrace in honour of Hera.

Theodosius I ended the Games around AD 393 as part of a wider suppression of pagan festivals; subsequent earthquakes, fires and river flooding from the nearby Alpheios and Kladeos buried the site under metres of silt, which is precisely why so much of it, including Pheidias' workshop, survived for archaeologists to recover intact many centuries later.
