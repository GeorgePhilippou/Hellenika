<!-- field: summary -->
Sanctuary of Zeus and home of the Olympic Games, held every four years from 776 BC for over a millennium.

<!-- field: significance -->
The Games created a shared Greek calendar and a Panhellenic identity that transcended constant warfare between the cities.

<!-- field: body -->
Olympia lies in Elis, in the north-western Peloponnese, in a valley where the Kladeos river meets the Alpheios. It was a sanctuary of Zeus from at least the 10th century BC and, by tradition from 776 BC, the site of the Olympic Games, held every four years for over a thousand years.

The Games became central to Greek identity. Before each festival heralds announced a sacred truce so that competitors and spectators could travel safely, and historians dated events by Olympiads, giving the divided Greek world a shared calendar. Events included the *stadion* sprint, the pentathlon, wrestling, boxing, the near-unrestricted *pankration*, and chariot and horse races. Competitors were free Greek men, competing naked; prizes were olive wreaths, but victory brought great honour at home. Married women were barred as spectators, but women could win the chariot race as owners — the Spartan princess Cynisca did so twice — and girls ran in a separate festival for Hera.

The Temple of Zeus, built around 470–456 BC, held Pheidias' great statue of the seated god in gold and ivory, about 12 m high and one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. Pheidias' workshop has been found nearby, with tools, moulds and a cup inscribed "I belong to Pheidias". The sanctuary also held the older Temple of Hera, treasuries of Greek cities, the stadium, and hundreds of statues of victors and gods.

The Games ended under the Christian emperor Theodosius I around AD 393. Earthquakes and floods later buried the site under metres of silt, which preserved it. German excavations since 1875 have uncovered much of the sanctuary, and the Olympic flame for the modern Games is still lit there.
