<!-- field: summary -->
The great library and colonnaded court built by Hadrian in the centre of Athens in AD 132.

<!-- field: significance -->
The grandest of Hadrian's gifts to Athens, combining a library, lecture halls and a garden court.

<!-- field: body -->
The Library of Hadrian lies north of the Acropolis, beside the Roman Agora. It was a large rectangular enclosure, about 122 by 82 metres, entered through a monumental gateway with columns of green-veined marble on its west side. Inside, a colonnaded court with a long pool and garden surrounded the main buildings. Pausanias, who saw it a generation later, counted a hundred columns.

On the east side was the library itself: a central hall with niches in its walls for wooden bookcases holding papyrus rolls, flanked by reading or lecture rooms and rooms at the corners that may have served as small auditoria. The complex was probably also used for public archives and as a place for teaching.

The library was damaged when the Heruli sacked Athens in AD 267, and its walls were then built into the late Roman defences. It was restored around 410 by a Roman prefect, and in the 5th century a church was built in the court; it was rebuilt several times over the following centuries. Part of its west façade, with its Corinthian columns, still stands on Areos Street in the Monastiraki district.
