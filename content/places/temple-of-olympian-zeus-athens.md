<!-- field: summary -->
The colossal temple of Zeus in Athens, begun around 520 BC and finished by Hadrian in AD 131/2.

<!-- field: significance -->
One of the largest temples of the ancient world, whose six-century construction spans the whole history of Athens from tyranny to Roman empire.

<!-- field: body -->
The temple stands south-east of the Acropolis, on the site of an older sanctuary of Zeus. Around 520 BC the sons of the tyrant Peisistratus began a vast limestone temple, meant to rival the great temples of Samos and Ephesus. When the tyranny fell in 510 BC the work was abandoned, and later Athenians saw the unfinished foundations as a monument to tyrants' arrogance.

In 174 BC the Seleucid king Antiochus IV paid to resume it, employing a Roman architect, Cossutius, who redesigned it in marble in the new Corinthian order. Work stopped again at the king's death. According to Pliny, when Sulla sacked Athens in 86 BC he carried some of its columns off to Rome for the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol.

The temple was finally completed by Hadrian, who dedicated it in AD 131/2. With 104 Corinthian columns about 17 metres high, it was among the largest temples in the Greek world. It housed a gold-and-ivory statue of Zeus and a statue of Hadrian himself. Probably damaged in the Herulian raid of 267, it was later quarried for stone. Fifteen columns still stand; a sixteenth was blown down in a storm in 1852 and lies where it fell.
