<!-- field: summary -->
The harbour city that replaced Palaepaphos as western Cyprus’s political centre and became the Ptolemaic capital of the island.

<!-- field: significance -->
The principal administrative and maritime centre of Hellenistic Cyprus.

<!-- field: body -->
Nicocles, the last king of Palaepaphos, established a new planned city beside a natural harbour near the end of the fourth century BC. The move did not abolish Old Paphos or its sanctuary: it created a maritime administrative centre while the older sacred centre continued inland. A gridded street plan, fortifications and harbour works connected the foundation to the wider urban transformation of the early Hellenistic Mediterranean.

After the Ptolemies eliminated the Cypriot kingships, Nea Paphos became the headquarters of the governor or *strategos* who administered the whole island from Egypt. Its position facing Alexandria made it a practical naval and fiscal capital. Coinage, inscriptions and imported goods show that it participated in Ptolemaic institutions while retaining Cypriot religious traditions.

The monumental record spans many centuries. The rock-cut “Tombs of the Kings” belonged to high officials rather than literal kings; the theatre began in the Hellenistic period and was repeatedly altered; elaborate mosaic houses belong mainly to Roman prosperity. Paphos retained its leading status after Rome annexed Cyprus, so the surviving city should not be mistaken for a purely Ptolemaic snapshot.
