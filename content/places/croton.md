<!-- field: summary -->
Achaean colony in southern Italy, famed for its athletes and doctors, and home to Pythagoras's community.

<!-- field: significance -->
Croton shows the intellectual and athletic weight of the western Greeks, and it is the setting for the earliest well-attested Pythagorean society.

<!-- field: body -->
Croton was founded on the Ionian coast of Calabria, traditionally about 710 BC, by settlers from Achaea in the northern Peloponnese. Strabo preserves the foundation story: Myscellus was told by Delphi to go where he found rain in fine weather, and was surprised when a woman he loved wept beside him. Its neighbour Sybaris, also Achaean and larger, dominated the coast at first.

By the late sixth century Croton was famous for two things. One was athletics: Crotonians took so many prizes at Olympia that it was said to be the healthiest place in Greece, and Milo, a six-times champion, became a byword for strength. The other was medicine. Democedes of Croton, whom Herodotus describes as the best doctor of his time, cured Darius of a sprain when Egyptian physicians had failed, and stayed at the Persian court as a virtual prisoner until he escaped home.

Pythagoras arrived about 530 BC and drew followers among the elite. The community that grew from his teaching combined philosophy, mathematics and ascetic rules, and it became politically influential. After Croton defeated and razed Sybaris in 510, quarrels over the division of the spoil and the influence of the Pythagoreans led to a revolt against them. Later sources place attacks on Pythagorean meeting houses at various dates and give conflicting stories of what happened to the founder.

In the third century Croton suffered from Bruttian, Syracusan and Roman pressure, and by the time of Hannibal it was much diminished. At Capo Colonna, the temple of Hera Lacinia, once its chief sanctuary, has one standing column.
