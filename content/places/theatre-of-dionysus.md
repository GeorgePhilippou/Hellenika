The great open-air theatre on the south slope of the Acropolis, where the masterpieces of Athenian tragedy and comedy were first performed.

<!-- field: significance -->
The birthplace of European drama and the model for later Greek theatres.

<!-- field: body -->
The Theatre of Dionysus lies on the south slope of the Acropolis, within the sanctuary of Dionysus Eleuthereus. It was the main venue of the City Dionysia, the spring festival at which the plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes were presented in competition before a mass audience.

In the fifth century BC the audience probably sat on timber seating or on the bare hillside, facing a circular dancing floor, the orchestra. The stone theatre seen today owes most to a rebuilding of the later fourth century BC, usually linked to the Athenian statesman Lycurgus, who also had standard texts of the three great tragedians deposited in the city's archive. The seating held on the order of fifteen thousand people.

The theatre was altered repeatedly in Hellenistic and Roman times, when the stage was rebuilt and marble thrones were installed for priests and officials; the central seat belonged to the priest of Dionysus Eleuthereus. Excavation began in the 1860s, and the site is now one of the best-known monuments of ancient Athens.
