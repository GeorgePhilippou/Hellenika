<!-- field: summary -->
A mound at Hisarlik with at least nine superimposed cities, one of which may be the Troy of the Iliad.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most argued-over site in archaeology, and the test case for whether Greek epic preserves historical memory.

<!-- field: body -->
Hisarlik controls the entrance to the Dardanelles, a strategic position that explains 3,000 years of continuous resettlement. Schliemann dug a trench through it in 1871; Dörpfeld and later Blegen imposed order, identifying nine main levels with numerous sub-phases.

Troy VI (c. 1700–1300 BC) is a substantial walled city with sloping stone walls and towers, destroyed in a way consistent with earthquake. Troy VIIa (c. 1300–1180 BC) reused those walls, crammed small houses inside them with storage jars sunk into the floors, and was destroyed by fire with unburied human remains and bronze arrowheads in the debris. Blegen argued VIIa is Homer's Troy.

Hittite archives independently name a city Wilusa — plausibly Greek *(W)ilios* — in this region, ruled by a king Alaksandu, and record friction with a power called Ahhiyawa, generally identified with Mycenaean Greeks. Manfred Korfmann's magnetometry from 1988 revealed a lower city with a defensive ditch, expanding the estimated population roughly tenfold. That a war happened here is plausible; that it was *the* war of the Iliad remains unproven.
