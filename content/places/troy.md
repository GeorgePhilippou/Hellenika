<!-- field: summary -->
A mound at Hisarlik with at least nine superimposed cities, one of which may be the Troy of the Iliad.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most argued-over site in archaeology, and the test case for whether Greek epic preserves historical memory.

<!-- field: body -->
Hisarlik controls the entrance to the Dardanelles, the narrow strait linking the Aegean to the Sea of Marmara and, beyond it, the Black Sea. Prevailing currents and winds through the strait run against a ship heading north for much of the year, so vessels making for the Black Sea's grain and metal routes regularly had to wait nearby for a favourable change — and whoever controlled the coastline at that waiting point controlled a natural toll booth. That single fact of geography, more than any literary tradition, is the best explanation for why people kept rebuilding on the same low mound for roughly four thousand years, across a documented sequence running from a small early Bronze Age settlement through a major Roman and Byzantine town, long after the political and mythic world of the Iliad had passed into history.

The identification of Hisarlik with Homeric Troy is older than its most famous excavator. In 1822 the Scottish geographer Charles Maclaren argued from ancient descriptions that the mound, not the nearby village long favoured by classical tourists, was the right location. Frank Calvert, a British expatriate whose family had land in the Troad, spent over a decade excavating there on a shoestring before Heinrich Schliemann, a wealthy retired businessman with a fixed idea and considerable self-promotional skill, arrived in the 1870s. Calvert shared his topographical knowledge, his conviction that Hisarlik was the site, and even part-ownership of the mound itself, since part of it sat on land his family held; Schliemann took the funding, the labour force, and afterwards nearly all of the credit, presenting the discovery in his own writings and public lectures as essentially his alone, with Calvert reduced to a helpful local contact. Modern historians of the excavation, drawing on Calvert's surviving letters and notebooks, have worked to restore his role as co-discoverer rather than assistant, but the popular story of "the man who found Troy" still belongs almost entirely to Schliemann — a useful reminder that who gets remembered as making a discovery and who actually made it are not always the same question.

Schliemann's most sensational find came in 1873: a hoard of gold jewellery, cups and weapons he announced to the world as "Priam's Treasure," supposedly smuggled out of Ottoman territory in his wife's shawl. He donated it to Berlin in 1880, where it was displayed for over sixty years, until the advancing Red Army seized it as war booty in 1945. It vanished from public knowledge for nearly fifty years, resurfacing only when the Pushkin Museum in Moscow admitted in the 1990s that it held the collection, where much of it remains on display today. The name has always been more romantic than accurate: modern stratigraphic dating places the hoard in Troy II, roughly 2400 BC, about a thousand years before any plausible date for a Trojan War and long before any figure named Priam could have existed even in legend.

Later excavators brought discipline the treasure hunt lacked. Wilhelm Dörpfeld, Schliemann's own assistant, argued after his employer's death that the walled, tower-studded Troy VI (roughly 1700–1300 BC) was a far better match for Homer's city than the earlier level Schliemann had favoured. Carl Blegen's campaigns in the 1930s subdivided the site's nine main levels into forty-six distinct sub-phases, a level of stratigraphic precision that turned Hisarlik from a treasure pit into a genuine chronological record. Blegen concluded that Troy VI most likely fell to an earthquake, not an army, but that the next level, Troy VIIa, told a different story: small houses crammed inside the reused Troy VI walls, storage jars sunk into their floors as if for a long siege, and a destruction layer containing unburied human remains and bronze arrowheads. Whether or not Homer's war stands behind it, something violent ended Troy VIIa around 1180 BC.

Independent written evidence exists, and it does not come from Greece. Hittite royal archives repeatedly mention a city called Wilusa in exactly this part of north-western Anatolia, ruled around 1280 BC by a king named Alaksandu, whose name is itself a plausible rendering of Greek *Alexandros* — Paris's other name in the Iliad. A separate document, the Tawagalawa letter, describes a rebel named Piyamaradu attacking Wilusa and forcing Alaksandu to appeal to the Hittite king for help, in a dispute that also involved a power called Ahhiyawa, which most specialists identify with the Mycenaean Greeks. None of this describes a ten-year siege by a coalition of Greek kings over a stolen queen. It does show that Wilusa/Troy was a real place embroiled in real conflicts involving Mycenaeans, which is the most that can honestly be said for any connection between Hittite records and the Iliad.

The site's modern profile changed again after 1988, when Manfred Korfmann began a new excavation campaign that ran until his death in 2005. Magnetometry survey under his direction revealed a defensive ditch and traces of a substantial lower settlement well beyond the citadel walls Schliemann and Blegen had focused on, suggesting a city roughly ten times larger than previously assumed and a plausible regional trading centre rather than a minor hilltop fort. That claim did not go unchallenged. In 2001 the ancient historian Frank Kolb publicly accused Korfmann of inflating both the lower city's extent and Bronze Age Troy's overall importance beyond what the excavated evidence — at the time perhaps two or three percent of the proposed lower city — could actually support. The dispute became sufficiently public that Korfmann's own team subsequently revised its population estimates downward, and the underlying question of just how large and important Bronze Age Troy really was remains open.

Troy was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998, and excavation continues under Turkish direction. Visitors today walk a signed circuit past the exposed Bronze Age walls, Dörpfeld's and Blegen's trenches, and a much later Roman odeon built when the site had long since become a Greco-Roman town called Ilion rather than a ruin — and, at the car park, a large wooden horse built for tourists rather than any army, a reminder of how thoroughly the legendary version of this place has outgrown the archaeological one in the popular imagination.

None of this settles the "Homeric question" the site is famous for. What it shows, layer by layer, is a real, strategically located city destroyed and rebuilt many times over three thousand years, occasionally visible in outside written records, on top of which later generations of Greeks built a story about a war — and which one, if any, of the destructions beneath the mound corresponds to that story is still argued over by people who have spent entire careers on the question.
