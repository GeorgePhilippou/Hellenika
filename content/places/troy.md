<!-- field: summary -->
A mound at Hisarlik with at least nine superimposed cities, one of which may be the Troy of the Iliad.

<!-- field: significance -->
The most argued-over site in archaeology, and the test case for whether Greek epic preserves historical memory.

<!-- field: body -->
Troy is the mound of Hisarlik in north-west Turkey, near the entrance to the Dardanelles, the strait between the Aegean and the Black Sea. It was occupied for some four thousand years, from about 3000 BC to Byzantine times, and holds at least nine cities built one on top of another. Ships bound for the Black Sea often had to wait nearby for the wind to change, so whoever held this coast controlled a valuable crossing point.

Charles Maclaren identified the mound as Troy in 1822, and Frank Calvert, who owned part of it, dug the first trenches in the 1860s. Heinrich Schliemann began large-scale digging in 1871 and took most of the credit. In 1873 he found a hoard of gold he called "Priam's Treasure"; it actually belongs to Troy II, about 2400 BC, a thousand years too early. Taken to Berlin and seized by the Red Army in 1945, it is now in Moscow.

Later excavators brought order. Wilhelm Dörpfeld argued that Troy VI (about 1700–1300 BC), with its great towered walls, best matched Homer's city. Carl Blegen in the 1930s concluded that Troy VI fell to an earthquake, but that Troy VIIa, crowded with houses and storage jars as if under siege, was destroyed by fire around 1180 BC, with arrowheads and unburied bodies in the ruins. From 1988 Manfred Korfmann's team found traces of a large lower town around the citadel, though how large and important Bronze Age Troy was is still disputed.

Hittite records mention a city called Wilusa in this region, involved in conflicts with the Ahhiyawa — probably Mycenaean Greeks — and ruled around 1280 BC by a king Alaksandu, a name like Paris' Greek name, Alexandros. This shows a real Troy entangled in real wars involving Greeks, not a ten-year siege over Helen.

In Greek and Roman times the site was the town of Ilion, honoured for its legend; Alexander sacrificed there in 334 BC. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998.
